<section id="dipole">

<div className="section-heading"><span>01</span><h2>Derive the radiation resistance of a short dipole</h2></div>

Chapter 101 showed that changing fields can detach from a conductor. To a circuit, the energy that leaves looks like power dissipated in a resistor. Calculating that **radiation resistance** turns an antenna into an ordinary circuit element.

<div className="derivation">

<span className="tiny-label">DERIVATION / RADIATED POWER AND RADIATION RESISTANCE</span>

1. **Far field of a short current element.** A uniform current $I$ (peak phasor) on a length $\ell \ll \lambda$ produces, at distance $r \gg \lambda$ and angle $\theta$ from its axis, a transverse electric field (the standard solution of Maxwell's equations for an oscillating dipole):
<Equation tex={String.raw`|E_\theta|=\frac{\eta\,I\,\ell\,\sin\theta}{2\lambda r},\qquad\eta=\sqrt{\mu_0/\varepsilon_0}\approx120\pi\ \Omega`}/>
   It falls as $1/r$, not $1/r^2$ or $1/r^3$ like near fields: that is what makes it radiation.

2. **Power density.** For a plane wave the time-averaged Poynting vector is $S = |E|^2/(2\eta)$ (peak phasors).

3. **Integrate over a sphere.** The power through an element of area $r^2\,d\Omega$ is $S\,r^2\,d\Omega$, and $r$ cancels:
<Equation tex={String.raw`P=\frac{\eta I^2\ell^2}{8\lambda^2}\int\sin^2\theta\,d\Omega=\frac{\eta I^2\ell^2}{8\lambda^2}\cdot\frac{8\pi}{3}=\frac{\pi\eta}{3}\left(\frac{\ell}{\lambda}\right)^2I^2`}/>
   using $\int\sin^2\theta\,d\Omega = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\int_0^\pi\sin^3\theta\,d\theta\,d\phi = 2\pi\cdot\tfrac43$.

4. **Define the resistance** that would dissipate the same power, $P = \tfrac12I^2R_{rad}$:
<Equation tex={String.raw`R_{rad}=\frac{2\pi\eta}{3}\left(\frac{\ell}{\lambda}\right)^2=80\pi^2\left(\frac{\ell}{\lambda}\right)^2\ \Omega`}/>

</div>

The derivation describes a **Hertzian current element**: current is uniform along its length. A short, centre-fed wire has open ends, so its current tapers approximately linearly from the feed current $I_0$ to zero. Radiation depends on the integral of that current, rather than physical length alone. Define the effective length by

<Equation tex={String.raw`\ell_{eff}=\frac{1}{I_0}\int_{-\ell/2}^{\ell/2}I(z)\,dz,\qquad I(z)\approx I_0\left(1-\frac{2|z|}{\ell}\right)`}/>

The area under this triangle is $I_0\ell/2$, so $\ell_{eff}=\ell/2$. Substitute this effective length for the uniform element's length in its far field. The field halves and radiated power quarters at the same feed current:

<Equation tex={String.raw`R_{rad,wire}=80\pi^2\left(\frac{\ell_{eff}}{\lambda}\right)^2=20\pi^2\left(\frac{\ell}{\lambda}\right)^2\ \Omega`}/>

A **1 m centre-fed wire dipole** at 1 MHz has $\lambda$ = 300 m and $R_{rad}$ ≈ **2.19 mΩ**. With 1 Ω of conductor and matching loss, radiation efficiency is only $R_{rad}/(R_{rad}+R_{loss})$ = **0.219%**. A uniform-current element with the same physical length would give 8.77 mΩ; use the current distribution that belongs to the actual structure. Top loading can change that distribution, but needs its own model.

Frequency raises the resistance rapidly while the antenna remains electrically short. At 10 MHz the same wire model gives 0.219 Ω and 18.0% efficiency with the same assumed loss. The fixed-loss assumption is only a comparison: skin effect and matching losses also vary with frequency. Once length approaches a significant fraction of a wavelength, use the antenna's full current distribution. A small antenna can be efficient if its losses are exceptionally low; small radiation resistance makes that difficult, rather than impossible.

The current-integral distinction is developed in [MIT's antenna notes, §3.2](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-661-receivers-antennas-and-signals-spring-2003/10025a78ab17e1e0866126246b751d11_ch3new.pdf).
</section>

<section id="practical">

<div className="section-heading"><span>02</span><h2>Use half-wave dipoles and monopoles</h2></div>

A real dipole is not short, and its current is not uniform: it falls to zero at the open ends. For a centre-fed half-wave dipole the current is approximately $I\cos(2\pi z/\lambda)$, and repeating the integration of section 01 with that distribution (which needs a numerical integral of the cosine-integral function) gives $R_{rad}$ ≈ **73 Ω** with a small inductive reactance that vanishes when the dipole is trimmed a few percent shorter than $\lambda/2$. That resistance is close to common coax impedances, which is one reason the half-wave dipole is the reference antenna.

**The monopole and image theory.** A quarter-wave vertical conductor over a large conducting plane behaves, above the plane, exactly like a half-wave dipole: the plane's currents produce the same field as a mirror-image conductor below it (the image argument of Chapter 101, section 04). But all of the power now goes into the upper half-space, so the same field is produced with half the power: $R_{rad}$ ≈ 36.5 Ω. A monopole needs its ground plane; a short whip on a small board uses the board itself, and the board's currents become part of the antenna.

<DipolePatternFigure/>

At 2.44 GHz, $\lambda$ = 123 mm in air. A quarter-wave monopole is about 31 mm before end-effect shortening. On a PCB the effective permittivity shortens printed antennas further, and nearby components, plastic and a hand all detune them; matching networks (Chapter 104) on the feed recover the match after the product is assembled.

</section>

<section id="gain">

<div className="section-heading"><span>03</span><h2>Define directivity, gain and effective area</h2></div>

**Directivity** compares the peak radiation intensity $U$ (power per steradian) with the average over all directions: $D = 4\pi U_{max}/P$. For the short dipole, $U \propto \sin^2\theta$ peaks at 1 and averages to $(8\pi/3)/4\pi = 2/3$, so $D = 1.5$ (1.76 dBi). The half-wave dipole's narrower pattern gives 1.64 (2.15 dBi). **Gain** includes losses: $G = e_{rad}D$ with radiation efficiency $e_{rad} = R_{rad}/(R_{rad} + R_{loss})$.

A receiving antenna is characterized by its **effective area**: the power delivered to a matched load divided by the incident power density.

<div className="derivation">

<span className="tiny-label">DERIVATION / EFFECTIVE AREA OF A SHORT DIPOLE, AND THE GENERAL RESULT</span>

1. **Open-circuit voltage.** A broadside field $E$ polarized along a short dipole induces $V_{oc}=E\ell_{eff}$. Use $\ell_{eff}=\ell$ for the uniform element or $\ell/2$ for a centre-fed short wire, consistently with its radiation resistance.

2. **Thévenin model.** The antenna is a source $V_{oc}$ behind its radiation resistance. A conjugate-matched load (Chapter 104) receives the available power $V_{oc}^2/(8R_{rad})$ (peak phasors, so the factor is 8 rather than 4).

3. **Divide by the incident power density** $S = E^2/(2\eta)$ and substitute $R_{rad}$ from section 01:
<Equation tex={String.raw`A_e=\frac{E^2\ell_{eff}^2/8R_{rad}}{E^2/2\eta}=\frac{\eta\ell_{eff}^2}{4R_{rad}}=\frac{3\lambda^2}{8\pi}`}/>
   The effective length cancels for a lossless, conjugate-matched short dipole at its best direction and polarization. This is a single-frequency result: it does not remove matching loss or guarantee useful bandwidth.

4. **General result.** $3/(8\pi) = 1.5/(4\pi)$ and 1.5 is this antenna's directivity, so $A_e = \lambda^2D/4\pi$. Reciprocity (an antenna's transmit and receive patterns are identical) extends this to every antenna, with gain in place of directivity once losses are included:
<Equation tex={String.raw`A_e=\frac{\lambda^2}{4\pi}\,G`}/>

</div>


**Keep three power ratios separate.** Directivity describes the pattern for a given total radiated power. Radiation efficiency is radiated power divided by accepted power. Gain combines those two. With a real reference impedance, the feed also rejects $|\Gamma|^2$ of the incident power; **realized gain** includes that mismatch:

<Equation tex={String.raw`G=e_{rad}D,\qquad G_{realized}=(1-|\Gamma|^2)e_{rad}D`}/>

For an example with directivity 1.5, efficiency 10% and $|\Gamma|=0.5$, gain is 0.15 (−8.24 dBi), and realized gain is 0.1125 (−9.49 dBi). Perfect matching would recover **1.25 dB**, while eliminating the loss would recover **10 dB**. A low S₁₁ alone cannot establish an efficient antenna: a well-matched resistor can absorb almost everything and radiate almost nothing. If a measured realized-gain specification already includes mismatch, do not subtract it again in a link budget.

<p>The triangular-current model gives $R_{rad}=20\pi^2(0.15/11.11)^2$ = <strong>0.0360 Ω</strong>. <section id="friis">

<div className="section-heading"><span>04</span><h2>Derive the Friis link equation</h2></div>

<div className="derivation">

<span className="tiny-label">DERIVATION / FREE-SPACE PATH LOSS</span>

1. **Spread the transmitted power.** An isotropic transmitter's power $P_t$ spreads over a sphere of area $4\pi d^2$. A transmitting gain $G_t$ concentrates it in the receiver's direction: $S = P_tG_t/(4\pi d^2)$.

2. **Collect it.** The receiving antenna delivers $P_r = SA_e$ with $A_e = \lambda^2G_r/4\pi$:
<Equation tex={String.raw`P_r=P_tG_tG_r\left(\frac{\lambda}{4\pi d}\right)^2`}/>

3. **In decibels.** The factor $(4\pi d/\lambda)^2$ is the free-space path loss. Power falls 20 dB per decade of distance, and the loss at a given distance grows 20 dB per decade of frequency only because the effective area of a fixed-gain antenna shrinks as $\lambda^2$.
<Equation tex={String.raw`P_r[\mathrm{dBm}]=P_t+G_t+G_r-20\log_{10}\frac{4\pi d}{\lambda}`}/>

</div>


Use gain in the direction of the other antenna, rather than its maximum gain unless that direction is aligned. For linearly polarized antennas with a relative angle $\psi$, the received field projects onto the receiver's axis, so received power gains a factor $\cos^2\psi$. A 45° mismatch costs 3.01 dB; an ideal 90° mismatch produces a null. Reflections and cross-polarization make real nulls finite. Cable loss, connector loss, mismatch and fading consume additional budget. Free-space propagation also assumes the antennas are sufficiently separated and unobstructed; it does not predict an indoor coverage map.

<span className="tiny-label">WORKED EXAMPLE / A 2.4 GHz LINK BUDGET</span><h3>0 dBm transmitter, two 2 dBi antennas, 1 MHz channel</h3>

<p><strong>Receiver floor.</strong> Chapter 106's thermal floor is −174 dBm/Hz; over 1 MHz it is −114 dBm. A 6 dB noise figure raises it to −108 dBm, and a demodulator needing 13 dB SNR sets the sensitivity at <strong>−95 dBm</strong>.</p>

<p><strong>Path.</strong> At 2.44 GHz, $\lambda$ = 0.123 m, and the loss at 1 m is $20\log_{10}(4\pi/0.123)$ = 40.2 dB. The budget allows $0 + 2 + 2 - (-95) = 99$ dB of path loss, which is 58.8 dB beyond the 1 m value, a factor of $10^{58.8/20}$ ≈ 870 in distance. Free-space range is about <strong>870 m</strong>.</p>

<p><strong>Margin.</strong> Walls, bodies and multipath fading can cost 20 dB or more indoors. Reserving 20 dB divides the range by ten, to about 87 m. Each 6 dB of extra margin halves free-space range again.</p>

</div>

<div className="section-heading"><span>05</span><h2>Locate the far field before measuring</h2></div>

Near a small antenna, within about $\lambda/2\pi$, the stored reactive fields dominate and fall faster than $1/r$; the ratio $E/H$ depends on whether the source is mainly electric (high impedance) or magnetic (low impedance). Gain and path-loss formulas apply only in the far field.

For an antenna with a largest dimension $D$ that is not small, a second condition applies: rays from its edges and centre must arrive nearly in phase. The extra path from an edge to a point at distance $r$ on axis is $\sqrt{r^2 + (D/2)^2} - r \approx D^2/(8r)$. Requiring this to be under $\lambda/16$ (a 22.5° phase error) gives

<Equation tex={String.raw`r\ge\frac{2D^2}{\lambda}`}/>

A 0.3 m dish at 10 GHz ($\lambda$ = 3 cm) needs 6 m before its pattern is meaningful. EMC measurements at 3 m on a 1 m product at 1 GHz are inside this boundary, which is why emission test standards specify distance and antenna arrangement exactly rather than leaving them to the formula.

</section>

<section id="emissions">

<div className="section-heading"><span>06</span><h2>Estimate what a board or cable radiates</h2></div>

Two small-antenna results cover most accidental radiators. A **current loop** of area $A$ much smaller than $\lambda$ is a magnetic dipole; repeating section 01 for it gives a far field $E = \eta\pi I A/(\lambda^2r)$ at its peak. Substituting $\eta = 120\pi$ and $\lambda = c/f$:

<Equation tex={String.raw`E_{loop}\approx1.32\times10^{-14}\,\frac{f^2AI}{r}\quad(\text{V/m, SI units})`}/>

A **short conductor** carrying approximately uniform common-mode current uses the current-element field $\eta I\ell/(2\lambda r)$. The current-integral form above applies if current varies along it. For an illustrative image-plane arrangement where the direct and reflected fields add in phase, doubling the field gives:

<Equation tex={String.raw`E_{cm}\approx1.26\times10^{-6}\,\frac{f\ell I}{r}`}/>

<BoardEmissionPlot/>

Both formulas here use **peak sinusoidal current and peak field**, inherited from section 01. For RMS current and RMS field the coefficient is the same, but a peak field expressed in dB is 3.01 dB above its RMS value. Instrument detectors and test limits need their specified conventions. The factor of two for the reflected field is a geometry assumption: polarization, antenna height, ground conductivity and phase can change the actual sum.

At 100 MHz and 3 m, the model gives **38.9 dBµV/m peak** for a 1 cm² loop carrying 20 mA peak, and **42.0 dBµV/m peak** for a **15 cm conductor** carrying 20 µA peak. A thousand times less current produces a comparable field because electric and magnetic dipoles couple differently. This is why a small amount of current escaping onto a connector can matter even when the differential signal looks clean.

The plotted comparison uses 10 m distance and 30–100 MHz. The conductor remains at most $\lambda/20$ long, and the observer is at least a wavelength away. Extending the short-conductor equation to a 1 m cable at 1 GHz would violate its assumptions. Long cables have standing-wave current, resonance and a frequency-dependent effective length; measure their current distribution or use a suitable antenna/channel model. A crossover inferred from invalid high-frequency extrapolation is not a design result.

**A measurement sequence.** Measure cable common-mode current with a characterized current probe, distinguish it from differential current, and compare emissions while changing cable position and the connector's return path. Then test a choke or connector filter for common-mode attenuation while checking that its parasitics preserve the intended differential signal. Keep the board's hot loops small and bond shields according to the enclosure and interface design. An emission estimate helps identify a mechanism; compliance depends on the applicable standard, detector, bandwidth, geometry and test site.
</section>
