<section id="rs485">

<div className="section-heading"><span>01</span><h2>Build an RS-485 bus and a separate message protocol</h2></div>

RS-485 defines a balanced electrical interface; it does not define the bytes, addresses or checksum of a message. UART framing and application protocols such as Modbus RTU can use that physical layer, while other framing is also possible. Two-wire half duplex shares one pair for both directions; four-wire arrangements use separate directions and need their own topology plan.

Use a trunk with short stubs and termination at its physical ends when the edge/channel conditions require it. A common 120 Ω cable with a 120 Ω resistor at each end presents approximately 60 Ω differential DC load to an active driver. Every node does not get a termination resistor: that would overload the bus. Classic unit-load accounting describes allowable receiver loading; modern fractional-unit-load devices can increase electrical node count, but capacitance, stubs and timing still impose limits.

Differential reception has a finite common-mode range. Check the chosen transceiver across ground offsets, transients and powered-off states; baseline RS-485 conventions do not imply unlimited voltage immunity. Include a suitable reference conductor or galvanic isolation for the system's conditions. Cable shield and signal reference have different jobs. A connector pin named A or B is not sufficient evidence of polarity because naming conventions differ; verify the manufacturer's truth table and expected differential waveform.

An idle bus with all drivers disabled needs a defined receiver state. Some transceivers guarantee fail-safe output for open, short or idle conditions; others need external bias. Bias resistors must overcome termination and leakage while staying within driver loading and common-mode limits. Apply bias in a planned place rather than adding strong pull-ups and pull-downs at every node.

For a simple symmetric bias example, pull one line to 5 V and the other to ground with equal resistors R; the two 120 Ω terminations are equivalent to 60 Ω between lines. The idle differential voltage is $5\times60/(2R+60)$. At R = 680 Ω it is 0.211 V. This nominal value leaves little margin around a 0.2 V decision scale, so tolerance, leakage and guaranteed receiver thresholds must be included; modern fail-safe parts may use a different bias requirement.

<Equation tex={String.raw`V_{idle,diff}=V_{bias}\frac{R_T}{2R+R_T},\qquad R_T=120\parallel120=60\ \Omega`}/>
Driver enable timing is part of half-duplex framing. Do not disable the driver merely when software writes the last byte or when a FIFO becomes empty: wait until the shift register has completed the final stop bit, then include disable delay and remote turnaround. At 115200 bit/s an 8N1 byte lasts 86.81 μs. A late enable can lose the start bit; early disable truncates the final byte; overlapping enables can create contention. Add protocol ownership and timeout rules.

Primary implementation reference: [TI, The RS-485 Design Guide](https://www.ti.com/lit/an/slla272d/slla272d.pdf). Use the selected transceiver's electrical limits with the cable and message protocol.

<section id="can">

<div className="section-heading"><span>02</span><h2>Understand dominant arbitration and fault containment</h2></div>

CAN combines a shared bus protocol with a compatible physical layer. In a common high-speed two-wire physical implementation, a dominant state creates differential voltage while recessive releases the bus toward its recessive condition. A dominant state wins over recessive, enabling nondestructive bitwise arbitration: a transmitter sending recessive but observing dominant stops competing while the winning frame continues.

For standard 11-bit data frames, a lower numerical identifier has priority when other framing conditions are equal because identifier bits are transmitted most significant first and dominant zero wins. The identifier labels message priority/meaning rather than necessarily a destination address. Extended frames and remote/data distinctions add arbitration bits; do not reduce every possible arbitration case to integer comparison alone.

A CAN controller handles framing, CRC, acknowledgment, stuffing and error state machinery. A transceiver converts controller TX/RX logic to bus voltages; it does not replace the controller. Classical CAN payloads reach eight bytes. CAN FD extends payload up to 64 bytes and can use a faster data phase after arbitration, requiring compatible controllers, transceivers and channel timing. A mixed network cannot assume that legacy active controllers accept FD frames without an appropriate compatibility strategy.

Arbitration requires nodes to observe the bus within the relevant bit timing. Round-trip cable delay, transceiver delay and sampling point consume propagation allowance. For a 20 m cable at 2 × 10⁸ m/s, one-way flight is 100 ns and round trip 200 ns, before transceiver and controller delay. That is 20% of a 1 μs arbitration bit and 100% of a 200 ns fast-data bit; these simple ratios show why length, rate and topology must be assessed together rather than as independent headline limits. FD data-phase sampling can use transmitter-delay compensation and different constraints, so the round-trip fraction is not itself a maximum-rate rule.

Terminate the physical bus at both ends according to the transceiver/cable design, commonly 120 Ω for high-speed CAN. Split termination can establish a filtered midpoint and reduce some common-mode emissions. Stubs, connector discontinuities and asymmetry matter especially during a faster FD data phase. Read bus common-mode and fault tolerances, not only differential thresholds.

Error counters and error-active, error-passive and bus-off behavior help contain faults. Software must define recovery and reporting rather than retrying blindly forever. A CAN ACK establishes that a receiver recognized the frame at the protocol level; it does not prove that the intended application processed it. Higher-level protocols assign identifiers, message contents, heartbeat and timeout semantics.

Primary references: [TI, Controller Area Network Physical Layer Requirements](https://www.ti.com/lit/an/slla270/slla270.pdf) and [Bosch, CAN FD Protocol](https://www.bosch-semiconductors.com/products/ip-modules/can-protocols/can-fd/).

<section id="usb">

<div className="section-heading"><span>03</span><h2>Keep USB signaling, host behavior and Type-C distinct</h2></div>

USB defines a host-managed connection with enumeration, descriptors, endpoints and transfer types. A device does not merely emit UART bytes on a differential pair. It needs a USB controller or suitable bridge, firmware behavior and an electrical interface meeting the selected speed's requirements. A USB-to-UART bridge encapsulates a byte stream over USB; those are two different links in one system.

USB 2.0 includes low speed 1.5 Mbit/s, full speed 12 Mbit/s and high speed 480 Mbit/s. Signaling, termination and speed detection differ, so do not build a generic LVDS driver and expect USB compatibility. Control transfers configure devices; bulk emphasizes reliable delivery with bandwidth availability rather than bounded latency; interrupt transfers provide scheduled service under their rules; isochronous prioritizes time delivery and has different error/retry behavior.

PCB D+/D− routing commonly targets 90 Ω differential impedance for the USB 2.0 channel, using the actual stack-up. Keep the pair and return continuous, control stubs and place low-capacitance protection according to the PHY and connector design. Avoid adding arbitrary termination: USB PHYs contain speed-specific structures. The connector, ESD device and common-mode filter must be part of the channel model and compliance test.

Type-C describes a reversible connector and attachment/role mechanisms using CC pins; it does not automatically mean a particular data speed or Power Delivery capability. A USB 2.0-only Type-C implementation still needs correct CC/role and power behavior. Power Delivery is an additional negotiated protocol. Use an appropriate controller or validated implementation for the intended role instead of assuming that every Type-C cable makes the board a power source.

A 480 Mbit/s wire rate is not 60 MB/s guaranteed application payload. Tokens, headers, handshakes, scheduling and bus sharing consume capacity. Driver buffering and operating-system timing matter to a measurement instrument; timestamp samples at acquisition when precise sample time is needed, rather than inferring time from when a USB read completes.

Primary references: [USB-IF, USB 2.0 Specification](https://www.usb.org/document-library/usb-20-specification) and [TI, USB Layout Basics](https://www.ti.com/video/6087491555001). The endpoint/controller documentation for the chosen device supplies concrete implementation limits.

</section>

<section id="ethernet">

<div className="section-heading"><span>04</span><h2>Separate MAC packets from the cable PHY</h2></div>

Ethernet uses a MAC for frame handling and a PHY for the electrical or optical channel. On common twisted-pair copper systems, the PHY provides coding, equalization, link training/negotiation and analog transmission. The MCU-to-PHY interface is a separate timed board interface such as MII, RMII or RGMII; its clock and delay constraints are not the same as the cable differential-pair constraints.

Common 100BASE-TX uses two cable pairs for transmit and receive directions; 1000BASE-T uses four bidirectional pairs with signal processing to manage echo and crosstalk. These are specific PHY families, not a definition of every Ethernet variant: single-pair and optical systems use other arrangements. Choose the precise standard, PHY and magnetics before assigning connector pins or routing pairs.

For a common copper PHY-to-magnetics route, 100 Ω differential is a typical design target. Transformer coupling, common-mode chokes, center-tap networks, termination and ESD paths depend on PHY architecture and magnetics. A voltage-mode PHY and current-mode PHY can require different center-tap treatment. Follow the selected PHY reference design; a copied network from another part can compromise link behavior or emissions.

Do not apply a blanket plane or chassis rule beneath every Ethernet component. The PHY-side pair, transformer barrier, cable side and shield have different return and isolation requirements. Keep the insulation barrier and chassis/ESD current path consistent with the design while preserving a controlled field path where required. Power over Ethernet adds detection, classification, power-conversion and isolation design; it is not permission to put an arbitrary supply on spare connector pins.

Frame overhead limits application rate. For a simplified 1500-byte payload frame, count 14-byte Ethernet header, four-byte FCS, eight-byte preamble/start delimiter and 12-byte inter-frame-gap time. The channel spends 1538 byte-times per 1500 bytes before VLAN and higher-layer overhead. Payload efficiency is about 97.53%. At 100 Mbit/s, that gives about 97.53 Mbit/s maximum frame-payload rate under continuous ideal traffic, with less available to an actual application.

<Equation tex={String.raw`\eta_{frame}=\frac{1500}{1500+14+4+8+12}=0.97529`}/>
IP, UDP/TCP headers, packet sizes, retransmission and software processing reduce useful rate further. UDP does not itself guarantee delivery or order; TCP provides an ordered reliable byte stream with congestion and buffering behavior that does not guarantee a fixed latency. Select an application framing and timestamp strategy appropriate to the measurement, independent of raw link speed.

Primary practical reference: [TI, Ethernet PHY PCB Design Layout Checklist](https://www.ti.com/lit/an/snla387/snla387.pdf). Consult the chosen PHY and relevant IEEE 802.3 specification for the concrete media and timing requirements.

</section>

<section id="choice">

<div className="section-heading"><span>05</span><h2>Compare the link by its failure and timing budgets</h2></div>

| Interface | Who controls access? | Electrical design focus | Application responsibility |
|---|---|---|---|
| RS-485 | Chosen message protocol | Trunk, end termination, bias, ground range | Framing, addresses, integrity, turnaround |
| CAN / CAN FD | Bitwise arbitration | Propagation, stubs, common mode, rate phase | Message meaning, priorities, timeouts |
| USB | Host scheduling | Speed-specific PHY, channel, attachment and power | Descriptors, endpoints, buffering, sample timestamps |
| Ethernet | Switched/framed link behavior | PHY, magnetics/media, channel and clock interfaces | Network transport, packet meaning, timing and recovery |

A differential pair alone does not make these interfaces interchangeable. Cable length, impedance, termination, bias and protocol access are joint design choices. RS-232 remains useful for certain point-to-point asynchronous links with a suitable voltage/inversion transceiver. LIN adds a defined low-cost shared single-wire automotive scheme with its own framing and scheduling. Digital audio I²S uses a clock and word-select interface with different synchronization behavior from addressed sensor buses. Choose the intended device ecosystem and electrical environment before the connector.

Protection needs both a component and a current path. An ESD diode with excellent rating can fail to protect a pin if the discharge return passes through sensitive logic ground or a long inductive route. Isolation controls ground-current paths but adds barrier capacitance and propagation delay. Document surge/ESD requirements, common-mode range, safe power states and recovery behavior for the actual installed system.

For a converter streaming data, calculate sustained payload, maximum outage buffering, sample timestamps and behavior on dropped packets. If 16-bit samples at 1 MS/s produce 2 MB/s and the host can pause 20 ms, at least 40 kB payload buffering is needed before margin and metadata. A nominally fast link can still lose measurements when its service is bursty. Validate the full system with realistic cable, traffic, faults and power sequencing.
