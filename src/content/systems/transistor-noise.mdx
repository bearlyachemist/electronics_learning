<section id="model">

<div className="section-heading"><span>01</span><h2>Model an amplifier's noise with e<sub>n</sub> and i<sub>n</sub></h2></div>

Any amplifier's internal noise can be replaced, at its input, by two sources: a voltage source $e_n$ in series and a current source $i_n$ in parallel. The voltage source matters when the source resistance is low; the current source, flowing through the source resistance, matters when it is high. With a source of resistance $R_s$, the total input-referred noise is
<Equation tex={String.raw`e_{tot}^2=e_n^2+(i_nR_s)^2+4kTR_s`}/>
assuming $e_n$ and $i_n$ are uncorrelated. The design goal is to make the first two terms small compared with the third, the source's own Johnson noise, which nothing can remove.

</section>

<section id="bjt">

<div className="section-heading"><span>02</span><h2>Derive a BJT's noise from its currents</h2></div>

<div className="derivation">

<span className="tiny-label">DERIVATION / BIPOLAR e<sub>n</sub> AND i<sub>n</sub></span>

1. **Collector shot noise.** The collector current has shot noise $\sqrt{2qI_C}$. Referred to the input, it is the base-emitter voltage that would produce the same current change: divide by $g_m = I_C/V_T$ (Chapter 30):
<Equation tex={String.raw`e_{n,shot}=\frac{\sqrt{2qI_C}}{g_m}=V_T\sqrt{\frac{2q}{I_C}}=\sqrt{\frac{2kT\,V_T}{I_C}}`}/>
   It falls as $1/\sqrt{I_C}$: at 1 mA, 0.46 nV/√Hz; at 10 µA, 4.6 nV/√Hz.

2. **Base spreading resistance.** The resistance $r_{bb'}$ between the base terminal and the active base adds Johnson noise $\sqrt{4kTr_{bb'}}$ in series. With 50 Ω, 0.91 nV/√Hz: a floor $e_n$ cannot fall below however much current flows.

3. **Base shot noise.** The base current $I_C/\beta$ has shot noise:
<Equation tex={String.raw`i_n=\sqrt{\frac{2qI_C}{\beta}}`}/>
   It rises as $\sqrt{I_C}$: 1.3 pA/√Hz at 1 mA with β = 200.

4. **Total voltage noise:** $e_n^2 = 4kTr_{bb'} + 2kTV_T/I_C$.

</div>

<section id="optimum">

<div className="section-heading"><span>03</span><h2>Choose the current for a given source</h2></div>

<div className="derivation">

<span className="tiny-label">DERIVATION / OPTIMUM COLLECTOR CURRENT AND NOISE RESISTANCE</span>

1. **Noise resistance.** Define $R_n = e_n/i_n$. When $R_s = R_n$ the two amplifier terms are equal; this is the source resistance at which the amplifier's noise figure is lowest (Chapter 114 derived $R_{opt} = e_n/i_n$).

2. **Optimum current.** Neglecting $r_{bb'}$, minimize $e_n^2 + i_n^2R_s^2 = 2kTV_T/I_C + 2qI_CR_s^2/\beta$ over $I_C$. Setting the derivative to zero, $2kTV_T/I_C^2 = 2qR_s^2/\beta$, and using $kT/q = V_T$:
<Equation tex={String.raw`I_{C,opt}=\frac{V_T\sqrt\beta}{R_s}`}/>

3. **Minimum noise factor** at that current:
<Equation tex={String.raw`F_{min}\approx1+\frac{1}{\sqrt\beta}`}/>
   With β = 200, $F_{min}$ = 1.07, a noise figure of 0.3 dB, independent of $R_s$, provided the current can be chosen freely and $r_{bb'}$ is small compared with $R_s$.

</div>

| Source resistance | $I_{C,opt}$ (β = 200) | Note |
|---|---|---|
| 100 Ω | 3.7 mA | $r_{bb'}$ noise now significant; use low-$r_{bb'}$ transistors or parallel devices |
| 10 kΩ | 37 µA | Typical sensor; easy |
| 1 MΩ | 0.37 µA | Low β at this current and 1/f noise argue for a JFET instead |

<section id="jfet">

<div className="section-heading"><span>04</span><h2>Compare JFETs</h2></div>

A JFET's voltage noise comes from Johnson noise of its channel, referred to the gate through its transconductance: $e_n^2 \approx 4kT(\tfrac23)/g_m$. Its current noise is the shot noise of the tiny gate leakage, $i_n = \sqrt{2qI_G}$: picoamperes of leakage give femtoamperes per root hertz, a thousand times less than a BJT's.

At comparable current, a JFET's $g_m$ is several times lower than a BJT's, so its $e_n$ is higher, typically 1–5 nV/√Hz for large-area parts. But its noise resistance $e_n/i_n$ is megohms to gigohms. **Above roughly 100 kΩ of source resistance, a JFET wins decisively**: piezoelectric sensors, capacitive microphones, photodiodes in transimpedance amplifiers (Chapter 92), electrometers. Below about 1 kΩ, a BJT at high current wins. In between, both can be good. MOSFETs offer still lower gate current but higher 1/f noise, making them best at high frequency or in chopper-stabilized amplifiers.

Gate leakage doubles roughly every 10 °C, so a JFET's current noise rises with temperature; specify it at the operating temperature.

</section>

<section id="low">

<div className="section-heading"><span>05</span><h2>Handle a very low source resistance</h2></div>

A source of a few ohms (a moving-coil microphone, a loudspeaker used as a sensor, a thermocouple or strain gauge) generates only nanovolt-level noise: 8 Ω gives 0.36 nV/√Hz. Even a single BJT at high current has $e_n$ near 1 nV/√Hz.

<div className="derivation">

<span className="tiny-label">DERIVATION / PARALLEL DEVICES AND STEP-UP TRANSFORMERS</span>

1. **N transistors in parallel**, each at the same current, sum their signals coherently but their noises in power. Their combined $e_n$ falls by $\sqrt N$ and their combined $i_n$ rises by $\sqrt N$, so the noise resistance falls by $N$. Ten devices at 1 mA each bring $e_n$ from 1 nV/√Hz to about 0.32 nV/√Hz.

2. **A transformer** with turns ratio $1:n$ multiplies the source voltage, and its Johnson noise, by $n$, and its resistance by $n^2$. The amplifier then sees $n^2R_s$. Choosing $n^2R_s = R_n$ matches the source to the amplifier's optimum:
<Equation tex={String.raw`n=\sqrt{\frac{R_n}{R_s}}`}/>

3. **Example.** A loudspeaker of 8 Ω used as a microphone, with a BJT at 1 mA ($e_n$ ≈ 1.0 nV/√Hz, $i_n$ = 1.3 pA/√Hz, $R_n$ ≈ 800 Ω): $n = \sqrt{800/8}$ = 10. The source now looks like 800 Ω with 3.6 nV/√Hz of Johnson noise, against an amplifier contributing $\sqrt{1.0^2 + (1.3\times0.8)^2}$ ≈ 1.4 nV/√Hz: noise figure about 0.6 dB, against about 9 dB connected directly.

</div>

Transformers cost bandwidth, pick up hum, and add their own winding resistance, so parallel transistors are often preferred on circuit boards; for the lowest noise from a few ohms, both are combined.

</section>
