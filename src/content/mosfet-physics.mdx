<section id="surface">

## 01 / The gate, oxide and semiconductor form a capacitor

A MOS structure places a conductor above a semiconductor with a thin insulating oxide between them. Gate charge is balanced by charge in the semiconductor. For a p-type body, a negative gate attracts majority-carrier holes toward the surface: **accumulation**. Moving the gate positive first repels holes, exposing fixed ionized acceptors: **depletion**. A sufficiently positive surface potential attracts enough electrons that the surface becomes n-like: **inversion**.

The oxide capacitance per area is εox/tox. Reducing oxide thickness raises capacitance and strengthens electrostatic control, but also changes field stress and leakage constraints. Threshold depends on gate/body work-function differences, oxide charge, body doping and the charge needed to establish inversion; it is not simply “the voltage required to cross the oxide.”

Above threshold, a first-order channel charge expression is Cox(VGS − VT − V(x)). The local channel voltage subtracts from effective gate drive. Integrating that charge along the device produced the triode and saturation laws in chapter 47. The same relation explains both: the drain end loses channel charge first because it is at the highest channel potential.

An enhancement n-channel device has positive threshold in the usual convention. A depletion n-channel MOSFET has negative threshold, so zero gate-source voltage already exceeds threshold. A negative threshold does not imply a negative drain current.

</section>

<section id="body">

## 02 / The source is not always tied to the body

When the source rises relative to a p-type body, the source-body junction becomes more reverse biased. More depletion charge must be balanced by the gate before strong inversion occurs. A common model is:

<Equation tex="V_T=V_{T0}+\gamma\left(\sqrt{2\phi_F+V_{SB}}-\sqrt{2\phi_F}\right)" />

Take VT0 = 1 V, γ = 0.5 √V and 2φF = 0.6 V. With VSB = 2 V, threshold rises to approximately 1.419 V. A follower whose source rises while its body stays at ground therefore needs more gate-source voltage than a body-tied-source model predicts.

Differentiating the threshold law gives a body-effect factor:

<Equation tex="\eta=\frac{\partial V_T}{\partial V_{SB}}=\frac{\gamma}{2\sqrt{2\phi_F+V_{SB}}},\qquad g_{mb}\approx\eta g_m" />

Using vbs = vb − vs, the incremental drain-current model becomes id = gmvgs + gmbvbs + gdsvds. With both drain and body at AC ground, follower gain is approximately gm/(gm + gmb + gds + GL). Body effect adds a restoring response to source motion, lowering gain.

For a discrete MOSFET with body internally tied to source, VSB remains near zero internally and this particular external body-effect mechanism is absent. Do not add a fixed-body term to a circuit whose body follows its source.

</section>

<section id="curves">

## 03 / Threshold is the beginning of conduction

A datasheet threshold voltage is usually measured at a specified small drain current. It is not the gate voltage that guarantees a low-resistance power switch. If a device's threshold spans 1–3 V, that does not establish useful on-resistance with a 3.3 V driver. The relevant guarantee is RDS(on) at the actual gate drive, temperature and other stated conditions.

In the simple strong-inversion model:

<Equation tex="I_D=\frac{k}{2}(V_{GS}-V_T)^2,\qquad g_m=\frac{2I_D}{V_{GS}-V_T}" />

At k = 4 mA/V², VT = 2 V and VGS = 3 V, current is 2 mA and gm = 4 mS provided VDS is at least 1 V. A 0.2 V threshold increase at the same gate voltage reduces the modeled current to 1.28 mA. This illustrates why open-loop MOS current settings can be inaccurate.

Below threshold, diffusion-related channel transport produces an approximately exponential current:

<Equation tex="I_D\propto e^{(V_{GS}-V_T)/(nU_T)},\qquad U_T=\frac{k_BT}{q}" />

At room temperature, the ideal n = 1 slope is about 60 mV per decade; ordinary devices have n greater than one and additional complications. The square-law plot deliberately shows a hard cutoff to isolate strong-inversion behavior. Do not use its zero-current prediction as a leakage guarantee.

</section>

<section id="compare">

## 04 / JFET and MOSFET: compare mechanisms

| Property | Conventional JFET | MOSFET |
|---|---|---|
| Gate structure | Reverse-biased junction | Insulated gate |
| Zero-bias channel | Normally present | Depends on enhancement/depletion type |
| Usual n-channel control | Negative VGS reduces current | VGS above threshold strengthens channel |
| Main input concerns | Junction leakage and forward bias | Leakage, oxide stress, capacitance and protection paths |
| Useful analog roles | Low-current sensor inputs, low-noise stages, current sources | Broad range of integrated analog, switching and power roles |
| Reverse conduction | Depends on channel/junction geometry | Discrete power parts usually include a body diode |

Both devices are voltage-controlled transconductors at a bias point. Both have output conductance, capacitance, noise and parameter spread. Neither possesses a universal fixed voltage drop analogous to an oversimplified diode rule.

Choosing between them requires the signal-source impedance and bandwidth. A device with excellent voltage noise may have excessive input capacitance for a high-resistance source. A low-leakage part may have insufficient transconductance at the allowed power. The useful comparison is an entire front-end error and power budget.

</section>

<section id="power">

## 05 / Why a power MOSFET is more than a scaled small one

A vertical power MOSFET carries current through many cells and a voltage-supporting drift region. The drift region must withstand the off-state electric field, yet its resistance contributes to on-state loss. Package, contacts, channel, accumulation regions and drift region all contribute to total RDS(on). The elementary 1/(ku) expression captures only a simplified channel contribution.

The intrinsic body diode of a conventional n-channel power MOSFET points from source to drain. It can conduct reverse current while the channel is off. When the channel is enhanced, reverse current can also flow through the channel with a lower voltage drop than the diode, depending on current and drive.

Blocking voltage, resistance, gate charge and output charge are coupled design tradeoffs. A larger die may reduce resistance while increasing charge that the driver and switching node must move. Technology-specific structures alter the balance, so “choose the lowest resistance” is not a complete switching-device selection method.

</section>

<section id="datasheet">

## 06 / Turn curves into conditional statements

Read every graph as “under these conditions.” Typical transfer curves are not guaranteed production bounds. Capacitance curves depend strongly on drain voltage; gate-charge curves depend on drain voltage and current. Maximum drain current may assume a case temperature or pulse condition your board cannot provide.

A useful reading order is pinout and body-diode orientation, absolute terminal ratings, guaranteed resistance at your gate drive, charge and timing conditions, thermal information, then the safe operating area. Separate limits that apply independently from limits that cannot all be reached simultaneously.

For linear circuits, look for behavior at the actual bias current and drain voltage. For switching, use a resistance and charge model in the fully enhanced state. Returning to the square-law model is valuable for intuition, but substituting it for measured power-device characteristics can produce very wrong heat and timing estimates.

</section>
