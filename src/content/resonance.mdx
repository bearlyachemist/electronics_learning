<div className="opening-grid">

<div><span className="tiny-label">A PRACTICAL QUESTION</span><p className="opening-question">A 1 V source drives a series inductor and capacitor. How can the capacitor end up with 31 V across it?</p></div>

<div>

An inductor’s reactance is positive and rises with frequency; a capacitor’s is negative and falls. At one frequency they are equal and opposite. In series they cancel and leave almost nothing to limit the current; in parallel they form an impedance that is almost infinite.

That cancellation is **resonance**. It escapes the RC limit of Chapter 23, giving narrow, selective responses, and it explains the hot-plug overshoot of Chapter 18. It also hides voltages and currents much larger than the source’s, which a designer has to anticipate.

</div>

</div>

<section id="series">

<div className="section-heading"><span>01</span><h2>Series resonance and Q</h2></div>

Put R, L and C in series across a source. From Chapter 20:

<Equation tex="Z=R+j\left(\omega L-\frac{1}{\omega C}\right)"/>

The reactance is zero when ωL = 1/(ωC):

<Equation tex="\omega_0=\frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}},\qquad f_0=\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{LC}}" label="RESONANT FREQUENCY"/>

At f₀ the impedance is just R, the current is V/R, and it is in phase with the source. Below f₀ the capacitor’s reactance dominates and the circuit is capacitive; above, the inductor’s dominates and it is inductive.

### The characteristic impedance and Q

At resonance the two reactances are equal. Their common value is the **characteristic impedance**, already met in the hot-plug analysis of Chapter 18:

<Equation tex="Z_0=\omega_0L=\frac{1}{\omega_0C}=\sqrt{\frac LC}"/>

The **quality factor** Q compares that reactance with the resistance:

<Equation tex="Q=\frac{Z_0}{R}=\frac{\omega_0L}{R}=\frac{1}{\omega_0RC}" label="SERIES Q"/>

Q has three equivalent meanings, each useful in a different situation:

- **Voltage magnification.** At resonance the current is V/R, so the voltage across L is I·Z₀ = Q·V. The capacitor has the same magnitude in the opposite direction. They cancel each other in the loop sum, but each is real and measurable.
- **Selectivity.** The current falls to 1/√2 of its peak (half power) when the net reactance equals R. These two frequencies are separated by a bandwidth B = R/(2πL), so B = f₀/Q.
- **Energy.** Q = 2π × (energy stored) / (energy dissipated per cycle). A high-Q circuit stores much more energy than it loses each cycle, so it keeps ringing after the drive stops.

<div className="worked-example">

<span className="tiny-label">WORKED EXAMPLE / THE OPENING QUESTION</span><h3>10 mH, 100 nF and 10 Ω, driven by 1 V</h3>

<p>f₀ = 1/(2π√(10 mH × 100 nF)) = <strong>5.03 kHz</strong>. Z₀ = √(10 mH/100 nF) = 316 Ω, so Q = 316/10 = <strong>31.6</strong> and the bandwidth is 5.03 kHz/31.6 = <strong>159 Hz</strong>.</p>

<p>At resonance I = 1 V/10 Ω = 100 mA, and |V_C| = |V_L| = 100 mA × 316 Ω = <strong>31.6 V</strong>. A capacitor rated for 25 V because “the supply is only 1 V” would be overstressed.</p>

<p>Detuned to 4.5 kHz, the current falls to 14.0 mA, leads the source by 82°, and V_C falls to 4.9 V. Just 10% away from f₀, nearly 90% of the current has gone.</p>

</div>

</section>

<section id="phasors">

<div className="section-heading"><span>02</span><h2>Phasor diagrams</h2></div>

A phasor diagram draws all the voltages and currents of a circuit as arrows at one frequency. Pick the quantity common to every element as the reference. In a series circuit that is the current, so draw I along the real axis. Then:

- V_R is parallel to I;
- V_L points 90° ahead of I;
- V_C points 90° behind I;
- placed tip to tail, the three sum to the source phasor.

At resonance V_L and V_C are equal and opposite, leaving V_R equal to the source. Below resonance V_C is longer and the sum tips below the current: the source voltage lags the current, a capacitive circuit. Above resonance it tips the other way.

### A circle hidden in the RC filter

Phasor diagrams also reveal geometry that algebra hides. In a series RC circuit, V_R and V_C are always perpendicular and always sum to Vin.

<RCLocusFigure/>

Every point on that semicircle corresponds to one frequency. At the corner frequency the tip sits at the bottom of the circle, where V_C and V_R are equal and the phase is −45°. This is the same low-pass response as Chapter 22, now as a picture.

For a parallel circuit, the shared quantity is voltage, so draw V along the real axis and add the branch currents: I_R in phase, I_C leading by 90°, I_L lagging by 90°.

</section>

<section id="parallel">

<div className="section-heading"><span>04</span><h2>Parallel resonance</h2></div>

Put L and C in parallel. Their admittances add: Y = j(ωC − 1/(ωL)). At ω₀ the susceptances cancel and the ideal parallel impedance is infinite. The tank draws no net current from the source, yet current circulates between L and C.

A real tank is limited by its losses, usually the inductor’s winding resistance r in series with L. Using the series-to-parallel conversion of Chapter 20, at resonance the inductor looks like a parallel resistance R_p = r(1 + Q²) ≈ Q²r, and the tank impedance is:

<Equation tex="Z_{\mathrm{tank}}(f_0)\approx Q^2r=QZ_0=\frac{L}{rC},\qquad Q=\frac{\omega_0L}{r}"/>

<TankFigure/>

<div className="worked-example">

<span className="tiny-label">WORKED EXAMPLE / A RADIO-FREQUENCY TANK</span><h3>100 µH, 1 nF, 5 Ω winding resistance</h3>

<p>f₀ = <strong>503 kHz</strong>, Z₀ = 316 Ω, Q = 316/5 = 63.2. At resonance the tank looks like L/(rC) = <strong>20 kΩ</strong> of pure resistance, built from components whose largest impedance is 316 Ω.</p>

<p>Feed it 1 mA at f₀ and the tank develops 20 V. The capacitor then carries 20 V/316 Ω = 63 mA: the circulating current is Q times the current supplied. Connect a 20 kΩ load across the tank and the total parallel resistance halves to 10 kΩ, halving Q to 31.6 and doubling the bandwidth. A tank’s selectivity depends on what loads it.</p>

</div>

Parallel tanks appear as the collector or drain load of tuned amplifiers, which then have high gain only near f₀; as the frequency-setting element of oscillators; and as the selecting element in radio receivers (Chapter 25). A series LC from a node to ground, conversely, is a **trap**: a near-short at one frequency, removing it like a notch.

</section>

<section id="lcfilter">

<div className="section-heading"><span>05</span><h2>LC filters</h2></div>

A series inductor followed by a shunt capacitor is a **second-order low-pass**. Including source resistance R_s and load R_L, the divider gives:

<Equation tex="H=\frac{1}{1+\dfrac{R_s}{R_L}-\omega^2LC+j\omega\left(R_sC+\dfrac{L}{R_L}\right)}"/>

At low frequency H approaches the resistive divider R_L/(R_s + R_L). Far above f₀ = 1/(2π√LC) the ω²LC term dominates and the response falls at **−40 dB/decade** (12 dB/octave): two reactive elements, two poles. Unlike an RC low-pass, an ideal LC filter dissipates nothing in its series element, which is why LC filters smooth power supplies: the DC passes without a resistive drop.

### Terminations set the Q

With R_s ≈ 0, the damping comes only from the load, and

<Equation tex="Q=\frac{R_L}{Z_0}"/>

A lightly loaded filter (R_L much greater than Z₀) has high Q and a resonant **peak** near f₀. For a second-order low-pass the peak gain is Q/√(1 − 1/(4Q²)) once Q exceeds 1/√2. Q = 1/√2 gives the maximally flat (**Butterworth**) response with no peak.

<div className="worked-example">

<span className="tiny-label">WORKED EXAMPLE / A SUPPLY RIPPLE FILTER</span><h3>100 µH and 10 µF</h3>

<p>f₀ = <strong>5.03 kHz</strong>, Z₀ = 3.16 Ω. Into a 3.16 Ω load (Q = 1) the peak is only 1.25 dB. A 2.24 Ω load (Q = 0.707) gives a flat, Butterworth response.</p>

<p>Into a 10 Ω load with 0.1 Ω of source resistance, Q ≈ 2.9 and the response peaks by about 9.3 dB. At 100 Ω, Q ≈ 16 and the peak is about 24 dB: noise near 5 kHz is <em>amplified</em> sixteen-fold.</p>

<p>Above resonance the filter works as intended. 100 kHz switching ripple is attenuated by approximately (100/5.03)² ≈ 395, or 52 dB, before capacitor ESR and ESL take over (Chapter 9).</p>

</div>

<LCFilterLab client:load/>

<p className="caption">Figure 2. Ideal LC low-pass with source and load resistance, compared with a single RC pole at the same corner and with the −40 dB/decade asymptote.</p>

High-pass LC filters swap the positions of L and C. Band-pass and band-stop LC filters use series and parallel resonators as their arms. Higher-order LC filters chain several sections; their component values come from tables (Butterworth, Chebyshev and others) normalized to a 1 Ω, 1 rad/s prototype and then impedance- and frequency-scaled exactly as in Chapter 20.

</section>

<section id="limits">

<div className="section-heading"><span>06</span><h2>Ringing, damping and real parts</h2></div>

### Q in the time domain

The step response of a second-order circuit is set by the same Q. With damping ratio ζ = 1/(2Q):

<div className="table-wrap">

<table><thead><tr><th>Q</th><th>ζ</th><th>Step response</th><th>Overshoot</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>0.5</td><td>1</td><td>Critically damped</td><td>0%</td></tr><tr><td>0.707</td><td>0.707</td><td>Butterworth</td><td>4.3%</td></tr><tr><td>1</td><td>0.5</td><td>Slight ringing</td><td>16.3%</td></tr><tr><td>5</td><td>0.1</td><td>Rings for about Q cycles</td><td>72.9%</td></tr></tbody></table>

</div>

The overshoot is e^(−πζ/√(1 − ζ²)). The hot-plug circuit of Chapter 18, with Q ≈ 12, overshot by nearly 90%. A peak in the frequency response and ringing in the time response are two views of one property.

### Components are not ideal

- **Inductor loss.** Winding resistance, core loss and skin effect limit Q, usually to tens or low hundreds. Iron or ferrite cores can saturate (Chapter 10), shifting f₀ with current.
- **Self-resonance.** An inductor’s winding capacitance makes it a parallel tank with a self-resonant frequency; a capacitor’s lead inductance makes it a series resonator (Chapter 9). Each works as intended only well below its self-resonance.
- **Tolerance.** f₀ depends on √(LC), so 5% tolerance on each component shifts f₀ by up to about 5%. A narrow filter (high Q) can be pushed entirely off its target; tunable components or trimming are common in selective circuits.
- **Voltage and current stress.** The magnified voltages and circulating currents are real. Rate capacitors for Q times the drive, and inductors for the circulating current.

<div className="takeaway"><span className="tiny-label">THE IDEA TO KEEP</span><p>At resonance the reactances cancel, so <em>resistance alone</em> sets the response. Q = Z₀/R tells you how sharp, how magnified and how long the ringing will be.</p></div>
