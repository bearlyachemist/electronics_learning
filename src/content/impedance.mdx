<div><span className="tiny-label">A PRACTICAL QUESTION</span><p className="opening-question">A 1 µF capacitor in series with 1 kΩ is driven by 1 V at 159 Hz. What current flows?</p></div>

<div>

It is tempting to add “1 kΩ” and “1 kΩ of capacitor” to get 2 kΩ, or to say the capacitor “has no resistance.” Both answers are wrong, and the right one, 0.707 mA, needs an idea that holds a magnitude and a phase together.

That idea is **impedance**: a complex number that plays the role of resistance for sinusoids. With it, every technique from the resistive chapters—Ohm’s law, series and parallel combination, dividers, Thévenin equivalents—carries over to circuits with capacitors and inductors, one frequency at a time.

<section id="capacitor">

<div className="section-heading"><span>01</span><h2>Capacitors in sinusoidal steady state</h2></div>

Apply v(t) = V_p cos ωt to an ideal capacitor. Its current is set by the rate of change of voltage:

<Equation tex="i=C\frac{dv}{dt}=-\omega CV_p\sin\omega t=\omega CV_p\cos(\omega t+90^\circ)"/>

Two facts appear at once. The current amplitude is ωC times the voltage amplitude, so the ratio of amplitudes is 1/(ωC). And the current **leads** the voltage by a quarter cycle: it peaks when the voltage is crossing zero most steeply, and is zero when the voltage is at its peak and momentarily not changing.

The amplitude ratio is the capacitor’s **reactance**, measured in ohms:

<Equation tex="X_C=\frac{1}{\omega C}=\frac{1}{2\pi fC}"/>

In phasor form, d/dt becomes jω, so I = jωC·V. The ratio V/I is a complex number containing both the magnitude ratio and the 90° shift. That ratio is the **impedance** of the capacitor:

<Equation tex="Z_C=\frac{\mathbf V}{\mathbf I}=\frac{1}{j\omega C}=-\frac{j}{\omega C}=X_C\angle-90^\circ" label="CAPACITOR IMPEDANCE"/>

The −j says the voltage lags the current by 90°, equivalently the current leads the voltage.

### Reading the limits

A 1 µF capacitor has 3.18 kΩ of reactance at 50 Hz and 0.159 Ω at 1 MHz. At DC (ω → 0) the reactance is infinite: a capacitor is an **open circuit** to steady voltages. At high frequency it approaches a **short circuit**. Every capacitor application follows from that sentence: blocking DC while passing signals, shorting noise to ground while leaving the supply voltage alone, or setting a frequency at which a circuit’s behavior changes.

A capacitor has reactance, not resistance. Over each cycle it absorbs energy for a quarter cycle and returns it for the next. An ideal capacitor dissipates no average power at any frequency—a point Chapter 21 makes quantitative.

</section>

<section id="inductor">

<div className="section-heading"><span>02</span><h2>Inductors, and how they differ</h2></div>

An inductor obeys v = L di/dt. Drive it with a sinusoidal current, or write the phasor relation directly:

<Equation tex="\mathbf V=j\omega L\,\mathbf I\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad Z_L=j\omega L=X_L\angle+90^\circ,\qquad X_L=\omega L" label="INDUCTOR IMPEDANCE"/>

Here the voltage **leads** the current by 90°. Inductive reactance rises with frequency: an inductor is a short to DC (ignoring winding resistance) and approaches an open circuit at high frequency. A 10 mH inductor has 3.14 Ω of reactance at 50 Hz and 6.28 kΩ at 100 kHz.

<aside className="margin-note"><strong>A mnemonic.</strong> “ELI the ICE man”: in an inductor (L) the voltage E comes before current I; in a capacitor (C) the current I comes before E. It is a memory aid, not a derivation; the derivation is the 90° rotation of jω.</aside>

### Inductors versus capacitors

Inductors and capacitors are mathematical mirror images: swap V and I, swap L and C, and every equation for one becomes an equation for the other. In practice they are far from symmetric. Capacitors are small, cheap, close to ideal over wide frequency ranges and available in enormous variety. Inductors at low frequencies are large and heavy, have significant winding resistance, couple magnetically to their surroundings, and saturate if their core is driven too hard (Chapter 10). For signal filtering below a few megahertz, designers therefore build filters from resistors and capacitors, and later from op-amps with capacitors. Inductors earn their place where energy must be stored and moved efficiently—switching power supplies—and at radio frequencies, where the required values become small and practical.

</section>

<section id="ohm">

<div className="section-heading"><span>03</span><h2>Ohm’s law generalized</h2></div>

With impedance defined, Ohm’s law holds for phasors:

<Equation tex="\mathbf V=\mathbf I\,Z,\qquad Z=R+jX" label="GENERALIZED OHM’S LAW"/>

The real part R is the **resistance** and the imaginary part X is the **reactance**. Positive X is inductive; negative X is capacitive. Magnitude and phase have direct physical meanings: |Z| is the ratio of voltage amplitude to current amplitude, and ∠Z is how far the voltage leads the current.

Kirchhoff’s laws hold at every instant for the real time-domain signals. They are linear, so they hold for the phasors too: around any loop the voltage phasors sum to zero, and at any node the current phasors sum to zero. Consequently, impedances combine exactly like resistances:

<Equation tex="Z_{\mathrm{series}}=Z_1+Z_2+\cdots,\qquad \frac{1}{Z_{\mathrm{parallel}}}=\frac{1}{Z_1}+\frac{1}{Z_2}+\cdots"/>

### Admittance

The reciprocal of impedance is **admittance**, Y = 1/Z = G + jB, measured in siemens. G is the conductance and B the susceptance. Parallel admittances add, which makes parallel circuits easier in admittance form.

One caution: G is not 1/R unless the impedance is purely resistive. For Z = 3 + 4j Ω:

<Equation tex="Y=\frac{1}{3+4j}=\frac{3-4j}{25}=0.12-0.16j\ \mathrm S"/>

Here G = 0.12 S, which corresponds to 8.33 Ω, not 3 Ω. A series 3 Ω + j4 Ω circuit behaves, at this one frequency, like 8.33 Ω in parallel with a reactance of 6.25 Ω.

<div className="worked-example">

<span className="tiny-label">WORKED EXAMPLE / THE OPENING QUESTION</span><h3>1 kΩ and 1 µF in series at 159.15 Hz</h3>

<p>At this frequency ω = 1000 rad/s and X_C = 1/(1000 × 1 µF) = 1 kΩ. The total impedance is Z = 1000 − 1000j Ω = <strong>1414 Ω ∠−45°</strong>.</p>

<p>The current is I = 1 V / (1414∠−45°) = <strong>0.707 mA ∠+45°</strong>, leading the source. The resistor voltage is 1 kΩ × I = 0.707 V ∠45°; the capacitor voltage is (−1000j)(I) = 0.707 V ∠−45°.</p>

<p>Check Kirchhoff: 0.707∠45° + 0.707∠−45° = (0.5 + 0.5j) + (0.5 − 0.5j) = 1 V. Two meters would read 0.707 V each, which add arithmetically to 1.414 V, not 1 V. The phasors add correctly; the magnitudes do not.</p>

<section id="combine">

<div className="section-heading"><span>04</span><h2>Combine R, L and C</h2></div>

### Series: the largest term dominates

For series R and C:

<Equation tex="Z=R-\frac{j}{\omega C},\qquad |Z|=\sqrt{R^2+\frac{1}{\omega^2C^2}},\qquad \angle Z=-\tan^{-1}\frac{1}{\omega RC}"/>

At low frequency the capacitor’s large reactance dominates; at high frequency the resistor does. The changeover is at ω = 1/RC, where the two are equal and the phase is −45°. A series RL behaves the other way around: resistive at low frequency, inductive at high frequency, with the changeover at ω = R/L. For 47 Ω and 10 mH at 1 kHz, Z = 47 + 62.8j = 78.5 Ω∠53.2°.

### Parallel: the smallest term dominates

For parallel R and C, add admittances and invert:

<Equation tex="Y=\frac1R+j\omega C\quad\Rightarrow\quad Z=\frac{R}{1+j\omega RC}"/>

At low frequency the capacitor’s admittance is negligible and Z → R. At high frequency the capacitor shorts the resistor and Z → 1/(jωC). For 10 kΩ ∥ 10 nF (changeover at 1.59 kHz):

### Series RLC: reactances cancel

Inductive and capacitive reactance have opposite signs, so in series they subtract:

<Equation tex="Z=R+j\left(\omega L-\frac{1}{\omega C}\right),\qquad \omega_0=\frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}}"/>

At ω₀ the reactances cancel and the impedance is purely R. For R = 100 Ω, L = 10 mH, C = 1 µF, f₀ = 1.59 kHz. At 500 Hz Z = 100 − 287j Ω (capacitive); at 1 kHz, 100 − 96j Ω; at 5 kHz, 100 + 282j Ω (inductive). In parallel the same pair produces an impedance *maximum* at ω₀. Resonance gets its own chapter (Chapter 24); here it is enough to see it as two reactances of opposite sign meeting.

### Series and parallel equivalents at one frequency

Any impedance R_s + jX_s can be represented at a single frequency as a parallel resistance and reactance. With Q = |X_s|/R_s:

<Equation tex="R_p=R_s\left(1+Q^2\right),\qquad X_p=X_s\left(1+\frac{1}{Q^2}\right)"/>

A 1 mH inductor with 10 Ω of winding resistance at 100 kHz has X_s = 628 Ω and Q = 62.8. In parallel form it is 628.5 Ω of reactance across **39.5 kΩ**. Small series loss looks like a large parallel resistance. This conversion is valid only at the frequency used; it is a description, not a different component.

<section id="manipulate">

<div className="section-heading"><span>06</span><h2>Read and manipulate impedance</h2></div>

### Magnitudes and phases of quotients

Many results take the form of a ratio. Its magnitude is the ratio of magnitudes and its phase is the difference of phases, so there is no need to rationalize first:

<Equation tex="\left|\frac{a+jb}{c+jd}\right|=\frac{\sqrt{a^2+b^2}}{\sqrt{c^2+d^2}},\qquad \angle\frac{a+jb}{c+jd}=\angle(a+jb)-\angle(c+jd)"/>

Rationalize—multiply by the conjugate of the denominator—when you need the real and imaginary parts separately, for example to find an equivalent series resistance.

### Impedance and frequency scaling

Every R, L and C enters a network’s behavior only through the combinations ωL, 1/(ωC) and R. Two consequences are powerful design tools:

- **Impedance scaling.** Multiply every R and L by k and divide every C by k. All impedances scale by k, so voltage ratios and the frequency response are unchanged. A 10 kΩ / 10 nF low-pass and a 100 kΩ / 1 nF low-pass have the same 1.59 kHz corner, but the second loads its source ten times less and is ten times more sensitive to stray capacitance and to its own load.
- **Frequency scaling.** Divide every L and C by k. Every reactance at frequency kf equals its old value at f, so the whole response moves up in frequency by k. Change 10 nF to 1 nF with the same 10 kΩ and the corner moves to 15.9 kHz.

### Back to reality

The impedances here are ideal. A real capacitor has series resistance and inductance, and above its self-resonant frequency it is an inductor (Chapter 9). A real inductor has winding resistance and winding capacitance, and above its self-resonance it is a capacitor. A resistor has a small inductance and capacitance of its own. Ideal models are accurate within a frequency range; part of using them well is knowing where that range ends.

<div className="takeaway"><span className="tiny-label">THE IDEA TO KEEP</span><p>Impedance is a <em>frequency-dependent resistance with a phase</em>. Capacitors fall as 1/f, inductors rise as f, and everything you know about resistor networks still works.</p></div>
