<section id="equation">

<div className="section-heading"><span>01</span><h2>Derive the step response</h2></div>

<RCSwitchFigure/>

Connect a voltage source $V_s$ through a resistor $R$ to a capacitor $C$ whose other terminal is the reference node. Let $v$ be the capacitor voltage. Current through $R$ is $(V_s - v)/R$; current entering $C$ is $C\,dv/dt$. There is no external output load, so those currents are equal:

<Equation tex="\frac{V_s-v}{R}=C\frac{dv}{dt},\qquad RC\frac{dv}{dt}+v=V_s"/>

After the source has switched to a constant value, define the difference from the eventual voltage as $u = v - V_s$. Its equation becomes:

<Equation tex="\frac{du}{dt}=-\frac{u}{RC}"/>

The rate of change is proportional to the difference still remaining. A large initial difference produces a large current and a fast change. As the difference shrinks, so does the current.

That sentence is worth pausing on, because it is the reason exponentials appear everywhere in nature: a cooling cup of coffee, a draining tank, radioactive decay and a charging capacitor all change at a rate proportional to how far they still have to go. Any process with that property follows the same curve.

Separate variables, integrate, and apply the initial voltage $V_0$:

<Equation tex="\frac{du}{u}=-\frac{dt}{RC}\quad\Rightarrow\quad u(t)=u(0)e^{-t/(RC)}"/>
<Equation tex="v(t)=V_s+(V_0-V_s)e^{-t/\tau},\qquad \tau=RC" label="01 / FINAL VALUE + DECAYING INITIAL ERROR"/>

This form covers charging, discharging, and a capacitor that starts above the final supply. It does not assume the initial voltage is zero. In words: **final value, plus (initial − final) times $e^{-t/\tau}$.** With that one sentence you can write down any first-order step response by inspection.

The current follows immediately:

<Equation tex="i(t)=\frac{V_s-V_0}{R}e^{-t/\tau}"/>

At the switching instant, capacitor voltage remains $V_0$, while current can change abruptly. As time tends to infinity, voltage tends to $V_s$ and current tends to zero.

<UniversalExponentialFigure/>

### Check the units

Ohms times farads give seconds: $(\text{V/A})(\text{A·s/V}) = \text{s}$. For $R = 10$ kΩ and $C = 10$ µF, $\tau =$ **0.1 s**, or 100 ms. A time constant is a property of this circuit configuration, not a generic property of the capacitor alone.

</section>

<section id="explore">

<div className="section-heading"><span>02</span><h2>Charge and discharge</h2></div>

The two modes below change the initial and final voltages. Charging starts at 0 V and approaches 5 V. Discharging starts at 5 V and connects the resistor to a 0 V ideal source, which is equivalent to connecting it to the reference node.

<RCLab client:load/>

<p className="caption">Exact first-order response for a linear RC network. A negative current in discharge means charge leaves the capacitor’s positive terminal.</p>

At one time constant, the remaining error is $e^{-1} \approx 36.8\%$ of the initial error. A 0-to-5 V charge therefore reaches about **3.161 V**. Discharging from 5 V reaches **1.839 V**. These are complementary examples of the same exponential, not separate rules.

<div className="table-wrap">

<table><thead><tr><th>Elapsed time</th><th>Initial error remaining</th><th>0 → 5 V charge</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>1τ</td><td>36.79%</td><td>3.161 V</td></tr><tr><td>3τ</td><td>4.979%</td><td>4.751 V</td></tr><tr><td>5τ</td><td>0.674%</td><td>4.966 V</td></tr></tbody></table>

</div>

An ideal exponential never reaches its limit exactly in finite time. "Fully charged" must mean sufficiently close for a stated purpose.

<SquareResponseFigure/>

A repeated input makes the time constant's meaning vivid. When the input changes slowly compared with $\tau$, the capacitor keeps up and the output copies the input with rounded edges. When the input changes quickly, the capacitor can only follow the average. That second regime is the basis of smoothing filters, and of the RC integrator in Chapter 8.

</section>

<section id="design">

<div className="section-heading"><span>03</span><h2>Timing and settling</h2></div>

If the remaining error must be no more than a fraction $\epsilon$ of the initial error:

<Equation tex="e^{-t/\tau}\leq\epsilon\quad\Rightarrow\quad t\geq-\tau\ln\epsilon"/>

One percent settling requires **4.605τ**, not exactly 5τ. The 10%–90% rise time is $\tau\ln 9 \approx$ **2.197τ**. Each further factor of ten in accuracy costs another $\ln 10 = 2.3$ time constants: 0.1% needs 6.9τ, and settling to 16-bit accuracy (15 ppm) needs about 11τ.

<div className="worked-example">

<span className="tiny-label">WORKED EXAMPLE / A THRESHOLD TIMER</span><h3>When does the node reach 3.3 V?</h3>
<p>A 10 kΩ resistor and 10 µF capacitor charge from 0 V toward 5 V. Set $3.3 = 5(1 - e^{-t/\tau})$, rearrange, and solve:</p>
<Equation tex="t=-100\ \mathrm{ms}\,\ln\!\left(1-\frac{3.3}{5}\right)=107.88\ \mathrm{ms}"/>
<p>This is an ideal threshold-crossing time. Resistor and capacitor tolerances, threshold variation, leakage, and supply changes all affect a real timer.</p>

</div>

### Design backward from a requirement

To settle within 1% in 20 ms, require $\tau \le 20\text{ ms}/4.605 =$ **4.343 ms**. With $R = 10$ kΩ, nominal $C$ must be at most 434.3 nF.

A 390 nF choice gives a nominal settling time of 17.96 ms. If $R$ can be 1% high and $C$ 10% high, maximum $\tau$ becomes $10\text{ k}\Omega \times 1.01 \times 390\text{ nF} \times 1.10 = 4.3329$ ms, giving about **19.95 ms**. That barely meets this particular tolerance budget; it does not leave room for other unmodeled effects.

<aside className="margin-note"><strong>Use the relevant error definition.</strong> Settling here is relative to the initial-to-final voltage difference. A fixed absolute error, a percentage of final output, and a comparator threshold are different specifications.</aside>

<DebounceFigure/>

<div className="worked-example">

<span className="tiny-label">WORKED DESIGN / A CLEAN BUTTON PRESS</span><h3>Debounce a switch that bounces for 5 ms</h3>
<p><strong>Press.</strong> The closed contact shorts the capacitor through a few ohms, so the node falls to 0 V in microseconds and stays low while the contact chatters: each brief opening lets the node climb only $5(1 - e^{-0.1/10}) \approx 50$ mV in a 100 µs gap.</p>
<p><strong>Release.</strong> Now the node charges through 10 kΩ toward 5 V with $\tau = 10\text{ k}\Omega \times 1\ \mu\text{F} = 10$ ms. A Schmitt input switches at about 3 V, reached at $t = -10\text{ ms} \times \ln(1 - 3/5) = 9.2$ ms. Any bounce in the first few milliseconds simply resets a node that was still far below threshold.</p>
<p><strong>Why a Schmitt input?</strong> An ordinary logic input would spend milliseconds near its single threshold on a slow ramp, where noise can make it toggle repeatedly, and some CMOS inputs draw extra supply current there. The Schmitt trigger's two thresholds turn a slow edge into one clean transition.</p>

</div>



<h3 id="book-notes">An RC delay depends on the decision threshold</h3>

An RC network becomes a timer when a comparator decides that its voltage has crossed a threshold. With a sudden supply step to $V_S$, initial capacitor voltage $V_0$ and a fixed threshold $V_H$:

<Equation tex={String.raw`t_H=RC\ln\frac{V_S-V_0}{V_S-V_H},\qquad V_0<V_H<V_S`}/>

A 100 kΩ resistor and 100 nF capacitor have $RC=10$ ms. Starting at zero and comparing with half the same supply gives $t_H=RC\ln2=6.93$ ms. A threshold at 60% of supply instead gives 9.16 ms. The time constant alone does not specify the delay.

The 50% threshold can be made ratiometric with a divider, so its fraction follows the final supply level. That still assumes a fast supply step. During a slow power ramp the capacitor already charges while the reference is rising, and the crossing can occur much earlier relative to “power good.” After a brief interruption, residual capacitor charge likewise shortens the next delay. A discharge path, comparator hysteresis and a defined valid-supply condition address three different problems. For precision timing, resistor/capacitor tolerances and input leakage also matter: 100 nA through 100 kΩ shifts the node by 10 mV.

<p className="reference-note">Further reading: Horowitz and Hill, §1.4.2, PDF pp. 54–55; Asadi, chapter 7, PDF pp. 197–198. This extension uses original explanations and design values.</p>

</section>

<section id="frequency">

<div className="section-heading"><span>04</span><h2>The same circuit in frequency</h2></div>

For a steady sinusoid, differentiating is equivalent to multiplying its phasor by $j\omega$. Here $j^2 = -1$ and $\omega = 2\pi f$; the complex number keeps track of both amplitude and phase. The capacitor relationship becomes $I = j\omega CV$, so its impedance is $1/(j\omega C)$. (Chapters 12 and 13 develop this fully; for now, treat it as a resistor-like quantity that shrinks as frequency rises.)

Use the impedance divider with output across $C$:

<Equation tex="H(j\omega)=\frac{V_{\mathrm{out}}}{V_{\mathrm{in}}}=\frac{1}{1+j\omega RC}"/>
<Equation tex="|H|=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+(\omega RC)^2}},\qquad \phi=-\tan^{-1}(\omega RC)"/>

At low frequency the capacitor voltage has time to follow the source. At high frequency, the required charging current creates a substantial drop across $R$, and the output becomes smaller and delayed.

The cutoff frequency is:

<Equation tex="f_c=\frac{1}{2\pi RC}"/>

At cutoff, gain is $1/\sqrt2$, about −3.01 dB, and phase is −45°. The time constant and cutoff describe the same energy-storage mechanism in different domains. For $\tau = 100$ ms, $f_c \approx$ **1.592 Hz**.

<RCFrequencyFigure/>

No abrupt boundary exists at cutoff. It is a useful reference point on a smooth response. A handy link between the two domains: the 10–90% rise time is $2.2\tau = 0.35/f_c$. A circuit with a 1 MHz cutoff cannot produce edges faster than about 350 ns.

</section>

<section id="loading">

<div className="section-heading"><span>05</span><h2>Find the resistance the capacitor sees</h2></div>

A capacitor connected to a linear resistive network responds according to that network’s Thévenin equivalent at the capacitor terminals. Find the open-circuit voltage and resistance with the capacitor removed, then restore it with its actual initial voltage:

<Equation tex="v(t)=V_{\mathrm{th}}+(V_0-V_{\mathrm{th}})e^{-t/(R_{\mathrm{th}}C)}"/>

Deactivate independent sources when finding resistance, keeping dependent sources active and using a test source if necessary. Include source resistance and any external load.

<LoadedRCFigure/>

<div className="worked-example">

<span className="tiny-label">WORKED EXAMPLE / A LOAD CHANGES TWO THINGS</span><h3>A 10 kΩ input attached to the node</h3>
<p>A 5 V source feeds the capacitor through 10 kΩ, and a 10 kΩ load also connects from the capacitor node to ground. The final voltage is now <strong>2.5 V</strong>, not 5 V.</p>
<p>The capacitor sees $10\text{ k}\Omega \parallel 10\text{ k}\Omega =$ <strong>5 kΩ</strong>. With 10 µF, its time constant is <strong>50 ms</strong>, not 100 ms.</p>
<p>The load both lowers the final voltage and speeds the exponential approach to that new voltage. A faster response does not imply the circuit is delivering the desired signal.</p>

</div>

### More than one capacitor

Several independent energy-storage states can create several time constants. Replacing every capacitor by an isolated "R times C" estimate does not generally solve a coupled network. The one-exponential result here relies on a first-order linear model.

At switching events, retain the capacitor voltage as an initial condition and analyze the new network. At high frequencies or very fast edges, capacitor ESR/ESL and wiring can introduce behavior beyond this model.

</section>

<section id="check">

<div className="section-heading"><span>06</span><h2>Check your understanding</h2></div>

<details>
<summary>1. A capacitor at 8 V is connected through a resistor to a 2 V source. Write its voltage as a function of time.</summary>
<div>

Final value plus (initial − final) times the exponential: $v(t) = 2 + 6e^{-t/\tau}$ V. It decays from 8 V toward 2 V; nothing about the formula requires starting at zero.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>2. Doubling $R$ doubles $\tau$. What happens to the initial current and to the total charge delivered?</summary>
<div>

The initial current halves. The total charge is unchanged, $C(V_s - V_0)$: it is delivered at half the rate over twice the time.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>3. How long to settle to 0.1% of the initial error?</summary>
<div>

$t = \tau\ln(1000) = 6.91\tau$.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>4. An RC low-pass has $f_c$ = 1 kHz. Roughly what is its 10–90% rise time?</summary>
<div>

$0.35/f_c = 350$ µs (from $\tau = 1/(2\pi f_c) = 159$ µs and $2.2\tau$).

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>5. A probe with 10 MΩ input resistance and 15 pF is connected to a node driven through 100 kΩ. What time constant does the probe add?</summary>
<div>

The node's Thévenin resistance is $100\text{ k}\Omega \parallel 10\text{ M}\Omega \approx 99$ kΩ, so $\tau \approx 99\text{ k}\Omega \times 15\text{ pF} = 1.5$ µs. Edges faster than a few microseconds will be visibly slowed, by the measurement itself.

</div>
</details>
