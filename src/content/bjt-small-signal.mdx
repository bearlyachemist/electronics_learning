<div><span className="tiny-label">A PRACTICAL QUESTION</span><p className="opening-question">An emitter follower at 1 mA has its base driven from 0 Ω. Why is its output impedance about 26 Ω and not zero?</p></div>

<div>

Chapter 3 introduced small-signal resistance: the slope of a nonlinear curve at an operating point, valid for changes small enough that the curve looks straight. The diode’s small-signal resistance was $nV_T/I$.

The transistor gets the same treatment. Around its bias point, every change of base–emitter voltage produces a proportional change of collector current, with a slope set only by the bias current. That slope, and three resistances derived from it, form the **small-signal model**: a linear circuit you can analyze with Ohm’s law, superposition and Thévenin equivalents. It answers the opening question and predicts the gain of every amplifier in the following chapters.

</div>

</div>

<section id="separate">

<div className="section-heading"><span>01</span><h2>Separate bias from signal</h2></div>

Every voltage and current in an amplifier is a sum of a steady part and a small changing part: $v_{BE}$ = $V_{BE}$ + $v_{be}$, $i_C$ = $I_C$ + $i_c$ (capitals for bias, lowercase for signal). Chapter 29 found the bias with DC analysis. For the signal, build an **incremental circuit**:

1. **DC supplies become AC ground.** A fixed voltage has no change, so for signals it is equivalent to ground.
2. **Large capacitors become shorts** at signal frequencies, and blocking/coupling effects are handled separately (Chapter 22).
3. **Resistors stay resistors.**
4. **The transistor is replaced by its small-signal model**, whose element values are computed from the bias current.

This separation is legitimate because the small-signal model is linear: superposition lets us add the signal response to the bias.

</section>

<section id="gm">

<div className="section-heading"><span>02</span><h2>Derive $g_m$ and $r_e$</h2></div>

From Chapter 26, the collector current depends exponentially on $V_{BE}$: $I_C$ = $I_S$ $e^{V_{BE}/V_T}$. Its slope is the **transconductance**:

<Equation tex="g_m=\frac{dI_C}{dV_{BE}}=\frac{I_S}{V_T}e^{V_{BE}/V_T}=\frac{I_C}{V_T}" label="TRANSCONDUCTANCE"/>

In this ideal forward-active BJT model, transistors have the same $g_m$ at the same collector current and temperature, regardless of area or current gain. At 1 mA and 300 K, $g_m$ = 1 mA/25.85 mV = **38.7 mS**: each millivolt of base–emitter change moves the collector current by 38.7 µA.

The inverse, seen from the emitter, is the **intrinsic emitter resistance**:

<Equation tex="r_e=\frac{1}{g_m}\approx\frac{V_T}{I_C}\approx\frac{25\ \Omega}{I_C/\mathrm{mA}}"/>

At 1 mA, $r_e$ = 25.9 Ω; at 10 µA, 2.6 kΩ; at 100 mA, 0.26 Ω. It behaves as a small resistor inside the emitter lead, in series with anything outside. It is the answer to the opening question: even with a perfect source, the follower’s output impedance cannot be lower than $r_e$.

### Seen from the base

A change $i_b$ in base current accompanies a change $\beta$ $i_b$ in collector current. The base–emitter voltage change needed is $v_{be}$ = $i_c$/$g_m$, so the base sees a resistance

<Equation tex="r_\pi=\frac{v_{be}}{i_b}=\frac{\beta}{g_m}=\beta r_e"/>

At 1 mA with $\beta$ = 100, $r_π$ = 2.59 kΩ. (The exact emitter-resistance convention is discussed below; the grounded-emitter hybrid-π input resistance is $r_π$.)

### Seen from the collector

The Early effect (Chapter 26) makes the collector current rise slightly with $V_{CE}$. The corresponding small-signal **output resistance** is

<Equation tex="r_o=\frac{V_A}{I_C}"/>

With $V_A$ = 100 V at 1 mA, $r_o$ = 100 kΩ. Often large enough to ignore, it limits the gain of high-gain stages and the quality of current sources.

</section>

<section id="hybrid">

<div className="section-heading"><span>03</span><h2>The hybrid-π model</h2></div>

<HybridPiFigure/>

The three elements assemble into the **hybrid-π model**: $r_π$ between base and emitter, a voltage-controlled current source $g_m$ $v_{be}$ from collector to emitter, and $r_o$ in parallel with it. Some designers prefer the equivalent **T model**: $r_e$ in the emitter lead and a current source $\alpha i_e$ (or $\beta i_b$) in the collector. They give identical results; the T model is faster when there is an emitter resistor, the hybrid-π when the emitter is grounded.

Two cautions. The model is valid only for small signals around the bias point, and only for the bias current used to compute it. And it describes changes only: a small-signal model never includes $V_{BE}$ = 0.65 V or the supply voltages.

</section>

<section id="explore">

<div className="section-heading"><span>04</span><h2>Explore the tangent</h2></div>

<p className="caption">Figure 1. The exponential collector characteristic and its tangent at the bias point; below, the current change produced by a sinusoidal base–emitter change, compared with the linear prediction.</p>

The tangent’s slope is $g_m$. Its intercept with the voltage axis is always $V_T$ below the bias point—an exponential’s tangent always crosses zero one “scale length” back—so the steepness of the tangent is proportional to the bias current.

</section>

<section id="looking">

<div className="section-heading"><span>05</span><h2>Looking into each terminal</h2></div>

Three results cover most transistor circuits. They are the first-model impedances of Chapter 28 with $r_e$ added.

<div className="table-wrap">

<table><thead><tr><th>Looking into</th><th>Conditions</th><th>Small-signal impedance</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>Base</td><td>Resistance $R_E$ (to AC ground) in the emitter</td><td>($\beta$ + 1)($r_e$ + $R_E$)</td></tr><tr><td>Emitter</td><td>Resistance $R_B$ (to AC ground) in the base</td><td>$r_e$ + $R_B$/($\beta$ + 1)</td></tr><tr><td>Collector</td><td>Emitter resistance $R_E$, base stiffly held</td><td>≈ $r_o$(1 + $g_m$($R_E$ ∥ $r_π$)), very large</td></tr></tbody></table>

</div>

The first two are the same rule: resistance in the emitter circuit is multiplied by $\beta$ + 1 when seen from the base, and resistance in the base circuit is divided by $\beta$ + 1 when seen from the emitter, with $r_e$ counted as part of the emitter circuit. The third says the collector is a good current source, especially with emitter degeneration (Chapter 31).

<div className="worked-example">

<span className="tiny-label">WORKED EXAMPLE / A FOLLOWER, PROPERLY</span><h3>1 mA, $R_E$ = 1 kΩ, source 10 kΩ, $\beta$ = 100</h3>

<p>$r_e$ = 25.9 Ω. Looking into the base: 101 × (25.9 + 1000) = <strong>103.6 kΩ</strong>.</p>

<p>Looking into the emitter: 25.9 + 10 000/101 = <strong>124.9 Ω</strong>; in parallel with the 1 kΩ emitter resistor, the stage’s output impedance is 111 Ω. The first model’s 99 Ω missed $r_e$.</p>

<p>Unloaded voltage gain $R_E$/($R_E$ + $r_e$) = 1000/1025.9 = <strong>0.975</strong>, slightly less than 1 because $r_e$ and $R_E$ form a divider for the emitter signal.</p>

</div>

### A procedure

1. Find the bias current (Chapter 29).
2. Compute $r_e$ = $V_T$/$I_C$, and $r_π$, $r_o$ if needed.
3. Draw the incremental circuit: supplies to ground, capacitors shorted, transistor replaced by its model.
4. Use the three impedance rules to collapse the circuit, then solve.

</section>

<section id="limits">

<div className="section-heading"><span>06</span><h2>How small is small?</h2></div>

The model replaces $e^{v/V_T}$ by 1 + v/$V_T$. The next term of the series, (v/$V_T$)²/2, measures the error. Relative to the linear term it is $v/(2V_T)$:

- 1 mV of base–emitter signal: error about **2%**.
- 10 mV: the positive current peak is **22%** larger and the negative peak **17%** smaller than predicted.

So “small” means a base–emitter signal of a few millivolts or less. That sounds restrictive, but in circuits with an emitter resistor the transistor’s $v_{be}$ is only a small fraction of the input: the rest appears across $R_E$. Emitter degeneration is what makes larger signals linear (Chapter 32).

The model also has a frequency limit. Junction and diffusion capacitances (Chapter 26) appear between base and emitter ($C_π$) and between base and collector ($C_μ$). Their importance depends on the surrounding impedances and stage gain; they can dominate well below the transistor’s transition frequency, and $C_μ$ in particular is magnified in amplifiers by the Miller effect, a later topic.

Finally, keep $h_{fe}$ (the small-signal $\beta$, a slope) and $h_{FE}$ (the DC ratio) distinct on datasheets, though for most designs they are interchangeable within their large tolerances.

### Distinguish the two definitions of intrinsic emitter resistance

If $r_e$ is defined exactly as $V_T/I_E$, then $r_e=\alpha/g_m$ and $r_π=(\beta+1)r_e$. If it is approximated as $1/g_m=V_T/I_C$, then $r_π=\beta r_e$. Both conventions appear in texts. They agree to about $1/\beta$, but mixing their exact formulas creates an unnecessary inconsistency.

For a grounded emitter, the exact hybrid-π input resistance is $r_π$; there is no extra emitter resistor to add. With external emitter resistance R, the exact input is $r_π+(\beta+1)R$. The convenient approximation $(\beta+1)(R+1/g_m)$ differs only in its intrinsic term.

<div className="worked-example">

<span className="tiny-label">WORKED EXAMPLE / CHOOSE THE SIGNAL SCALE</span><h3>1 mA bias and a 1 mV base–emitter sine</h3>

<p>At 300 K, $g_m=38.7$ mS and the linear peak current change is 38.7 µA. The exact positive change is $1\ \mathrm{mA}(e^{1/25.85}-1)=39.4$ µA; the negative magnitude is $1\ \mathrm{mA}(1-e^{-1/25.85})=37.9$ µA. The roughly 2% peak errors are small but observable.</p>

<p>Multiply the bias current by ten and all three current changes grow by ten. Their percentage errors stay the same because they depend on $v_{be}/V_T$, not on the bias current. An emitter resistor reduces the junction’s signal fraction and therefore improves linearity.</p>

</div>

<div className="takeaway"><span className="tiny-label">THE IDEA TO KEEP</span><p>At bias current $I_C$, the transistor’s small-signal behavior is <em>$g_m$ = $I_C$/$V_T$</em>, regardless of type or size. $r_e$, $r_π$ and $r_o$ follow from it, and three impedance rules do the rest.</p></div>

</section>

<section id="check">

<div className="section-heading"><span>07</span><h2>Check your understanding</h2></div>

<details>
<summary>1. Where do the small-signal element values come from?</summary>
<div>

First solve the DC operating point, then differentiate the device law there. A model at a different bias current describes a different tangent.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>2. What replaces a fixed DC voltage source in an incremental circuit?</summary>
<div>

A short circuit, usually to AC ground. Independent DC current sources are opened; dependent sources remain active.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>3. Is $r_e$ a physical resistor that dissipates I²$r_e$ bias power?</summary>
<div>

No. It represents an incremental slope. Apply the device’s actual voltage and current to calculate power, not a fictitious DC resistor drop.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>4. Why does 1 mV give similar percentage curvature at 0.1 mA and 1 mA?</summary>
<div>

The exponential expansion depends on $v_{be}/V_T$. Bias scales the absolute current response, not that normalized error.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>5. Can capacitance be ignored whenever frequency is below $f_T$?</summary>
<div>

No. Source resistance, current gain and Miller multiplication can create poles far below the device transition frequency. Compute the circuit poles.

</div>
</details>
