<div className="opening-grid">

<div><span className="tiny-label">A PRACTICAL QUESTION</span><p className="opening-question">A sensor produces 5 mV, but its leads also pick up a volt of 50 Hz hum—identically on both wires. How do you amplify the 5 mV and not the volt?</p></div>

<div>

Every amplifier so far measured its input against ground. When the signal of interest is the *difference* between two wires, and anything common to both is interference, a single-ended amplifier amplifies the interference too.

The **differential amplifier** responds to the difference between two inputs and, ideally, not at all to their common level. It is the input stage of every op-amp, the core of instrumentation and comparators, and the reason a 5 mV signal can be recovered from a volt of common-mode noise. It is also a beautiful application of the Ebers–Moll law: its large-signal behavior can be solved exactly.

</div>

</div>

<section id="signals">

<div className="section-heading"><span>01</span><h2>Differential and common-mode signals</h2></div>

Any pair of input voltages $v_1$ and $v_2$ can be rewritten as a **differential** part and a **common-mode** part:

<Equation tex="v_d=v_1-v_2,\qquad v_{cm}=\frac{v_1+v_2}{2},\qquad v_1=v_{cm}+\frac{v_d}{2},\ \ v_2=v_{cm}-\frac{v_d}{2}"/>

A linear differential amplifier has a gain for each part:

<Equation tex="v_{\mathrm{out}}=A_d\,v_d+A_{cm}\,v_{cm},\qquad \mathrm{CMRR}=\left|\frac{A_d}{A_{cm}}\right|\quad(\text{often in dB: }20\log_{10}\mathrm{CMRR})"/>

The **common-mode rejection ratio** measures how well the amplifier ignores what both inputs share. With 5 mV differential and 1 V common mode, an amplifier with CMRR = 100 (40 dB) produces equal output contributions from each—useless. With CMRR = 10⁵ (100 dB), the interference is 500 times smaller than the signal at the output.

</section>

<section id="largesignal">

<div className="section-heading"><span>02</span><h2>The large-signal tanh law</h2></div>

The **long-tailed pair** is two matched transistors with their emitters joined and fed from a common “tail” current $I_T$ (a resistor to a negative supply, or a current source). Each collector has a load resistor $R_C$. The inputs go to the two bases.

<DiffPairFigure/>

The emitters share one node, so the difference in base voltages appears entirely as a difference in base–emitter voltages: $V_{BE1}$ − $V_{BE2}$ = $v_d$. Applying Ebers–Moll to each transistor:

<Equation tex="\frac{I_{C1}}{I_{C2}}=\frac{I_Se^{V_{BE1}/V_T}}{I_Se^{V_{BE2}/V_T}}=e^{v_d/V_T},\qquad I_{C1}+I_{C2}=I_T"/>

The second equation is KCL at the shared emitter node, taking $\alpha \approx 1$. Solve the two equations together.

<div className="derivation">

<span className="tiny-label">DERIVATION / THE TANH STEERING LAW</span>

1. **Eliminate $I_{C2}$.** From the ratio, $I_{C2} = I_{C1}e^{-v_d/V_T}$. Substitute into the sum:
<Equation tex="I_{C1}\left(1+e^{-v_d/V_T}\right)=I_T\quad\Longrightarrow\quad I_{C1}=\frac{I_T}{1+e^{-v_d/V_T}}"/>
   By the same steps with the roles exchanged, $I_{C2} = I_T/(1+e^{+v_d/V_T})$.

2. **Form the difference.** Put both over a common form by multiplying the top and bottom of $I_{C1}$ by $e^{v_d/2V_T}$ and of $I_{C2}$ by $e^{-v_d/2V_T}$. Then both denominators equal $e^{v_d/2V_T} + e^{-v_d/2V_T}$:
<Equation tex="I_{C1}-I_{C2}=I_T\,\frac{e^{v_d/2V_T}-e^{-v_d/2V_T}}{e^{v_d/2V_T}+e^{-v_d/2V_T}}=I_T\tanh\frac{v_d}{2V_T}" label="LONG-TAILED PAIR"/>

3. **Small-signal check.** Near $v_d = 0$, $\tanh u \approx u$, so $I_{C1} - I_{C2} \approx I_Tv_d/2V_T$. Each side changes by half of that, $\pm(I_T/2)v_d/(2V_T) = g_m\,v_d/2$ with $g_m = (I_T/2)/V_T$: each transistor sees half the differential input.

</div>

The tail current is **steered** from one side to the other by the input difference. The common-mode voltage does not appear at all: raising both bases together raises the shared emitter node with them, and the tail current, split equally, does not change.

<div className="table-wrap">

<table><thead><tr><th>$v_d$</th><th>$I_{C1}$ / $I_T$</th><th>Comment</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>0</td><td>0.500</td><td>Balanced</td></tr><tr><td>10 mV</td><td>0.596</td><td>Nearly linear</td></tr><tr><td>26 mV ($V_T$)</td><td>0.732</td><td>Visibly compressing</td></tr><tr><td>50 mV</td><td>0.874</td><td>Strongly nonlinear</td></tr><tr><td>100 mV</td><td>0.980</td><td>Fully switched</td></tr></tbody></table>

</div>

The slope has fallen by 10% from its center value at only ±17 mV. A plain pair is linear only for a few tens of millivolts; beyond ±100 mV it is a switch, which is what a comparator wants. **Emitter degeneration** (a resistor $R_E$ in each emitter) widens the linear range: $v_d$ = $V_T$ ln($I_{C1}$/$I_{C2}$) + ($I_{C1}$ − $I_{C2}$)$R_E$. With $R_E$ = 500 Ω at 1 mA tail, 100 mV of input steers only 59% of the current to one side.

### A quantitative linear range

The current difference is $I_T\tanh(v_d/(2V_T))$. For $x=v_d/(2V_T)$, the expansion $\tanh x=x-x^3/3+\cdots$ gives a fractional gain compression of about $x^2/3$.

<Equation tex="\frac{\text{departure from the linear current difference}}{\text{linear prediction}}\approx\frac{v_d^2}{12V_T^2}"/>

A 1% departure corresponds to $|v_d|\approx\sqrt{0.12}V_T=8.95$ mV at 300 K. This is a peak transfer-curve error, not a 1% harmonic-distortion specification. With emitter resistors, the exact relationship becomes $v_d=V_T\ln(I_1/I_2)+(I_1-I_2)R_E$: degeneration spends some input voltage on resistors instead of junction exponentials.

</section>

<section id="smallsignal">

<div className="section-heading"><span>03</span><h2>Small-signal gains and CMRR</h2></div>

### Differential gain: the half circuit

For a small differential input, $v_1$ rises by $v_d$/2 and $v_2$ falls by $v_d$/2. The shared emitter node does not move: it is a virtual AC ground for differential signals.

<div className="derivation">

<span className="tiny-label">DERIVATION / WHY THE TAIL NODE IS A DIFFERENTIAL GROUND</span>

1. **KCL at the tail node** for small signals. Each half delivers an emitter current that depends on its own base-to-tail voltage through the half's emitter impedance $r_e + R_E$. With the tail node at $v_t$:
<Equation tex="\frac{v_d/2-v_t}{r_e+R_E}+\frac{-v_d/2-v_t}{r_e+R_E}=\frac{v_t}{R_T}"/>

2. **The $v_d$ terms cancel** on the left, leaving $-2v_t/(r_e+R_E) = v_t/R_T$. The only solution is $v_t = 0$, for any value of $R_T$.

3. **Consequence.** With the tail node fixed, each half is an ordinary common-emitter stage driven by $\pm v_d/2$, and Chapter 32's result applies directly. The emitter current of side 1 is $(v_d/2)/(r_e+R_E)$, and side 2's is equal and opposite.

</div>

Each half is then a common-emitter stage with input $v_d$/2:

<Equation tex="i_{c1}=-i_{c2}=\frac{v_d/2}{r_e+R_E},\qquad A_d=\frac{v_{c2}}{v_d}=\frac{R_C}{2(r_e+R_E)}\ \ (\text{single-ended}),\qquad A_{d,\mathrm{diff}}=\frac{R_C}{r_e+R_E}"/>

Taking the output from one collector gives half the gain of taking it between the two collectors. With a 1 mA tail, each side runs at 0.5 mA, $r_e$ = 51.7 Ω, and with $R_C$ = 10 kΩ the single-ended gain is 10 000/(2 × 51.7) = **96.7**.

### Common-mode gain: the other half circuit

For a common-mode input both bases rise together and both collector currents change equally. Split the tail resistance $R_T$ into two resistors $2R_T$ in parallel, one for each half. This changes nothing: the two half-tails together carry the same current at the same voltage. By symmetry the wire joining the two emitters then carries no current and can be cut. Each half is now a common-emitter stage with $2R_T$ of degeneration (plus its own $R_E$), so Chapter 32's gain formula gives $A_{cm}$ directly. Divide $A_d$ by it to get the CMRR:

<Equation tex="A_{cm}=-\frac{R_C}{2R_T+r_e+R_E}\approx-\frac{R_C}{2R_T},\qquad \mathrm{CMRR}=\left|\frac{A_d}{A_{cm}}\right|\approx\frac{R_T}{r_e+R_E}"/>

A 14.3 kΩ tail from −15 V (giving 1 mA) yields $A_{\mathrm{cm}}$ = −10 000/28 650 = −0.349 and CMRR = 277, or **49 dB**. Replacing the tail resistor with a transistor current source whose output resistance is several megohms raises CMRR to about 10⁵, nearly **100 dB**. That is the main reason the tail is almost always a current source.

<div className="worked-example">

<span className="tiny-label">WORKED EXAMPLE / THE OPENING QUESTION</span><h3>5 mV of signal under 1 V of hum</h3>

<p>With the 14.3 kΩ tail: the signal gives 96.7 × 5 mV = 484 mV at the output; the hum gives 0.349 × 1 V = 349 mV. The output is barely recognizable.</p>

<p>With a 5 MΩ current-source tail: the hum contributes 10 000/10⁷ × 1 V ≈ 1 mV, nearly 500 times smaller than the signal. The circuit now does what was asked—provided the transistors and resistors match well enough, a limit discussed in section 06.</p>

</div>

<section id="activeload">

<div className="section-heading"><span>05</span><h2>Current-mirror active load</h2></div>

Taking the output from one collector wastes half the signal: the other collector’s current change is simply dumped into its resistor. And high gain needs a large $R_C$, which needs a large supply voltage to carry the bias current.

Replace both collector resistors with a PNP current mirror. $Q_3$ (diode-connected) senses $Q_1$’s collector current; $Q_4$ copies it into the output node, where it meets $Q_2$’s collector current.

<ActiveLoadFigure/>

For a differential input, $Q_1$’s current rises by i and $Q_2$’s falls by i. The mirror copies $Q_1$’s increase to the output node, so the output node receives +i from $Q_4$ while $Q_2$ draws i less: the net current into the output is 2i. Both halves now contribute.

<div className="derivation">

<span className="tiny-label">DERIVATION / GAIN OF THE MIRROR-LOADED PAIR</span>

1. **Short-circuit output current.** Hold the output node at a fixed voltage, so no current flows in any $r_o$. From the half-circuit, $i_{c1} = +i$ and $i_{c2} = -i$, with $i = (v_d/2)/(r_e + R_E)$.

2. **The mirror.** $Q_3$'s diode carries $i_{c1}$; $Q_4$ copies it, so $Q_4$ pushes $+i$ into the output node.

3. **KCL at the output node.** Current in from $Q_4$ is $+i$; current out through $Q_2$'s collector is $i_{c2} = -i$, which is the same as $+i$ in. The net short-circuit current into the node is
<Equation tex="i_{sc}=i-(-i)=2i=\frac{v_d}{r_e+R_E}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad G_m=\frac{1}{r_e+R_E}"/>

4. **Output resistance.** With the inputs at rest, the node connects to signal ground through $Q_2$'s collector ($r_{o2}$, raised a little by degeneration) and $Q_4$'s collector ($r_{o4}$). These are in parallel. Each transistor carries $I_T/2$, so each $r_o = V_A/(I_T/2)$, and the parallel pair is $V_A/I_T$.

5. **Open-circuit gain** is the short-circuit current times the output resistance (a Norton-to-Thévenin step):
<Equation tex="A_d=G_m\,(r_{o2}\parallel r_{o4})=\frac{1}{r_e+R_E}\cdot\frac{V_A}{I_T}" label="ACTIVE-LOAD GAIN"/>
   With $R_E = 0$ and $r_e = V_T/(I_T/2)$, the currents cancel and $A_d = V_A/(2V_T)$.

</div>

The gain depends only on the Early voltage. For $V_A$ = 100 V it is 100/(2 × 0.02585) ≈ **1930** from one stage—with a single-ended output and no large resistors. With 100 Ω of degeneration it falls to about 660 but becomes more linear. This is the input stage of most bipolar op-amps; its high output impedance also means it must drive a high-impedance next stage, usually a follower or another gain stage.

</section>

<section id="limits">

<div className="section-heading"><span>06</span><h2>Offsets, bias currents and limits</h2></div>

**Input offset voltage.** If the transistors’ $V_{BE}$ values differ by 1 mV at equal currents, the pair behaves as if 1 mV were applied between its inputs: the output is balanced only when $v_d$ = −1 mV. A 5% $I_S$ mismatch gives $V_T$ ln 1.05 ≈ 1.3 mV; collector-resistor mismatch adds more. Discrete pairs offset by millivolts; monolithic matched pairs by a fraction of a millivolt. Offset also drifts, roughly by ($V_{OS}$/T), about 3.3 µV/°C for each millivolt of offset.

**Input bias and offset currents.** Each base draws $I_T$/(2β): 5 µA for a 1 mA tail and $\beta$ = 100. That current flows through whatever source resistance drives each input, producing an extra offset voltage. Matching the source resistances at the two inputs cancels the bias current’s effect, leaving only the *difference* between base currents, the input offset current.

**Mismatch limits CMRR.** If the two halves are not identical, a common-mode change produces unequal collector currents, which look like a differential signal. In practice, mismatch rather than the tail impedance often sets CMRR, which is why precision differential amplifiers use matched pairs or laser-trimmed resistors.

**Input ranges.** The common-mode input must stay within the range where both transistors and the tail source remain active: above the tail’s compliance and low enough that the collectors stay above the bases. The differential input must not exceed the emitter–base breakdown voltage (about 6 V) of the reverse-biased side; input protection diodes are common.

**Uses.** Besides op-amp inputs, the pair is a comparator (driven well past ±100 mV), a voltage-controlled amplifier (the gain is proportional to the tail current), and the core of analog multipliers and mixers.

<div className="worked-example">

<span className="tiny-label">WORKED EXAMPLE / INPUT RESISTANCE AND OFFSET</span><h3>A 1 mA tail with β = 100</h3>

<p>At balance, each transistor has 0.5 mA collector current, $g_m=19.34$ mS and $r_π=5.17$ kΩ. A floating differential source sees approximately $2r_π=10.34$ kΩ between the bases. With 100 Ω in each emitter, this rises to $2[r_π+(β+1)R_E]=30.54$ kΩ.</p>

<p>Each input also draws about 5 µA of DC bias current. A 10 kΩ difference between source resistances creates about 50 mV of differential input error—far larger than the 9 mV linear range without degeneration. Equal source resistances cancel the effect of equal bias currents, while input offset current and transistor mismatch remain.</p>

<p>Check the DC input range separately. With 10 kΩ collector resistors and 0.5 mA per side on +15 V, both collectors sit near 10 V. Forward active operation requires bases below those collector voltages. At the other end, the tail source must retain its own headroom above −15 V. Small-signal CMRR does not promise rejection outside that range.</p>

</div>



<h3 id="book-notes">The lowest-noise collector current depends on source resistance</h3>

More collector current raises transconductance and lowers the voltage-equivalent collector shot noise. It also raises base shot noise, which becomes a voltage when it flows through source resistance. These effects compete even before power and headroom are considered.

For one input transistor, retain only collector and base shot noise, use $g_m=I_C/U_T$ and a constant beta, and assume independent noise sources. Collector shot noise $2qI_C$ (A²/Hz) referred to the input divides by $g_m^2$, giving $2qI_C\,U_T^2/I_C^2 = 2qU_T^2/I_C$. Base shot noise $2qI_B = 2qI_C/\beta$ flows through $R_S$ and becomes a voltage $2qI_CR_S^2/\beta$. Uncorrelated noise powers add. To find the minimum, set the derivative with respect to $I_C$ to zero: $-2qU_T^2/I_C^2 + 2qR_S^2/\beta = 0$. With source resistance $R_S$:

<Equation tex={String.raw`e_{n,\mathrm{device}}^2\approx\frac{2qU_T^2}{I_C}+\frac{2qI_C R_S^2}{\beta},\qquad I_{C,\mathrm{opt}}=\frac{U_T\sqrt\beta}{R_S}`}/>

At $U_T=25.85$ mV and beta = 100, the simplified optimum is **25.85 µA** for 10 kΩ, **2.585 mA** for 100 Ω and **2.585 µA** for 100 kΩ. Two identical input devices with equal source resistances add two copies of these terms to differential input noise; the optimum current per device is unchanged in this symmetrical model.

Source-resistor thermal noise remains $4k_BT R_S$ per side and is not reduced by selecting a different collector current. Base spreading resistance, flicker noise, finite-frequency beta, bias-current accuracy and unequal source impedances can all move the practical optimum. This is a method for understanding the trade, not a universal current recommendation. In particular, a high-resistance sensor can prefer a FET input even if a BJT has a lower voltage-noise specification in isolation.

<p className="reference-note">Further reading: Huijsing, §4.2, PDF pp. 94–96; Horowitz and Hill, chapter 8 input-noise analysis. This extension uses original explanations and design values.</p>

<div className="takeaway"><span className="tiny-label">THE IDEA TO KEEP</span><p>A long-tailed pair <em>steers a fixed tail current</em>: the difference of the inputs decides how it divides, and their common level does not. A current-source tail gives high CMRR; a mirror load gives high gain.</p></div>

<section id="check">

<div className="section-heading"><span>07</span><h2>Check your understanding</h2></div>

<details>
<summary>1. What happens to the sum of the two currents when differential input increases?</summary>
<div>

In the ideal fixed-tail model it stays at the tail current. The input steers the split rather than increasing the sum.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>2. Why is single-ended resistive gain half the differential-output gain?</summary>
<div>

Each side responds to half the differential input, and the two collector voltage changes have opposite signs. Subtracting the outputs adds their magnitudes.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>3. Does emitter degeneration increase gain?</summary>
<div>

It lowers transconductance and gain, while increasing input resistance and the useful linear input range.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>4. Can an ideal tail current source remove input mismatch?</summary>
<div>

It removes the tail’s common-mode current variation in the ideal model, but cannot eliminate unequal devices, unequal loads or unequal source-resistance drops.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>5. Why does a current-mirror load recover both signal currents at one output?</summary>
<div>

It copies the left current change into the right output node, where it adds to the opposite change in the right transistor’s current.

</div>
</details>
