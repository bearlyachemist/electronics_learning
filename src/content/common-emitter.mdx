<div className="opening-grid">

<div><span className="tiny-label">A PRACTICAL QUESTION</span><p className="opening-question">A microphone produces 10 mV. You need about 100 mV for the next stage. Can one transistor and a few resistors do it, and do it predictably?</p></div>

<div>

The emitter follower copies a voltage. Move the output to the collector and put a resistor there, and the stage converts the follower’s emitter current into a voltage across that resistor. If the collector resistor is larger than the emitter resistor, the output voltage change is larger than the input change: **voltage gain**.

This is the common-emitter amplifier, so called because for signals the emitter is common to input and output. It is the basic voltage-gain stage of discrete electronics and the conceptual core of the op-amp’s first stages.

</div>

</div>

</div>

<section id="gain">

<div className="section-heading"><span>01</span><h2>Gain from a ratio of resistors</h2></div>

Bias the stage as in Chapter 29: a stiff divider sets the base, $R_E$ sets the current, $R_C$ sets the collector voltage. Couple the signal into the base. Then follow a small change:

1. The emitter follows the base: $\Delta v_E$ = $\Delta v_B$.
2. The emitter current changes by $\Delta i_E$ = $\Delta v_B$/$R_E$.
3. The collector current changes by nearly the same amount: $\Delta i_C$ ≈ $\Delta i_E$.
4. The collector voltage changes by the drop across $R_C$, in the opposite direction: $\Delta v_C$ = −$\Delta i_C$ $R_C$.

<Equation tex="G=\frac{\Delta v_C}{\Delta v_B}=-\frac{R_C}{R_E}" label="FIRST-MODEL GAIN"/>

The minus sign means the output is **inverted**: when the input rises, the collector falls. With $R_C$ = 7.5 kΩ and $R_E$ = 1 kΩ the gain is −7.5. $\beta$ does not appear. The gain is a ratio of two resistors, as predictable as the resistors are.

The input impedance is the bias divider in parallel with ($\beta$ + 1)$R_E$; the output impedance, looking back into the collector, is $R_C$ (the collector itself being a current source of very high impedance, Chapter 31).

</section>

<section id="refine">

<div className="section-heading"><span>02</span><h2>Refine with $r_e$: degeneration and bypassing</h2></div>

The first model assumed $\Delta v_E = \Delta v_B$ exactly. Replace the transistor with its hybrid-π model (Chapter 30) and derive the gain properly.

<div className="derivation">

<span className="tiny-label">DERIVATION / COMMON-EMITTER GAIN BY NODAL ANALYSIS</span>

1. **Small-signal circuit.** Short the supply to ground and treat coupling capacitors as shorts. Drive the base with an ideal source $v_b$. Between base and emitter is $r_\pi$; from collector to emitter, the controlled source $g_mv_{be}$ (neglect $r_o$ for now); from emitter to ground, $R_E$; from collector to ground, $R_C$.

2. **KCL at the emitter node.** The current arriving through $r_\pi$ plus the controlled-source current must leave through $R_E$:
<Equation tex="\frac{v_b-v_e}{r_\pi}+g_m(v_b-v_e)=\frac{v_e}{R_E}"/>

3. **Solve for $v_{be}$.** Write $v_{be} = v_b - v_e$ and use $g_m + 1/r_\pi = (\beta+1)/r_\pi$. Then $v_{be}(\beta+1)/r_\pi = v_e/R_E$. Substitute $v_e = v_b - v_{be}$:
<Equation tex="v_{be}=v_b\,\frac{r_\pi}{r_\pi+(\beta+1)R_E}"/>
   Only a small fraction of the input appears across the junction; most of it appears across $R_E$.

4. **KCL at the collector node.** The controlled source pulls $g_mv_{be}$ out of the collector node, and the only other path is $R_C$ to ground, so $v_c/R_C = -g_mv_{be}$:
<Equation tex="v_c=-g_mR_C\,v_{be}=-\frac{\beta\,R_C}{r_\pi+(\beta+1)R_E}\,v_b"/>

5. **Rewrite in terms of $r_e$.** Divide numerator and denominator by $\beta+1$, and note $r_\pi/(\beta+1) = r_e$ (exact with $r_e = V_T/I_E$) and $\beta/(\beta+1) = \alpha$:
<Equation tex="G=\frac{v_c}{v_b}=-\alpha\,\frac{R_C}{R_E+r_e}\approx-\frac{R_C}{R_E+r_e}"/>
   The first model's $-R_C/R_E$ is the limit $r_e \ll R_E$. The $r_e$ term is the junction's share of the input.

6. **Output resistance.** Set $v_b = 0$ and drive the collector with a test source. With $r_o$ neglected, the controlled source is an ideal current source, so the only path for the test current is $R_C$: $R_{out} = R_C$. Including $r_o$ adds the Chapter 30 result in parallel: $R_{out} = R_C\parallel r_o[1 + g_m(R_E\parallel r_\pi)]$, which is $R_C$ to within a percent or two.

</div>

At 0.89 mA, $r_e$ = 29 Ω, so the gain of the example stage is −7500/1029 = **−7.29**, close to −7.5 because $R_E$ ≫ $r_e$.

### Bypassing the emitter for maximum gain

A large capacitor across $R_E$ keeps the emitter at a fixed DC voltage (bias still set by $R_E$) but grounds it for signals. Set $R_E = 0$ in the derivation's result. Then only $r_e$ remains, and using $r_e \approx V_T/I_C$:

<Equation tex="G_{\max}=-\frac{R_C}{r_e}=-g_mR_C=-\frac{I_CR_C}{V_T}=-\frac{V_{R_C}}{25\ \mathrm{mV}}" label="BYPASSED GAIN"/>

The maximum gain depends only on the DC voltage across the collector resistor. With 6.7 V across $R_C$ it is about −258. That is a large gain from one transistor, but at a cost:

- **It depends on $I_C$**, and so on temperature and $\beta$ (through the bias).
- **It is nonlinear.** $r_e$ changes during the signal itself, because the current changes. A 5 mV input raises the current 21% on one peak and lowers it 18% on the other; the output is visibly distorted.
- **Input impedance collapses** from ($\beta$ + 1)($R_E$ + $r_e$) ≈ 100 kΩ to ($\beta$ + 1)$r_e$ ≈ 2.9 kΩ, in parallel with the divider.

### The compromise: partial bypassing

Split $R_E$ into an unbypassed part $R_{E1}$ and a bypassed part $R_{E2}$. The bias sees $R_{E1}$ + $R_{E2}$; the signal sees only $R_{E1}$, so replace $R_E$ by $R_{E1}$ in the gain formula:

<Equation tex="G\approx-\frac{R_C}{R_{E1}+r_e}"/>

With $R_{E1}$ = 100 Ω, the example’s gain becomes −7500/129 = −58, still mostly set by resistors. This resistance in the emitter is called **emitter degeneration**. It trades gain for predictability, linearity and input impedance—an early example of negative feedback.

</section>

<section id="swing">

<div className="section-heading"><span>04</span><h2>Output swing and loading</h2></div>

The collector can rise only to $V_{CC}$ (when the transistor cuts off) and fall only to about $V_E$ + 0.2 V (when it saturates). With $V_C$ = 8.3 V, $V_E$ = 0.9 V and $V_{CC}$ = 15 V, an unloaded stage can swing about +6.7 V and −7.2 V.

A load changes both the gain and the swing. If the output drives a load $R_L$ through a coupling capacitor, KCL at the collector in step 4 gains a second path to ground, so $R_C$ is replaced by $R_C$ ∥ $R_L$:

<Equation tex="G=-\frac{R_C\parallel R_L}{R_E+r_e}"/>

A 10 kΩ load reduces the example’s gain from −7.29 to −4.17. It also limits the **upward** swing: as the transistor turns off, collector current can decrease by at most $I_{CQ}$, giving an upward swing of about $I_{CQ}(R_C\parallel R_L)$ = 0.89 mA × 4.29 kΩ = 3.8 V. The symmetric swing into 10 kΩ is therefore ±3.8 V, not ±6.7 V. Larger load currents need more quiescent current, a lower $R_C$, or a follower between the collector and the load.

</section>

<section id="frequency">

<div className="section-heading"><span>05</span><h2>Frequency response</h2></div>

Three capacitors set the low-frequency limit. Each is a high-pass corner, 1/(2πRC), where R is the resistance the capacitor sees:

- **Input coupling**: the source resistance plus the stage’s input impedance.
- **Output coupling**: $R_C$ plus the load.
- **Emitter bypass**: the bypassed resistor in parallel with whatever looks into the emitter: $r_e$ + $R_{E1}$ + ($R_{TH}$ ∥ $R_s$)/($\beta$ + 1).

The bypass capacitor sees a very small resistance and must therefore be large. Fully bypassing the example’s 1 kΩ for a 20 Hz corner, with $r_e$ = 29 Ω, needs about 280 µF.

At high frequencies, the transistor’s capacitances take over. The base–collector capacitance is especially harmful: it connects the input to an inverted output with gain G, so it draws a current as if it were (1 + |G|) times larger. This **Miller effect** makes high-gain common-emitter stages slow, and motivates the cascode and other configurations in a later chapter.

</section>

<section id="design">

<div className="section-heading"><span>06</span><h2>Design a complete stage</h2></div>

<div className="worked-example">

<span className="tiny-label">WORKED EXAMPLE / GAIN OF −10 INTO 10 kΩ</span><h3>15 V supply, 20 Hz low-frequency corner</h3>

<p><strong>Bias.</strong> Keep the Chapter 29 design: 82 kΩ / 10 kΩ divider, $R_C$ = 7.5 kΩ, total $R_E$ ≈ 1 kΩ, $I_C$ = 0.89 mA, $r_e$ = 29 Ω, $V_C$ = 8.3 V.</p>

<p><strong>Gain.</strong> Signals see $R_C$ ∥ $R_L$ = 4.29 kΩ. For −10, $R_{E1}$ + $r_e$ = 429 Ω, so $R_{E1}$ ≈ 400 Ω. Choose <strong>390 Ω unbypassed and 620 Ω bypassed</strong> (1.01 kΩ total, bias unchanged). Gain: −4286/(390 + 29) = <strong>−10.2</strong>.</p>

<p><strong>Input.</strong> $Z_{\mathrm{in}}$ = 8.91 kΩ ∥ 101 × 419 Ω = <strong>7.36 kΩ</strong>. A 20 Hz input corner needs $C_{\mathrm{in}}$ ≥ 1.1 µF; use 4.7 µF.</p>

<p><strong>Output.</strong> The output capacitor sees 7.5 + 10 kΩ, so $C_{\mathrm{out}}$ ≥ 0.45 µF; use 2.2 µF.</p>

<p><strong>Bypass.</strong> $C_E$ sees 620 Ω ∥ 419 Ω ≈ 250 Ω, needing 32 µF for 20 Hz; use 100 µF so this corner sits well below the others.</p>

<p><strong>Swing.</strong> About ±3.8 V into the load, limited by cutoff as computed above. Distortion stays low because 390 Ω of degeneration is thirteen times $r_e$.</p>

</div>

Notice what made the design robust: the gain depends on $R_C$, $R_L$ and $R_{E1}$, with $r_e$ as a modest correction; the bias depends on the divider and $R_E$; $\beta$ appears only in the input impedance. When higher gain is needed, it is better to cascade two degenerated stages than to rely on one bypassed stage—or to wrap the gain inside an overall feedback loop.

### A bypass capacitor produces a shelf, not simply an output high-pass

For split emitter resistance, the signal emitter impedance is $R_{E1}$ in series with $R_{E2}\parallel C_E$, that is, $Z_E=R_{E1}+R_{E2}/(1+j\omega R_{E2}C_E)$. Replace $R_E$ by $Z_E$ in the nodal result (the derivation holds for any emitter impedance), neglecting base loading and Early effect:

<Equation tex="A_v(j\omega)\approx-\frac{R_C\parallel R_L}{r_e+R_{E1}+R_{E2}/(1+j\omega R_{E2}C_E)}" />

To find its corners, multiply the numerator and denominator by $1 + j\omega R_{E2}C_E$ and let $R_1 = r_e + R_{E1}$:

<Equation tex="A_v=-\frac{R_C\parallel R_L}{R_1+R_{E2}}\cdot\frac{1+j\omega R_{E2}C_E}{1+j\omega\,(R_1\parallel R_{E2})\,C_E}"/>

The zero is at $\omega_z = 1/(R_{E2}C_E)$, where the capacitor's reactance equals $R_{E2}$. The pole is at the higher frequency $\omega_p = 1/[(R_1\parallel R_{E2})C_E]$, because there the capacitor sees $R_{E2}$ in parallel with the resistance looking up into the emitter. Below $\omega_z$ the gain is $-(R_C\parallel R_L)/(R_1 + R_{E2})$; above $\omega_p$ it is $-(R_C\parallel R_L)/R_1$.

At low frequency, both emitter resistors degenerate the stage. At high frequency, only $R_{E1}$ remains. The gain rises between two finite levels, with an associated phase change. Calling the bypass "a short" is justified only after checking its impedance across the intended band.

For $R_{E1}=390\ \Omega$, $R_{E2}=620\ \Omega$, $r_e=29\ \Omega$ and $C_E=100$ µF: $f_z = 1/(2\pi\cdot620\cdot100\ \mu\text{F})$ ≈ 2.57 Hz, and $R_1\parallel R_{E2} = 419\parallel620$ ≈ 250 Ω gives $f_p$ ≈ 6.37 Hz, with a stiff base source. Coupling capacitors add their own high-pass responses. Putting each individual corner at 20 Hz does not make the complete amplifier only 3 dB down at 20 Hz; their losses and phases combine.

<div className="takeaway"><span className="tiny-label">THE IDEA TO KEEP</span><p>A common-emitter stage converts input voltage to emitter current with $R_E$, then current back to voltage with $R_C$: <em>G = −$R_C$/($R_E$ + $r_e$)</em>. Degeneration buys predictability with gain.</p></div>

<section id="check">

<div className="section-heading"><span>07</span><h2>Check your understanding</h2></div>

<details>
<summary>1. Why is collector voltage inverted?</summary>
<div>

A positive base signal increases collector current, increasing the collector-resistor drop and lowering collector voltage.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>2. Does bypassing the emitter resistor remove its DC bias feedback?</summary>
<div>

No. The capacitor carries changing current but is open in steady DC, so the resistor still sets the bias.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>3. Why does an AC-coupled collector load reduce maximum positive swing?</summary>
<div>

Collector current can decrease by only $I_{CQ}$ before cutoff. That current change across $R_C\parallel R_L$ gives the available upward voltage swing.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>4. Why use partial rather than complete bypassing?</summary>
<div>

It retains signal degeneration and input impedance while increasing gain, leaving the DC emitter resistance substantially unchanged.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>5. Can several individually chosen 20 Hz corners give a total 20 Hz −3 dB point?</summary>
<div>

Usually not. Multiply the individual transfer functions and account for the bypass shelf; their losses and phases combine.

</div>
</details>
