<div><span className="tiny-label">A PRACTICAL QUESTION</span><p className="opening-question">You need ±10 V into an 8 Ω loudspeaker. A single emitter follower would idle at more than a watt per channel even in silence. Is there a better way?</p></div>

<div>

Chapter 28 found that an NPN follower can source current but only sinks through its emitter resistor. To swing a low-impedance load symmetrically, that resistor must carry the full peak load current all the time. Most of the supply power becomes heat, whether or not a signal is present.

The cure is to give the circuit a transistor for each direction: an NPN follower that pushes current into the load and a PNP follower that pulls it out. Each works only half the time, and neither needs to idle at the peak current. The price is a new kind of distortion near zero, and a thermal-stability problem that took the industry years to tame.

</div>

<section id="classa">

<div className="section-heading"><span>01</span><h2>Why class A wastes power</h2></div>

A single NPN follower with its emitter resistor returned to −V_CC conducts over the whole cycle: **class A**. To pull an 8 Ω load to −10 V, the emitter resistor must sink 1.25 A at that moment, so the quiescent current must be at least 1.25 A. From ±15 V supplies that is 37.5 W of continuous supply power to deliver at most 6.25 W of sine-wave power.

In general, with the quiescent current set just high enough for full swing, the supply delivers 2V_CC I_Q continuously while the load receives at most V_CC²/(2R_L) with I_Q = V_CC/R_L:

<Equation tex="\eta_{\max}=\frac{V_{CC}^2/2R_L}{2V_{CC}^2/R_L}=25\%\qquad(\text{resistor-loaded class A})"/>

And the 25% is reached only at full output. At idle, everything is wasted. Class A is still used where its simplicity and low distortion justify the heat: low-power stages, headphone drivers, some audio amplifiers.

</section>

<section id="classb">

<div className="section-heading"><span>02</span><h2>Class B: efficiency and dissipation</h2></div>

Join the emitters of an NPN follower (collector to +V_CC) and a PNP follower (collector to −V_CC), drive both bases from the input, and connect the load from the joined emitters to ground. On positive half-cycles the NPN conducts and the PNP is off; on negative half-cycles the reverse. With no signal, neither conducts. Each transistor carries a half-sine of current, which is **class B**.

For a sinusoidal output of peak V_p into R_L, each supply delivers current only on its half-cycle, with average value V_p/(πR_L). The total supply power and the load power are

<Equation tex="P_S=2V_{CC}\frac{V_p}{\pi R_L},\qquad P_L=\frac{V_p^2}{2R_L},\qquad \eta=\frac{P_L}{P_S}=\frac{\pi}{4}\frac{V_p}{V_{CC}}\le78.5\%" label="CLASS B"/>

<ClassBPowerFigure/>

The transistors dissipate the difference, P_S − P_L. It is zero with no signal—the great advantage of class B—and it is *not* greatest at full output. Differentiating with respect to V_p and setting the result to zero:

<Equation tex="\frac{d}{dV_p}\left(\frac{2V_{CC}V_p}{\pi R_L}-\frac{V_p^2}{2R_L}\right)=0\ \Rightarrow\ V_p=\frac{2V_{CC}}{\pi},\qquad P_{D,\max}=\frac{2V_{CC}^2}{\pi^2R_L}"/>

For ±15 V and 8 Ω: maximum sine output 14.1 W (ideal), but the transistors run hottest at about 9.5 V peak, where together they dissipate **5.7 W**. Size heat sinks for that, not for full output or idle. Real stages lose another volt or two to V_BE and saturation, so practical efficiency at full output is closer to 60–70%.

</section>

<section id="crossover">

<div className="section-heading"><span>03</span><h2>Crossover distortion</h2></div>

With both bases tied to the input, the NPN does not conduct until the input exceeds its emitter by about 0.6 V, and the PNP not until the input is 0.6 V below it. In between is a **dead zone** of about 1.2 V where the output barely moves. Each zero crossing of the signal is flattened.

Unlike clipping, crossover distortion is worst for *small* signals. A 1 V-peak sine loses most of its shape; a 10 V-peak sine loses a small fraction near each zero crossing. In the lab’s model, total harmonic distortion is about 44% at 1 V, 13% at 3 V and 4% at 10 V. Because quiet passages of music and small control signals live near zero, the distortion is very audible and very harmful to precision.

</section>

<section id="classab">

<div className="section-heading"><span>05</span><h2>Class AB bias and thermal stability</h2></div>

### Biasing out the dead zone

Separate the two bases by about 2V_BE so that both transistors are already slightly conducting at zero input. Two diodes in series, fed from current sources or resistors, provide that voltage; the input drives the midpoint. Now there is no dead zone: as one transistor takes over, the other is still conducting. This is **class AB**: each transistor conducts for slightly more than half the cycle.

In the lab, two 0.65 V diode drops with 0.47 Ω emitter resistors set a quiescent current of about 40 mA and bring THD at 1 V from 44% to about 0.05%. The cost is 2V_CC I_Q = 1.2 W of standing dissipation, much less than class A’s.

### The thermal problem

The quiescent current depends exponentially on the difference between the bias voltage and the transistors’ V_BEs. As the output transistors warm, their V_BE falls about 2.1 mV/°C; with a fixed bias voltage the current rises, which heats them further.

Suppose the heat sink rises 30 °C. Each output V_BE falls 63 mV, so the bias now exceeds the transistors’ needs by 126 mV. Without emitter resistors the quiescent current would rise by e<sup>63/26</sup> ≈ 11 times. With 0.47 Ω emitter resistors, the extra 126 mV must also drop across 2 × 0.47 Ω and the two r_e, roughly 2.2 Ω in total, so the current rises by about 56 mA: from 40 mA to nearly 100 mA. Better, but still a large change that would increase heating.

The complete fix has three parts:

- **Emitter resistors** (0.1–0.5 Ω) provide local negative feedback against current increases and help paralleled devices share (Chapter 40).
- **Thermal tracking**: the bias diodes, or better a transistor, are mounted on the output transistors’ heat sink, so the bias voltage falls as the output V_BEs fall.
- **An adjustable bias generator**: the V_BE multiplier.

<VbeMultiplierFigure/>

The V_BE multiplier is a transistor with a divider from collector to emitter whose tap drives its base. It conducts whatever current flows, while holding its collector–emitter voltage at V_BE(1 + R₂/R₁). The resistor ratio sets the bias; its V_BE, on the heat sink, makes the bias fall with temperature at a rate (1 + R₂/R₁) × 2.1 mV/°C, matching the two (or more) output junctions it compensates.

</section>

<section id="practice-design">

<div className="section-heading"><span>06</span><h2>Feedback, protection and practical design</h2></div>

### Feedback hides the dead zone

Put the class B stage inside a negative feedback loop, as an op-amp output stage is. The amplifier driving the bases compares the actual output with the desired one; when the output stalls in the dead zone, the error grows and the driver swings the bases rapidly across the gap. With loop gain A the dead zone shrinks by roughly A: the lab’s gain of 200 brings 1 V distortion from 44% to under 0.2%. Feedback cannot fix the gap instantaneously, so a fast driver is needed, and most real designs combine class AB bias *and* feedback.

### Protection

A short circuit on the output turns a follower into a dissipator of V_CC times whatever current it can deliver. A common protection adds a small resistor in each emitter and a sensing transistor across it: when the voltage across the resistor reaches about 0.6 V, the sensing transistor steals base drive and limits the current. With 0.33 Ω, the limit is about 1.8 A. Output transistors must also stay inside their **safe operating area**, which shrinks at high voltage because of second breakdown (Chapter 40).

### A design outline

For 10 W into 8 Ω: V_p = √(2 × 10 × 8) = 12.6 V, I_p = 1.6 A. Allow about 2 V for V_BE, emitter-resistor drop and driver headroom: ±15 V supplies. Expect up to about 5.7 W of transistor dissipation at the worst amplitude. Choose output transistors rated for at least 2–3 A, 40 V and the dissipation with margin at the heat-sink temperature; drive them from Darlingtons or driver transistors (Chapter 38), because 1.6 A at β = 50 needs 32 mA of base current; bias with a V_BE multiplier for 20–50 mA quiescent; add emitter resistors and current limiting; and close a feedback loop around the whole amplifier.

<div className="takeaway"><span className="tiny-label">THE IDEA TO KEEP</span><p>Class B wastes nothing at idle and at most 21.5% at full swing, but <em>leaves a dead zone</em>. Bias it into class AB, make the bias track temperature, and let feedback clean up the rest.</p></div>
