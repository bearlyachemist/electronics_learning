<div><span className="tiny-label">A PRACTICAL QUESTION</span><p className="opening-question">An integrated circuit needs thirty bias currents, all proportional to one reference. Thirty resistor-and-divider current sources would waste area and drift independently. What does an IC designer do instead?</p></div>

<div>

Chapter 34 introduced the idea: two transistors with the same $I_S$ and the same $V_{BE}$ carry the same collector current. Let one transistor *measure* a reference current by developing the $V_{BE}$ it needs, and let others *reuse* that $V_{BE}$. The result is the current mirror, the most common circuit inside analog integrated circuits.

The basic mirror is two transistors and a resistor. Its imperfections are small but instructive, because each one comes from a different line of the Ebers–Moll model: finite $\beta$, the Early effect, $V_{BE}$ mismatch, temperature. This chapter treats each error quantitatively and then shows the circuit fix that removes it.

</div>

<section id="why">

<div className="section-heading"><span>01</span><h2>Why mirrors</h2></div>

The emitter-degenerated current source of Chapter 31 sets its current with a base voltage and a resistor. That works, but every source needs its own reference voltage and its own volt or so of emitter headroom. A mirror needs neither. One reference current is generated once; each copy costs a single transistor.

Mirrors have three properties that make them valuable:

- **Programmability.** All outputs track the reference. Change one resistor, or drive the reference from another circuit, and every copy follows.
- **Compliance.** The basic mirror needs no emitter-resistor voltage. It conducts down toward a few tenths of a volt, but accuracy degrades as the collector–base junction becomes forward-biased; specify compliance at an allowed current error.
- **Matching rather than accuracy.** The absolute value of $V_{BE}$ never appears. Only the *difference* between two transistors matters, and transistors made side by side on one chip match to a fraction of a millivolt.

</section>

<section id="basic">

<div className="section-heading"><span>02</span><h2>The basic mirror, derived</h2></div>

<MirrorFigure/>

$Q_1$ is diode-connected: collector tied to base, so $V_{CB}$ = 0 and it stays in the active region. A resistor R from $V_{CC}$ delivers the reference current:

<Equation tex="I_{\mathrm{ref}}=\frac{V_{CC}-V_{BE}}{R}"/>

$Q_1$ settles at whatever $V_{BE}$ makes its collector current, plus the two base currents, equal to $I_{\mathrm{ref}}$. $Q_2$ shares the same $V_{BE}$. With matched saturation currents and ignoring the Early effect for the moment, $Q_2$ carries the same collector current $I_C$ as $Q_1$. Counting currents at the collector of $Q_1$:

<Equation tex="I_{\mathrm{ref}}=I_C+\frac{I_C}{\beta}+\frac{I_C}{\beta}\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad I_{\mathrm{out}}=I_C=\frac{I_{\mathrm{ref}}}{1+2/\beta}" label="BASIC MIRROR"/>

On 15 V, R = 14.3 kΩ gives $I_{\mathrm{ref}}$ ≈ 1.00 mA. With $\beta$ = 100 the output is 0.980 mA, a 2% error; with $\beta$ = 30 it is 6% low.

### Ratios and multiple outputs

On a chip, an output transistor with n times the emitter area has n times $I_S$ and carries n times the current at the same $V_{BE}$: a **ratioed mirror**. Several output transistors can share one reference; each adds a base current, so with N outputs the error becomes (N + 1)/$\beta$. Discrete designs obtain ratios with unequal emitter resistors instead (section 05).

### Count base current when the copies have unequal sizes

For N output devices with area ratios $n_1,\ldots,n_N$, each collector carries $n_k I_{C1}$ in the matched, infinite-Early-voltage model. Kirchhoff’s law at the shared node gives:

<Equation tex="I_{ref}=I_{C1}\left[1+\frac{1+\sum_k n_k}{\beta}\right],\qquad I_k=\frac{n_k I_{ref}}{1+(1+\sum_j n_j)/\beta}"/>

Ten unity copies at β = 100 each deliver 90.1% of the reference. A single 10× copy has the same total base-current burden. The familiar 2/β error belongs specifically to two equal transistors; count total output area when designing a mirror bank.

</section>

### The Early effect

$Q_1$ always sits at $V_{CE}$ = $V_{BE}$ ≈ 0.65 V. $Q_2$’s collector sits wherever the load puts it. From Ebers–Moll, collector current scales as (1 + $V_{CE}$/$V_A$), so

<Equation tex="\frac{I_{\mathrm{out}}}{I_{C1}}\approx\frac{1+V_{\mathrm{out}}/V_A}{1+V_{BE}/V_A}\approx1+\frac{V_{\mathrm{out}}-V_{BE}}{V_A},\qquad R_{\mathrm{out}}=r_o=\frac{V_A}{I_{\mathrm{out}}}"/>

In the lab’s Ebers–Moll model with $V_A$ = 100 V, the ratio $I_{\mathrm{out}}$/$I_{\mathrm{ref}}$ is 0.984 at 1 V, 1.023 at 5 V and 1.072 at 10 V; the output resistance is about 100 kΩ. A mirror is a *current source*, but a modest one.

### Mismatch and emitter degeneration

A $V_{BE}$ difference ΔV between $Q_1$ and $Q_2$ (from unequal $I_S$, or a temperature difference of ΔV/2.1 mV degrees) changes the output by $e^{ΔV/V_T}$: 3.9% for 1 mV. Adding equal emitter resistors $R_E$ to both transistors changes that. If the voltage across each resistor is $V_{RE}$, a mismatch ΔV must now be absorbed by the resistors as well as the junctions:

<Equation tex="\frac{\Delta I}{I}\approx\frac{\Delta V}{V_{RE}+V_T}"/>

With 0.25 V across the resistors, 1 mV of mismatch causes only 0.36% error instead of 3.9%. The same degeneration multiplies the output resistance, as in Chapter 31: with 1 kΩ resistors (about 0.94 V across them) the lab’s mirror rises from 100 kΩ to about **2.5 MΩ**. The cost is a volt of compliance and headroom.

</section>

<section id="improved">

<div className="section-heading"><span>05</span><h2>$\beta$-helper and Wilson</h2></div>

### The $\beta$-helper

Replace $Q_1$’s collector-to-base wire with a third transistor, an emitter follower $Q_3$ whose base is driven from $Q_1$’s collector and whose emitter supplies both bases. The reference resistor now has to supply only $Q_3$’s base current, which is the two base currents divided by $\beta$ + 1:

<Equation tex="I_{\mathrm{out}}=\frac{I_{\mathrm{ref}}}{1+\dfrac{2}{\beta(\beta+1)}}"/>

At $\beta$ = 100 the base-current error drops from 2% to 0.02%. $Q_1$’s collector now sits at $2V_{BE}$ instead of $V_{BE}$, which slightly changes the Early-effect mismatch but does not remove it; the output resistance is still about $r_o$.

<ImprovedMirrorsFigure/>

### The Wilson mirror

The Wilson mirror uses three transistors in a feedback loop. $Q_2$ is diode-connected and sets the $V_{BE}$ for $Q_1$. $Q_3$ carries the output current through $Q_2$. $Q_1$’s collector, fed by the reference, drives $Q_3$’s base.

Follow a disturbance. If the output current tries to rise (say, because the output voltage rose), $Q_2$’s current rises, raising $V_{BE}$, so $Q_1$ draws more of the reference current, pulling $Q_3$’s base down and reducing the output current. The loop holds the output equal to the reference. Two consequences follow from the analysis:

<Equation tex="\frac{I_{\mathrm{out}}}{I_{\mathrm{ref}}}=1-\frac{2}{\beta^2+2\beta+2},\qquad R_{\mathrm{out}}\approx\frac{\beta r_o}{2}"/>

The base-current error is second order in 1/$\beta$ (0.02% at $\beta$ = 100), and the output resistance is $\beta$/2 times larger than a single transistor’s: in the lab, about 5.2 MΩ against 102 kΩ for the basic mirror. $Q_1$ and $Q_2$ also operate at different collector voltages ($2V_{BE}$ and $V_{BE}$); an **improved Wilson** adds a fourth, diode-connected transistor to equalize them.

</section>

<section id="widlar">

<div className="section-heading"><span>06</span><h2>Widlar sources, ratios and active loads</h2></div>

### Small currents from small resistors

Suppose a chip needs 10 µA from a 15 V supply. A basic mirror would need R = 14.35 V/10 µA ≈ 1.4 MΩ, an enormous resistor on silicon. Bob Widlar’s solution keeps a 1 mA reference and puts a resistor $R_E$ only in the emitter of the output transistor. The two transistors now have different $V_{BE}$, and the difference appears across $R_E$:

<Equation tex="V_{BE1}-V_{BE2}=V_T\ln\frac{I_{C1}}{I_{C2}}=I_{C2}R_E\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad R_E=\frac{V_T}{I_{\mathrm{out}}}\ln\frac{I_{\mathrm{ref}}}{I_{\mathrm{out}}}" label="WIDLAR CURRENT SOURCE"/>

For 10 µA from 1 mA: $R_E$ = (25.85 mV/10 µA) × ln 100 = **11.9 kΩ**, a hundred times smaller than the brute-force resistor. The output is very insensitive to the reference: a small change in ln $I_{\mathrm{ref}}$ changes $I_{\mathrm{out}}$ at a local slope $V_T$/($R_E$ + $r_e$) ≈ 1.8 µA per unit of log-reference change and its output resistance is very high because of the emitter degeneration: about 25 MΩ for the lab’s 20 µA example.

### Ratios with emitter resistors

In discrete circuits, emitter areas cannot be chosen, but emitter resistors can. With degeneration large compared with $V_T$, the currents divide in the inverse ratio of the resistors: 1 kΩ under $Q_1$ and 500 Ω under $Q_2$ gives roughly twice the current, plus a small $V_T$ ln 2 correction.

### PNP mirrors and active loads

A PNP mirror, with emitters at the positive supply, sources currents into loads returned to ground. The most important use of mirrors, however, is as the collector load of an amplifier. A transistor collector looks like a large resistance, so a mirror in place of a collector resistor gives enormous voltage gain without a large supply voltage. Chapter 36 uses exactly this to turn a differential pair into a single high-gain stage.

### A Widlar sensitivity calculation

Ignoring base currents and the Early effect, write $I_{out}R_E=V_T\ln(I_{ref}/I_{out})$. Differentiate while temperature and resistor stay fixed:

<Equation tex="\frac{d\ln I_{out}}{d\ln I_{ref}}=\frac{V_T}{V_T+I_{out}R_E},\qquad \frac{d\ln I_{out}}{d\ln R_E}=-\frac{I_{out}R_E}{V_T+I_{out}R_E}"/>

For the 1 mA-to-10 µA design, $I_{out}R_E=119$ mV. Reference sensitivity is 0.179: a small 10% reference change makes about 1.8% output change. Resistor sensitivity is −0.821: a 1% larger emitter resistor makes about 0.82% less current. Suppressing one sensitivity does not suppress every source of error.

<div className="worked-example">

<span className="tiny-label">WORKED EXAMPLE / ACCURACY AND COMPLIANCE</span><h3>A 1 mA mirror across a 2–10 V output range</h3>

<p>At β = 100 and $V_A=100$ V, take the reference collector voltage as 0.65 V. The collector ratio is $k=(1+V_{out}/V_A)/(1+0.65/V_A)$, and exact base-current counting gives $I_{out}/I_{ref}=k/[1+(1+k)/β]$. At 2 V this is 0.9934; at 10 V it is 1.0705. Early effect can outweigh the nominal 2% base-current deficit.</p>

<p>A Wilson mirror improves flatness but needs roughly one junction voltage plus the output transistor’s saturation margin at its output. An emitter-degenerated mirror needs its emitter-resistor voltage as well. Compare each topology over the required output range, then budget mismatch, reference tolerance and temperature separately.</p>

<section id="check">

<div className="section-heading"><span>07</span><h2>Check your understanding</h2></div>

<details>
<summary>1. Does a basic mirror output exactly the reference current?</summary>
<div>

Only in an ideal limit. With equal collectors and finite β, $I_{out}=I_{ref}/(1+2/β)$; unequal collector voltages add Early-effect error.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>2. Why does adding ten outputs worsen the base-current error?</summary>
<div>

The reference node supplies all eleven base currents. At equal device size, the denominator becomes $1+11/β$.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>3. Which connection distinguishes the helper from the Wilson?</summary>
<div>

The helper’s third collector goes to the positive rail and its emitter supplies both bases. In the Wilson, the third collector is the output, while its emitter returns to the diode-connected second transistor.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>4. Does a Wilson retain the basic mirror’s low compliance voltage?</summary>
<div>

No. Its output transistor sits above another transistor’s junction voltage, so additional output headroom is required.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>5. Why is a Widlar source useful for microamp currents?</summary>
<div>

Its output emitter resistor absorbs a logarithmic junction-voltage difference. A modest resistor can generate a small current from a much larger reference, avoiding a very large reference resistor.

</div>
</details>
