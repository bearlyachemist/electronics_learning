<div className="opening-grid">

<div><span className="tiny-label">A PRACTICAL QUESTION</span><p className="opening-question">A design works perfectly with the transistor on your bench. The next one from the same reel has $\beta$ = 400 and the stage is dead. What went wrong?</p></div>

<div>

Audio, sensor and radio signals swing both positive and negative, but an NPN transistor only conducts with its base above its emitter and its collector above that. The cure is to give the transistor a steady **quiescent** current and voltages—a **bias**—and to superimpose the signal on top, usually through coupling capacitors.

Choosing the bias point is half of the job. The other half is making it *stay* there, whatever the transistor’s $\beta$, whatever the temperature, and whatever the resistor tolerances. This chapter treats the emitter follower first, then the common-emitter stage, then the effects of $\beta$ and temperature.

</div>

</div>

<span className="tiny-label">THE PATH THROUGH THIS CHAPTER</span><p>Why a stage needs bias → Biasing an emitter follower → Biasing a common-emitter stage → Explore bias stability → Temperature and feedback → A biasing recipe → Check your understanding.</p>

</div>

<section id="why">

<div className="section-heading"><span>01</span><h2>Why a stage needs bias</h2></div>

Feed a ±1 V signal straight into the base of an emitter follower whose emitter resistor goes to ground. On negative half cycles the base–emitter junction is off and the output is zero; even on positive peaks the output is 0.65 V low. Half the signal is lost.

Raise the base to a steady DC level instead—say, half the supply—and add the signal through a capacitor. The capacitor passes the signal (a high-pass filter, Chapter 15) while blocking the DC level of the source. The transistor now operates around a fixed point and responds to signal swings in both directions.

The **quiescent point** consists of the resting collector current $I_{CQ}$ and the resting terminal voltages. It determines how far the output can swing before cutoff or saturation, what the input and output impedances are (they depend on current through $r_e$, Chapter 30), and how much power the stage wastes.

</section>

<section id="follower">

<div className="section-heading"><span>02</span><h2>Biasing an emitter follower</h2></div>

For the largest symmetric swing, put the emitter near the middle of the supply. Then set the base one $V_{BE}$ higher with a divider, and choose the divider so that the base current does not pull it significantly away from its unloaded voltage.

### The stiffness rule

The divider is a Thévenin source $V_{TH}$ behind $R_{TH}$ = $R_1$ ∥ $R_2$ (Chapter 1). Looking into the base, the transistor presents ($\beta$ + 1)$R_E$ (Chapter 28). Treat this as a voltage divider between $R_{TH}$ and ($\beta$ + 1)$R_E$.

<div className="derivation">

<span className="tiny-label">DERIVATION / THE STIFFNESS RULE</span>

1. **Base loop.** From $V_{TH}$ through $R_{TH}$, across the junction, and down $R_E$: $V_{TH} = I_BR_{TH} + V_{BE} + I_ER_E$. With $I_E = (\beta+1)I_B$:
<Equation tex="I_B=\frac{V_{TH}-V_{BE}}{R_{TH}+(\beta+1)R_E}"/>

2. **How far the base sags.** The base voltage is $V_B = V_{TH} - I_BR_{TH}$. Measured from the unloaded value, as a fraction of the voltage available across the junction and $R_E$:
<Equation tex="\frac{V_{TH}-V_B}{V_{TH}-V_{BE}}=\frac{R_{TH}}{R_{TH}+(\beta+1)R_E}"/>
   This is exactly a divider between $R_{TH}$ and the transistor's input resistance $(\beta+1)R_E$.

3. **Choose the ratio.** Make $R_{TH}$ one tenth of $(\beta+1)R_E$ and the sag is $1/11$, about 9%. Because the sag is already small, a change in $\beta$ changes it only by a fraction of that 9%:
<Equation tex="R_{TH}\le\frac{(\beta_{\min}+1)R_E}{10}"/>

</div>

Use the *minimum* $\beta$. A divider ten times stiffer than the transistor’s input keeps the base voltage set by the resistors, not by the transistor.

<div className="worked-example">

<span className="tiny-label">WORKED EXAMPLE / AN AUDIO FOLLOWER ON 15 V</span><h3>1 mA quiescent, input coupled from 20 Hz</h3>

<p>Put the emitter at 7.5 V with 1 mA: $R_E$ = <strong>7.5 kΩ</strong>. The base should be near 8.15 V, and with $\beta$ ≥ 100 the stiffness rule asks for $R_{TH}$ ≤ 101 × 7.5 kΩ/10 ≈ 76 kΩ.</p>

<p>$R_1$ = 130 kΩ (top) and $R_2$ = 150 kΩ (bottom) give $V_{TH}$ = 15 × 150/280 = 8.04 V and $R_{TH}$ = 69.6 kΩ. The emitter rests at about 7.39 V with $I_Q$ ≈ 0.98 mA.</p>

<p>The signal source sees the divider in parallel with the transistor: $Z_{\mathrm{in}}$ = 69.6 kΩ ∥ 757 kΩ = <strong>63.8 kΩ</strong>. For a 20 Hz corner the input capacitor must be at least 1/(2π × 20 × 63.8 kΩ) = 0.125 µF; choosing <strong>0.47 µF</strong> puts the corner at 5.3 Hz, so 20 Hz passes nearly unattenuated.</p>

</div>

Note the price of stiffness: the divider dominates the input impedance. The transistor offered 757 kΩ; the circuit offers 64 kΩ. Techniques that preserve the high input impedance (bootstrapping, a later topic) exist because of this.

</section>

<section id="ce">

<div className="section-heading"><span>03</span><h2>Biasing a common-emitter stage</h2></div>

The common-emitter amplifier (Chapter 32) takes its output from the collector, through a collector resistor $R_C$ to the supply. Its bias design has three targets:

- **Collector current**, set by the base voltage and an emitter resistor: $I_C$ ≈ ($V_B$ − 0.65)/$R_E$.
- **Collector voltage**, near the middle of the range it can swing over: up to $V_{CC}$ at cutoff and down to roughly $V_E$ + 0.2 V at saturation.
- **Emitter voltage** around 1 V or more, so that $V_{BE}$’s variations (tens of millivolts with temperature and current) are small compared with it.

Solve the base loop exactly, with the divider replaced by its Thévenin equivalent.

<div className="derivation">

<span className="tiny-label">DERIVATION / COLLECTOR CURRENT OF THE DIVIDER-BIASED STAGE</span>

1. **Replace the divider** by $V_{TH} = V_{CC}R_2/(R_1+R_2)$ in series with $R_{TH} = R_1\parallel R_2$.

2. **KVL around the base loop**, from $V_{TH}$ to ground through $R_{TH}$, the junction and $R_E$:
<Equation tex="V_{TH}=I_BR_{TH}+V_{BE}+I_ER_E"/>

3. **Express both currents in terms of $I_C$**, using $I_B = I_C/\beta$ and $I_E = I_C(\beta+1)/\beta$:
<Equation tex="V_{TH}-V_{BE}=I_C\,\frac{R_{TH}+(\beta+1)R_E}{\beta}"/>

4. **Solve:**
<Equation tex="I_C=\frac{\beta\,(V_{TH}-V_{BE})}{R_{TH}+(\beta+1)R_E}=\frac{V_{TH}-V_{BE}}{R_E\,(\beta+1)/\beta+R_{TH}/\beta}" label="DIVIDER BIAS"/>

5. **Large-$\beta$ form.** For $\beta \gg 1$, $(\beta+1)/\beta \approx 1$, giving $I_C \approx (V_{TH}-V_{BE})/(R_E + R_{TH}/\beta)$. As $R_{TH}/\beta \to 0$ this reduces to the simple $I_C \approx (V_B - V_{BE})/R_E$.

</div>

$\beta$ appears only in the term $R_{TH}$/$\beta$ (and weakly in $(\beta+1)/\beta$). If that is small compared with $R_E$, $\beta$ hardly matters—which is just the stiffness rule again.

<div className="worked-example">

<span className="tiny-label">WORKED EXAMPLE / 1 mA ON 15 V</span><h3>$R_C$ = 7.5 kΩ, $R_E$ = 1 kΩ, divider 82 kΩ / 10 kΩ</h3>

<p>$V_{TH}$ = 15 × 10/92 = 1.63 V and $R_{TH}$ = 8.91 kΩ. With $\beta$ = 100: $I_C$ = 100 × (1.63 − 0.65)/(8.91 k + 101 × 1 k) = <strong>0.892 mA</strong>, $V_C$ = 15 − 0.892 × 7.5 = 8.31 V, $V_E$ = 0.90 V.</p>

<p>With $\beta$ = 50 the current is 0.818 mA; with $\beta$ = 500, 0.961 mA. A tenfold range of $\beta$ moves the collector current by only ±8%. Using $R_1$ = 75 kΩ instead would bring the nominal current to 1.02 mA.</p>

</div>

### The tempting alternative

A single resistor from the supply to the base sets $I_B$ = ($V_{CC}$ − 0.65)/$R_B$ and therefore $I_C$ = $\beta I_B$. Choosing $R_B$ = 1.61 MΩ matches the design above at $\beta$ = 100. But at $\beta$ = 50 the current halves to 0.45 mA, and at $\beta$ = 500 the transistor saturates. This “base-resistor bias” makes the circuit’s operation proportional to the least predictable parameter of the transistor. Avoid it.

</section>

The green curve is almost flat because the emitter resistor provides **negative feedback**: if the collector current tries to rise, $V_E$ rises, which reduces $V_{BE}$ and pulls the current back down. Formal feedback theory comes later, but you can already see it working here.

</section>

<section id="temperature">

<div className="section-heading"><span>05</span><h2>Temperature and feedback</h2></div>

$V_{BE}$ at fixed current falls about 2.1 mV/°C (Chapter 26). In the divider stage the base voltage is fixed, so a falling $V_{BE}$ raises $V_E$ and therefore the current. To quantify this, differentiate the divider-bias result with respect to $V_{BE}$:

<Equation tex="\frac{\partial I_C}{\partial V_{BE}}=-\frac{\beta}{R_{TH}+(\beta+1)R_E}\approx-\frac{1}{R_E}"/>

Divide by $I_C \approx (V_{TH}-V_{BE})/R_E = V_E/R_E$ to get the fractional change. The $R_E$ cancels, leaving only the emitter voltage:

<Equation tex="\frac{\Delta I_C}{I_C}\approx\frac{-\Delta V_{BE}}{V_E}=\frac{2.1\ \mathrm{mV/^\circ C}\times\Delta T}{V_E}"/>

With $V_E$ ≈ 0.9 V, a 60 °C rise increases the current by roughly 13–14%: in the lab, from 0.89 mA at 25 °C to 1.01 mA at 85 °C. If $V_E$ were only 0.1 V, the same rise would more than double the current. That is the reason for the rule “a volt or so across the emitter resistor.” In power circuits, where self-heating raises temperature, which raises current, which raises heating, inadequate emitter degeneration leads to **thermal runaway**.

### Collector-to-base feedback

Another way to stabilize bias is to connect the base resistor to the collector instead of the supply. If the current rises, the collector voltage falls, reducing the base current.

<div className="derivation">

<span className="tiny-label">DERIVATION / COLLECTOR-TO-BASE FEEDBACK BIAS</span>

1. **KCL at the collector.** $R_C$ carries both the collector current and the base current that leaves the collector through $R_B$: $I_{R_C} = I_C + I_B = (\beta+1)I_B$.

2. **KVL from the supply to ground** through $R_C$, $R_B$ and the grounded-emitter junction:
<Equation tex="V_{CC}=(\beta+1)I_BR_C+I_BR_B+V_{BE}"/>

3. **Solve for $I_B$ and multiply by $\beta$:**
<Equation tex="I_C=\frac{\beta\,(V_{CC}-V_{BE})}{(\beta+1)R_C+R_B}\approx\frac{V_{CC}-V_{BE}}{R_C+R_B/\beta}"/>

4. **Why it cannot saturate hard.** The collector voltage is $V_C = V_{BE} + I_BR_B$, which is always above $V_{BE}$. The collector can never fall below the base, so the base–collector junction never becomes strongly forward-biased.

</div>

With $R_C$ = 7.5 kΩ and $R_B$ = 750 kΩ, $\beta$ = 50 gives 0.63 mA and $\beta$ = 500 gives 1.59 mA. That is far better than the single base resistor (it can never saturate hard), but much worse than the divider with $R_E$. Its advantage is that the emitter can be grounded directly, which is useful at high frequencies. It also lowers the stage’s gain and input impedance, a feedback effect treated later.

### Other stable schemes

Biasing the base from a split supply through a resistor to ground, biasing the emitter with a current source (Chapter 31), and matching a diode’s $V_{BE}$ drift with another junction in the divider are all used. They share a principle: **make the operating point depend on resistors and voltages, not on $\beta$ or $V_{BE}$.**

</section>

<section id="recipe">

<div className="section-heading"><span>06</span><h2>A biasing recipe</h2></div>

1. **Choose the quiescent current** from the needs of the stage: noise, speed, output impedance and drive capability argue for more; power and input impedance for less. 0.1–1 mA is typical for small-signal stages.
2. **Choose $V_E$** of about 1 V (more for very stable designs, less if supply voltage is scarce). Then $R_E$ = $V_E$/$I_C$.
3. **Choose $R_C$** for a common-emitter stage so that $V_C$ sits about halfway between $V_{CC}$ and $V_E$ plus a few tenths of a volt—or wherever the expected signal swing needs it to be.
4. **Design the divider** for $V_B$ = $V_E$ + 0.65 V with $R_{TH}$ ≤ ($\beta_{\mathrm{min}}$ + 1)$R_E$/10.
5. **Check the extremes**: $\beta$ from minimum to maximum, resistor tolerances, temperature range, and supply variation. Confirm the transistor stays active with the full signal swing superimposed.
6. **Choose coupling and bypass capacitors** for the lowest signal frequency using the impedances they see (Chapters 15 and 32).

Expect the exact current to differ from the nominal by several percent: $V_{BE}$ is not exactly 0.65 V, and the divider and emitter resistors have tolerances. A good bias design does not need it to be exact; it needs it to be predictable and to leave margin for the signal.

### Quantify the remaining β sensitivity

For the unsaturated divider stage, $I_C=\beta(V_{TH}-V_{BE})/[R_{TH}+(\beta+1)R_E]$. The useful measure is the fractional change in current per fractional change in $\beta$, $\partial\ln I_C/\partial\ln\beta = (\beta/I_C)\,\partial I_C/\partial\beta$. Take the logarithm, then differentiate with respect to $\beta$:

<Equation tex="\ln I_C=\ln\beta+\ln(V_{TH}-V_{BE})-\ln\left[R_{TH}+(\beta+1)R_E\right]"/>

<Equation tex="\frac{\partial\ln I_C}{\partial\beta}=\frac{1}{\beta}-\frac{R_E}{R_{TH}+(\beta+1)R_E}"/>

Multiply by $\beta$ and put both terms over a common denominator:

<Equation tex="\frac{\partial\ln I_C}{\partial\ln\beta}=\frac{R_{TH}+(\beta+1)R_E-\beta R_E}{R_{TH}+(\beta+1)R_E}=\frac{R_{TH}+R_E}{R_{TH}+(\beta+1)R_E}" />

For the chapter’s $R_{TH}=8.91$ kΩ, $R_E=1$ kΩ and β = 100, the sensitivity is 0.0902. A small 10% β change causes only about 0.90% current change locally. Making the divider ten times weaker raises the sensitivity to 0.474; a good voltage ratio does not by itself make a good bias source.

<div className="worked-example">

<span className="tiny-label">WORKED CHECK / COLLECTOR HEADROOM AT TWO EXTREMES</span><h3>Keep the stage active when it warms</h3>

<p>With the 82 kΩ / 10 kΩ divider, at β = 50 and 25 °C the fixed-drop model gives $I_C=0.818$ mA, $V_E=0.834$ V and $V_C=8.87$ V. At β = 500 and 85 °C it gives about 1.085 mA, 1.087 V and 6.86 V.</p>

<p>Both DC states are active, but their available output swings differ substantially. Resistor tolerances and supply extremes must be included before assigning a guaranteed signal swing. Bias stability means bounded, useful behavior across the specified conditions, not identical collector current.</p>

</div>

<div className="takeaway"><span className="tiny-label">THE IDEA TO KEEP</span><p>Set the base with a stiff divider and the current with an emitter resistor carrying about a volt. Then <em>$\beta$ and $V_{BE}$ barely matter</em>, which is the only safe way to depend on them.</p></div>

</section>

<section id="check">

<div className="section-heading"><span>07</span><h2>Check your understanding</h2></div>

<details>
<summary>1. Why use the divider’s Thévenin resistance as well as its voltage?</summary>
<div>

Base current causes a voltage drop through that resistance. An unloaded divider ratio alone cannot predict the operating point.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>2. How can a divider keep its ratio but become less stable?</summary>
<div>

Scale both resistors upward. The open-circuit voltage stays the same, but Thévenin resistance rises and base-current loading becomes more significant.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>3. Why does emitter resistance reduce current drift?</summary>
<div>

A current rise increases emitter voltage, reducing base–emitter voltage and opposing the original change.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>4. Does choosing a middle-of-supply collector voltage finish bias design?</summary>
<div>

No. Check β, temperature, resistor and supply extremes, then verify that the full signal swing remains within cutoff and saturation limits.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>5. What is the cost of a very stiff divider?</summary>
<div>

More standing current and lower signal input impedance. The divider can dominate the impedance seen by a weak source.

</div>
</details>
