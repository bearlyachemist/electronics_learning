<section id="field">

## 01 / A field controls charge

A resistor carries more current when the electric field along it increases. A field-effect transistor adds a second control: an electric field across the conducting path changes how much mobile charge that path contains. The gate changes the channel's conductance; the drain supply provides the energy delivered to the load. A gate with almost no steady current can therefore control a substantial drain current without violating energy conservation.

For an n-channel device, mobile electrons move predominantly from source toward drain. Conventional positive drain current points into the drain and out of the source. We define both terminal voltages relative to the source: gate-source voltage VGS and drain-source voltage VDS. A p-channel device reverses voltage and current polarities. Do not silently reuse n-channel numerical signs for it.

In drift transport, current density equals charge density times drift velocity, and velocity is approximately mobility times electric field. If a channel has width W and mobile sheet charge magnitude Qn, the current at a position x is:

<Equation tex="I_D=W|Q_n(x)|\,\mu_n\,\frac{dV(x)}{dx}" />

A large sheet charge means many carriers are available; large mobility means they move readily. The field across the channel controls the first quantity, while the field along it drives motion. This distinction is the physical core of the device.

</section>

<section id="families">

## 02 / Existing channels and induced channels

<FetStructure />

A **JFET** starts with an existing semiconductor channel. Its gate is a p–n junction. Reverse bias widens the depletion regions, leaving a narrower conducting path. For a conventional n-channel JFET, making the gate more negative reduces drain current. Zero gate-source voltage normally leaves the device on. The gate junction must usually remain reverse biased.

An **enhancement MOSFET** has an insulated gate. In the basic n-channel structure, the body is p type; sufficiently positive gate voltage attracts electrons to the surface and forms an inversion channel between n-type source and drain. A **depletion MOSFET** has a conducting channel at zero gate bias; negative gate voltage depletes it, while positive voltage can strengthen it. Its insulated gate distinguishes it from a JFET even when their transfer curves look similar.

The fourth MOS terminal is the body. In many discrete MOSFETs it is internally connected to the source; in integrated circuits it may be a shared substrate or well. A body connection is part of the circuit, not a drawing detail. The body junctions also create the intrinsic diode relevant to power switching.

A useful classification has two independent axes: n versus p channel, and enhancement versus depletion operation. “MOSFET” does not automatically mean normally off; “FET” does not automatically mean MOSFET.

</section>

<section id="derive">

## 03 / Derive the long-channel MOS equation

Take a long, uniform n-channel MOSFET above threshold, with body effects temporarily ignored. Let the channel potential measured from the source be V(x). The gate's effective attraction at that location is reduced by V(x). With oxide capacitance per area Cox and threshold VT:

<Equation tex="|Q_n(x)|=C_{ox}[V_{GS}-V_T-V(x)]" />

Define overdrive u = VGS − VT. Substitute the charge expression into the drift equation, separate variables and integrate from source to drain. Current is the same through each channel slice in steady state:

<Equation tex="I_D\int_0^L dx=\mu_n C_{ox}W\int_0^{V_{DS}}(u-V)\,dV" />
<Equation tex="I_D=k\left(uV_{DS}-\frac{V_{DS}^2}{2}\right),\qquad k=\mu_nC_{ox}\frac WL" />

Units provide a useful check: k has units A/V²; the bracket has units V². This is the **triode or ohmic-region equation**, valid while the channel exists all the way to the drain. At very small VDS, its quadratic term is negligible:

<Equation tex="I_D\approx kuV_{DS},\qquad R_{\mathrm{channel}}\approx\frac1{ku}" />

As drain voltage reaches u, the inversion charge at the drain end approaches zero. In the simplest model the current becomes:

<Equation tex="I_{D,\mathrm{sat}}=\frac{k}{2}(V_{GS}-V_T)^2,\qquad V_{DS}\ge V_{GS}-V_T" />

This derivation assumes gradual variation along a long channel, constant mobility, strong inversion and negligible series resistance. It is a foundation for reasoning, not an accurate universal fit to modern power MOSFETs.

</section>

<section id="regions">

## 04 / Pinch-off is not cutoff

**Cutoff** means insufficient gate bias for the modeled conducting channel. **Pinch-off at the drain end** means the drain-end charge has collapsed while carriers still travel through the channel and are swept through the high-field region. Current does not stop. Increasing drain voltage then produces much less current change than it did in the ohmic region.

FET **saturation** is the approximately current-source region used for voltage amplification. BJT saturation is the strongly driven state with both junctions forward biased, usually associated with switching. The same word describes different physics. A MOSFET used as a low-loss on-switch should generally be well into its ohmic region, not sitting at a large drain voltage in FET saturation.

For example, let k = 2 mA/V² and VT = 2 V. At VGS = 4 V, u = 2 V. At VDS = 0.1 V the current is 0.39 mA; at 1 V it is 3 mA; at 2 V it reaches 4 mA. The ideal model stays at 4 mA for larger drain voltage. A current of 4 mA does not identify the region by itself: both terminal voltages matter.

</section>

<section id="small">

## 05 / Linearize the curves

At a chosen bias point, small changes obey:

<Equation tex="i_d=g_m v_{gs}+g_{ds}v_{ds},\qquad g_m=\left.\frac{\partial I_D}{\partial V_{GS}}\right|_{V_{DS}},\qquad r_o=\frac1{g_{ds}}" />
<Equation tex="g_m=ku=\frac{2I_D}{u}=\sqrt{2kI_D}\quad\text{in ideal saturation}" />

The ideal flat drain curves imply infinite output resistance. Real curves slope because the effective channel changes with drain voltage, among other effects. A simple empirical extension multiplies the current by (1 + λVDS). Its exact local output conductance is λ times the unmodified current; the common estimate ro ≈ 1/(λID) assumes λVDS is small.

For ID = 2 mA and u = 1 V, gm = 4 mS: a 10 mV gate-source perturbation produces approximately 40 μA drain-current change. A 5 kΩ drain load converts that into about −0.2 V. The minus sign comes from the increased voltage drop across the load, not from a negative transconductance.

</section>

<section id="choose">

## 07 / What this model leaves out

Below threshold, real MOSFET current decreases approximately exponentially rather than becoming exactly zero. Near threshold, the square-law model is particularly unreliable. At large fields, mobility degradation and velocity saturation change the law; drain and source resistances add voltage drops. Leakage, breakdown and self-heating create additional limits.

A practical analysis has three layers. First establish DC voltages and the operating region. Then linearize around that point for gain, impedance and bandwidth. Finally compare excursions, dissipation and terminal stress with real device data. An elegant gain calculation cannot repair an invalid bias point.

The charge-control derivation follows the framework taught in [MIT's gradual-channel MOSFET lecture](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-012-microelectronic-devices-and-circuits-fall-2009/resources/mit6_012f09_lec11_gradual/). Subsequent chapters use deliberately simple parameters so each step can be checked by hand.

</section>
