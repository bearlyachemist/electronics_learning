## 01 / A high-side n-MOS gate must rise above its source

For a low-side switch, the source stays near ground. For a high-side n-channel device, the source rises toward the positive rail when the switch turns on. Keeping VGS at 10 V on a 24 V rail therefore requires a gate near 34 V relative to ground. A 10 V ground-referenced gate command is insufficient.

A floating high-side driver can obtain this voltage from an isolated supply, charge pump or bootstrap capacitor. A bootstrap charges when the switching node is low and then rides upward with it. Its voltage droops as it supplies gate charge and driver current:

<Equation tex="\Delta V_{\mathrm{boot}}\approx\frac{Q_g+I_{\mathrm{driver}}t_{\mathrm{on}}+Q_{\mathrm{other}}}{C_{\mathrm{boot}}}" />

For Qg = 30 nC and 20 nC of other demand, keeping ideal droop under 0.5 V requires at least 100 nF. Real selection needs margin for capacitor tolerance, DC-bias derating, leakage and recharge conditions. A basic bootstrap cannot sustain indefinite 100% duty without an opportunity to recharge.

A p-channel high-side device turns on when its gate is pulled below its source. This simplifies some low-frequency circuits, but a ground pull-down from a high supply can exceed the gate-source rating. A clamp and current-limiting path can set a safe gate swing. Its resistance and charge still have to meet the application, rather than being inferred from the simpler drive.

</section>

<section id="bridge">

## 02 / A half bridge has more than two current paths

A half bridge places one switch from the positive rail to the switching node and another from that node to the negative rail. An inductive load can keep current flowing while either command changes. Depending on current direction, a body diode or enhanced reverse channel provides the freewheel path.

If both switches conduct simultaneously, they short the rails. Dead time deliberately leaves both off during transitions. Too little invites shoot-through; too much forces extra diode conduction and can distort an inverter waveform. Propagation-delay mismatch, gate discharge and Miller-induced turn-on all affect the necessary interval.

For current I, diode forward drop VF, two equal dead intervals td per cycle and frequency fs, a first estimate is:

<Equation tex="P_{\mathrm{dead}}\approx2V_F I t_d f_s" />

At 0.8 V, 5 A, 100 ns and 100 kHz, that is 0.08 W. Reverse recovery can add further stress when the opposite switch turns on. This estimate applies only when the stated current flows in the relevant diode during both intervals; actual modulation and current direction determine the result.

An H bridge uses two half bridges to apply either polarity across a load. The inductive current's direction can differ from the commanded voltage, so analyze all four quadrants rather than assuming energy always travels from supply to load.

</section>

<section id="buck">

## 03 / Derive a buck converter's first design numbers

In an ideal continuous-current buck, the high-side switch connects VIN to the inductor for fraction D of each cycle. During the remaining interval, a diode or synchronous low-side MOSFET provides the return path. Steady-state inductor volt-seconds sum to zero:

<Equation tex="D(V_{\mathrm{in}}-V_o)+(1-D)(-V_o)=0,\qquad V_o=DV_{\mathrm{in}}" />

For 24 V to 12 V, D = 0.5. At 100 kHz with L = 100 μH, on-time is 5 μs and ripple is:

<Equation tex="\Delta I_L=\frac{(V_{\mathrm{in}}-V_o)D}{Lf_s}=0.60\ \mathrm A" />

At a 5 A average load, current runs from 4.7 to 5.3 A, so continuous-current operation is consistent. Each switch's conduction calculation uses its own interval and hot resistance. For a 20 mΩ high-side device:

<Equation tex="P_H\approx R_H D\left(I_o^2+\frac{\Delta I_L^2}{12}\right)=0.2503\ \mathrm W" />

A similar low-side device contributes about the same conduction loss in this example. Switching overlap, driver losses, diode intervals, inductor copper/core loss and capacitor ESR remain additional terms.

Ignoring ESR, a first output-ripple estimate is ΔVo ≈ ΔIL/(8fsC). With C = 100 μF it is 7.5 mV peak to peak. An ESR of 20 mΩ adds approximately 12 mV peak to peak from the triangular ripple current. Their waveforms differ, so simple addition is a conservative rough estimate rather than a universal exact sum.

This is the power-stage operating-point calculation. A regulated converter also needs a feedback-loop design, startup strategy, current limiting and a layout suitable for its switching currents. Duty alone does not guarantee a regulated output under changing input and load.

</section>

## 04 / Reverse blocking and controlled inrush

A back-to-back MOSFET pair can interrupt current in either direction because its intrinsic diodes oppose one another. It can serve as a disconnect or be controlled to act like an ideal-diode path. The orientation and control law determine whether the circuit prevents reverse polarity, reverse current, or both; those functions are not interchangeable.

<BackToBack />

During connection to an uncharged capacitor, the pass MOSFET initially sees both substantial voltage and charging current. With constant current I into C, charging time to VSUPPLY is CVS/I. Integrating MOSFET dissipation gives:

<Equation tex="E_{\mathrm{pass}}=\int_0^{CV/I}(V-It/C)I\,dt=\frac12CV^2" />

For 1000 μF charged to 24 V at 1 A, charging takes 24 ms. The capacitor stores 0.288 J, and the ideal linear pass device also dissipates 0.288 J. Initial device power is 24 W, falling linearly toward zero. Reducing current stretches the event and lowers peak power but does not change this ideal total energy.

If there is a load during startup, it adds pass-device stress and may prevent charging. Choose the device from the appropriate transient SOA, not solely its continuous current rating. A current limiter that protects the downstream circuit can overheat its own pass transistor during a persistent short.

</section>

<section id="igbt">

## 05 / The IGBT combines a MOS gate with bipolar conduction

An insulated-gate bipolar transistor uses a MOS-controlled channel to enable carrier injection into a voltage-supporting region. Minority carriers increase conductivity in that region, reducing its resistance when conducting. This conductivity modulation is particularly useful for some high-voltage, high-current operating points.

The stored carriers must be removed when the device turns off. A current tail can persist after the gate has been discharged, causing loss while substantial collector-emitter voltage is present. A MOS gate therefore does not make an IGBT's turn-off identical to a majority-carrier MOSFET's.

An IGBT's on-state is often approximated locally by a voltage intercept plus resistance:

<Equation tex="V_{CE}\approx V_0+rI,\qquad P_{\mathrm{cond}}\approx D(V_0I+rI^2)" />

For rough calculations at a specified current, a given VCE(sat) can be used as a fixed drop. Switching energy is often more useful than edge-time estimates:

<Equation tex="P_{\mathrm{sw}}=(E_{\mathrm{on}}+E_{\mathrm{off}})f_s" />

Use energies at the relevant bus voltage, current, gate drive and temperature. Tail current, diode recovery and test-circuit details influence the values. An ordinary IGBT does not provide the same useful intrinsic antiparallel diode behavior as a conventional power MOSFET; modules and discrete parts may include a separate diode, and reverse-conducting variants differ.

[Toshiba's MOSFET–IGBT comparison](https://toshiba.semicon-storage.com/us/semiconductor/knowledge/faq/mosfet_igbt/igbt-002.html) connects the difference in carrier transport to conduction voltage and switching behavior.

</section>

<section id="choose">

## 06 / Compare losses at an actual operating point

Consider deliberately hypothetical alternatives at 10 A and 50% duty. A MOSFET with hot resistance 0.15 Ω dissipates 7.5 W in conduction. An IGBT with 1.8 V on-state drop dissipates 9 W. If their total switching energies per cycle are respectively 0.2 mJ and 0.6 mJ, at 20 kHz switching losses are 4 W and 12 W. Totals are 11.5 W and 21 W before other terms.

At 30 A with the same simplified resistance and voltage-drop assumptions, conduction becomes 67.5 W versus 27 W. But switching energies also change with current; reusing the 10 A energies would invalidate a final comparison. The example explains a crossover mechanism, not a recommendation for real parts.

MOSFETs, IGBTs and newer power technologies have overlapping application spaces. Voltage rating alone does not select a winner. Compare the actual conduction waveform, switching energy, diode behavior, thermal path, gate requirements and fault conditions at the intended operating point.

</section>
