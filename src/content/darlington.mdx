<div className="opening-grid">

<div><span className="tiny-label">A PRACTICAL QUESTION</span><p className="opening-question">A power transistor with $\beta$ = 50 must switch 1 A, but the controller can supply only a milliamp or two. Where does the extra current gain come from?</p></div>

<div>

Power transistors have low $\beta$, often 20–80, so they need large base currents. The obvious fix is to drive the power transistor’s base from another transistor’s emitter: a follower driving a follower. The pair behaves like a single transistor whose current gain is roughly the product of the two.

That combination, the **Darlington connection**, is everywhere: in power switches, in output stages, in arrays that drive relays and solenoids from logic. Its complementary cousin, the **Sziklai pair**, offers the same gain with different tradeoffs. Neither is free: both cost a base–emitter drop that a single transistor does not pay.

</div>

</div>

<section id="connection">

<div className="section-heading"><span>01</span><h2>The connection and its current gain</h2></div>

<CompoundPairsFigure/>

Connect the emitter of $Q_1$ to the base of $Q_2$ and tie the two collectors together. The composite has a base ($Q_1$’s), an emitter ($Q_2$’s) and a collector (both). Follow a base current $I_B$. KCL at $Q_1$ says its emitter current is its base current plus its collector current, $(\beta_1+1)I_B$, and all of it becomes $Q_2$'s base current. KCL at the shared collector node then adds the two collector currents:

<Equation tex="I_{E1}=(\beta_1+1)I_B=I_{B2},\qquad I_C=\beta_1I_B+\beta_2(\beta_1+1)I_B\quad\Rightarrow\quad \beta_{\mathrm{Darl}}=\beta_1\beta_2+\beta_1+\beta_2\approx\beta_1\beta_2" label="DARLINGTON CURRENT GAIN"/>

With $\beta$₁ = 150 and $\beta$₂ = 50 the composite gain is 7700; the lab’s Ebers–Moll model gives exactly that in the active region. As a follower, the pair presents an input impedance of about $\beta_1\beta_2$ times its emitter load. Apply Chapter 28's result twice: $Q_2$'s base looks like $(\beta_2+1)R_L$, and that is $Q_1$'s emitter load, so $Q_1$'s base looks like $(\beta_1+1)(\beta_2+1)R_L$. With the betas above, 8 Ω looks like 61 kΩ.

The composite’s base–emitter voltage is the sum of two: **$V_{BE}$ ≈ 1.2–1.5 V**. In the lab, switching 1 A takes about 1.45 V between base and emitter, because the power transistor runs at high current.

</section>

<section id="costs">

<div className="section-heading"><span>02</span><h2>What it costs</h2></div>

### Saturation voltage

$Q_2$ can saturate only if its base is held above its emitter by $V_{BE2}$, and its base is $Q_1$’s emitter. $Q_1$ cannot pull its own emitter above its collector by more than $V_{CE(\mathrm{sat})}$. Write KVL from the composite collector down through $Q_1$ (collector to emitter) and then through $Q_2$'s base–emitter junction to the composite emitter: $V_{CE} = V_{CE1} + V_{BE2}$. The smallest $V_{CE1}$ can be is $V_{CE(\mathrm{sat})1}$, so the composite’s collector cannot fall below

<Equation tex="V_{CE,\min}\approx V_{BE2}+V_{CE(\mathrm{sat})1}\approx0.7\text{–}1\ \mathrm V"/>

In the lab, the Darlington bottoms out at 0.76 V at about 1 A, against 0.09 V for a hard-driven single transistor. At 1 A that is 0.71 W versus 0.09 W of dissipation: a heat sink versus none. In a battery-powered circuit, the lost 0.7 V may matter as much as the heat.

### Speed

When a Darlington turns off, $Q_1$ stops conducting first, and $Q_2$’s base is left with no path to remove its stored charge except through leakage. Turn-off can take many microseconds. A **bleeder resistor** from $Q_2$’s base to emitter (a few hundred ohms for power devices, often built into Darlington packages) provides the path. It also stops $Q_1$’s leakage current, multiplied by $\beta$₂, from partly turning on $Q_2$ when hot.

### Leakage and temperature

$Q_1$’s collector-to-base leakage is amplified by both transistors, and the two $V_{BE}$ values both fall about 2 mV/°C, so the composite’s $V_{BE}$ falls about 4 mV/°C—twice the thermal sensitivity of a single transistor in bias circuits.

</section>

<section id="sziklai">

<div className="section-heading"><span>03</span><h2>The Sziklai complementary pair</h2></div>

The **Sziklai** or **complementary-feedback pair** uses an NPN driver $Q_1$ and a PNP output $Q_2$. $Q_1$’s collector pulls down on $Q_2$’s base; $Q_2$’s emitter is the composite’s collector terminal, and $Q_2$’s collector returns to $Q_1$’s emitter, the composite’s emitter. The pair behaves as a single NPN transistor:

- **Current gain** ≈ $\beta_1\beta_2$, as for the Darlington: $Q_1$’s collector current $\beta_1I_B$ is $Q_2$’s base current, so $Q_2$ carries $\beta_2\beta_1I_B$. KCL at the composite emitter (where $Q_1$'s emitter and $Q_2$'s collector meet) gives $I_E = (\beta_1+1)I_B + \beta_1\beta_2I_B$, and the composite collector current is $Q_2$'s emitter current, $\beta_1(\beta_2+1)I_B$. The composite gain is therefore $\beta_1(\beta_2+1) \approx \beta_1\beta_2$.
- **Input $V_{BE}$ is one junction** (about 0.74 V in the lab), because only $Q_1$’s base–emitter is between the input and output terminals. The pair is also more thermally stable: only $Q_1$’s $V_{BE}$ sets the input voltage, and $Q_1$ is a small transistor that does not heat itself.
- **Saturation is no better.** KVL from the composite collector ($Q_2$'s emitter) to the composite emitter, through $Q_2$'s emitter–base junction and then $Q_1$'s collector–emitter, gives $V_{CE} = V_{EB2} + V_{CE1}$. The output can fall only to $Q_2$’s emitter–base voltage plus $Q_1$’s saturation voltage—about 0.76 V in the lab, the same as the Darlington. A common belief that the Sziklai pair “saturates like a single transistor” is not correct.

The Sziklai pair also has an internal feedback loop through $Q_2$, which makes it faster to respond but more prone to oscillation; small capacitors or resistors are sometimes needed to keep it stable. It is popular in audio output stages, where an NPN Darlington on the upper side and a PNP-equivalent Sziklai pair (PNP driver, NPN power output) on the lower side formed a “quasi-complementary” stage using NPN power transistors on both sides.

</section>

<section id="uses">

<div className="section-heading"><span>05</span><h2>Uses</h2></div>

**High-impedance followers.** A Darlington follower presents $\beta_1\beta_2$ times its load at its input. That lets a weak source drive a loudspeaker or motor, and it is the standard driver in push–pull output stages (Chapter 37).

**Logic-driven loads.** Darlington arrays, such as the ULN2003 family of seven channels with built-in flyback diodes, switch relays, solenoids, lamps and stepper motors directly from logic outputs. Their ~1 V on-voltage is acceptable at a few hundred milliamps.

**Current sources and regulators.** A Darlington pass transistor reduces the base current a regulator’s control circuit must provide. The extra $V_{BE}$ raises the regulator’s dropout voltage; low-dropout regulators use a PNP or MOSFET pass device instead.

**Alternatives.** Superbeta transistors ($\beta$ of thousands at low current) serve low-current applications. At higher currents, a logic-level MOSFET often beats both: no steady gate current, and an on-voltage set by I × $R_{DS(\mathrm{on})}$ that can be millivolts. MOSFETs come later in the course.

</section>

<section id="design">

<div className="section-heading"><span>06</span><h2>A design example</h2></div>

<div className="worked-example">

<span className="tiny-label">WORKED EXAMPLE / A SOLENOID FROM A 3.3 V PIN, REVISITED</span><h3>24 V, 250 mA; the pin can supply 8 mA</h3>

<p>Chapter 27’s practice problem found that a single transistor would need 25 mA of base drive. With a Darlington whose worst-case composite gain is 1000, the minimum base current is 0.25 mA; with fourfold overdrive, <strong>1 mA</strong>.</p>

<p>The input needs about 1.4 V, so $R_B$ = (3.3 − 1.4) V/1 mA ≈ <strong>1.8 kΩ</strong>. Add a bleeder of a few kilohms on $Q_1$’s base if the logic pin can float, and keep the flyback diode across the solenoid.</p>

<p>On-voltage is about 0.8 V, so the solenoid sees 23.2 V and the Darlington dissipates 0.8 × 0.25 = <strong>0.2 W</strong>, a power level whose acceptability depends on package thermal resistance and ambient temperature. A MOSFET with 0.1 Ω on-resistance would dissipate 6 mW, but needs an on-resistance specified at a gate drive no greater than 3.3 V; threshold voltage alone does not establish that.</p>

</div>

### Give stored charge a way out

A floating base does not guarantee a fast off state. A base–emitter bleeder on the power transistor drains leakage and stored charge, but also takes part of the driver current while on. At a 0.7 V junction voltage, a 1 kΩ bleeder consumes 0.7 mA. If the power transistor needs 5 mA of base drive, its driver must now supply about 5.7 mA.

A first estimate of charge-removal time is $t\approx Q_{stored}/I_{reverse}$. For an illustrative 20 nC and 2 mA reverse base current, the charge-removal contribution is 10 µs. This is a charge budget, not a universal transistor delay: saturation depth, previous collector current and the drive circuit determine the stored charge.

<div className="worked-example">

<span className="tiny-label">WORKED EXAMPLE / CHECK THE ENTIRE INPUT BUDGET</span><h3>One milliamp from a 3.3 V output</h3>

<p>If two base–emitter drops total 1.4 V and the logic output may fall to 3.0 V under load, a 1.8 kΩ resistor supplies only $(3.0-1.4)/1800=0.889$ mA. To guarantee 1 mA under those assumptions, choose at most 1.6 kΩ, then check the maximum current at the highest logic voltage and lowest junction drops.</p>

<p>Use the composite device’s specified collector-current and saturation-voltage conditions to choose base drive. Multiplying typical small-signal β values does not guarantee saturated switching. Finally check on-state power, pulse duration, package thermal resistance and the turn-off clamp.</p>

</div>

<div className="takeaway"><span className="tiny-label">THE IDEA TO KEEP</span><p>A compound pair multiplies $\beta$, so a weak drive controls a heavy load. Both the Darlington and the Sziklai pay for it with <em>an extra base–emitter drop in their on-voltage</em>; the Sziklai saves the drop at the input.</p></div>

<section id="check">

<div className="section-heading"><span>07</span><h2>Check your understanding</h2></div>

<details>
<summary>1. Which current drives the second Darlington transistor?</summary>
<div>

The first transistor’s emitter current, which is its collector current plus its base current.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>2. Why can a Darlington have a larger on-voltage than a single saturated transistor?</summary>
<div>

The power transistor’s base needs one forward junction drop, and the driver still needs collector–emitter headroom above it.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>3. Does this Sziklai connection put the PNP collector at the load?</summary>
<div>

No. The PNP emitter is at the composite collector/load port; its collector returns to the NPN driver emitter at ground.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>4. What tradeoff does an output base–emitter bleeder introduce?</summary>
<div>

It improves leakage control and charge removal, but diverts some on-state driver current away from the power transistor’s base.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>5. Is a large typical composite β enough to choose a switch’s base resistor?</summary>
<div>

No. Use guaranteed drive and saturation conditions at the intended current and temperature, then budget logic-output voltage and resistor tolerance.

</div>
</details>
