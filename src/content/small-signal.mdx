<section id="slope">

<div className="section-heading"><span>01</span><h2>The resistance of a change</h2></div>

For a linear resistor, the graph of current against voltage is a straight line through the origin. The ratio $V/I$ is the same everywhere, and any change obeys $\Delta V = R\,\Delta I$.

For a nonlinear device, the graph curves. The ratio from the origin to a point on that curve need not equal the slope of the tangent at that point.

Choose a DC operating point, or **bias point**, $Q = (V_Q, I_Q)$. Separate the total quantities into their bias values and small variations:

<Equation tex="V(t)=V_Q+v(t),\qquad I(t)=I_Q+i(t)" label="01 / TOTAL = BIAS + VARIATION"/>

Here uppercase symbols identify DC values and lowercase symbols identify variations. A lowercase current can be negative: it then means less current than the bias value, not necessarily a reversal of the total current.

If $I = F(V)$, expand around $V_Q$:

<Equation tex="I_Q+i=F(V_Q)+F'(V_Q)v+\frac{1}{2}F''(V_Q)v^2+\cdots"/>

Because $F(V_Q) = I_Q$, the bias terms cancel. If the higher-order terms are negligible, the remaining relation is linear:

<Equation tex="i\approx g_d v,\qquad g_d=\left.\frac{dI}{dV}\right|_Q"/>
<Equation tex="r_d=\frac{1}{g_d}=\left.\frac{dV}{dI}\right|_Q" label="02 / DIFFERENTIAL CONDUCTANCE AND RESISTANCE"/>

The reciprocal relation assumes a finite nonzero slope that can be locally inverted. Conductance has units of amperes per volt, or siemens; resistance has units of volts per ampere, or ohms.

<details className="prerequisite">
<summary>If derivatives are new: think of a shrinking measurement</summary>
<div>

<p>Measure a voltage and current, move a little along the curve, and measure again. Divide the change in voltage by the change in current. This gives the slope of a short chord.</p>
<p>Make the change smaller and the chord approaches a tangent. The derivative is the limiting slope. We use it because a short section of a smooth curve can be approximated by a straight line.</p>
<p>This is a local approximation, not a claim that the entire device obeys Ohm’s law with one fixed resistor value.</p>

</div>
</details>

### Three quantities that are easy to confuse

<table><thead><tr><th>Quantity</th><th>Meaning</th><th>Use</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>$V_Q/I_Q$</td><td>DC ratio from the origin</td><td>Describe one operating point</td></tr><tr><td>$\Delta V/\Delta I$</td><td>Finite chord between points</td><td>Describe a measured change</td></tr><tr><td>$dV/dI$ at Q</td><td>Local tangent resistance</td><td>Predict sufficiently small changes</td></tr></tbody></table>

The bias must be found first. A differential resistance is a property of a device **at an operating point**, not a replacement for calculating that operating point.

### Why this matters beyond diodes

Almost every useful electronic device is nonlinear: diodes, transistors, lamps, thermistors, batteries. Analyzing nonlinear circuits exactly is hard, but analyzing linear ones is easy, and we already have the tools (dividers, Thévenin). Small-signal modeling is the trade that lets us use them: solve the nonlinear circuit once, for its DC bias; then treat each device as a linear element for signals that stay close to that bias. Every amplifier chapter in this book follows this two-step plan.

</section>

<section id="diode">

<div className="section-heading"><span>02</span><h2>Derive the diode model</h2></div>

Use a simplified diode with fixed temperature, negligible series resistance, and no capacitive effects. Its current is modeled by:

<Equation tex="I=I_S\left(e^{V/(nV_T)}-1\right),\qquad V_T=\frac{kT}{q}"/>

$I_S$ is the saturation-current parameter; $n$ is the ideality factor; $V_T$ is the thermal voltage. At approximately 300 K, $V_T$ is 25.85 mV. Here $k$ is Boltzmann’s constant, $q$ is the magnitude of the electron charge, and $T$ is absolute temperature in kelvin. The thermal voltage is a physical scale, not the diode’s forward voltage.

Differentiate the exponential, then express the result in terms of current:

<Equation tex="\frac{dI}{dV}=\frac{I_S}{nV_T}e^{V/(nV_T)}=\frac{I+I_S}{nV_T}"/>

At the chosen bias point:

<Equation tex="r_d=\frac{nV_T}{I_Q+I_S}\approx\frac{nV_T}{I_Q}\quad\text{when }I_Q\gg I_S" label="03 / MORE BIAS CURRENT → LESS INCREMENTAL RESISTANCE"/>

For $n = 1$ and $I_Q = 1$ mA, $r_d \approx 25.85\ \Omega$. At 0.1 mA, it is 258.5 Ω. At 10 mA, it is 2.585 Ω, within this model.

Notice what the result does *not* contain: $I_S$, which varies by orders of magnitude between diode types, has dropped out. Every junction that obeys the exponential law has the same small-signal resistance at the same current. That universality is what makes transistor amplifiers predictable (Chapter 30).

<ResistanceVsBiasFigure/>

A real diode can depart from this prediction because $n$ changes with operating region, series resistance adds a voltage drop, temperature changes, or stored charge matters. We are building a useful model with a stated domain, not a universal description of a particular part.

<div className="worked-example">

<span className="tiny-label">WORKED EXAMPLE / TWO DIFFERENT RATIOS</span><h3>A 1 mA bias point</h3>
<p>Take $I_S = 10^{-12}$ A, $n = 1$, and $V_T = 25.85$ mV. Inverting the model gives $V_Q = V_T\ln(1 + I_Q/I_S) \approx$ <strong>0.5357 V</strong>.</p>
<p>The DC ratio $V_Q/I_Q$ is approximately <strong>535.7 Ω</strong>. The small-signal resistance is approximately <strong>25.85 Ω</strong>.</p>
<p>A +10 µA current change produces a linear voltage estimate of $25.85\ \Omega \times 10\ \mu\text{A} =$ <strong>0.2585 mV</strong>. The exact model gives $V_T\ln(1.01) \approx$ <strong>0.2572 mV</strong>, with $I_S$ negligible compared with the bias.</p>
<p>Using the DC ratio would predict 5.357 mV, over twenty times too much. It uses a line from the origin where we needed the tangent at Q.</p>

</div>

</section>

<section id="explore">

<div className="section-heading"><span>03</span><h2>Explore the operating point</h2></div>

The source in this figure sets the diode voltage directly. Changing the bias-current control chooses a new $I_Q$ and computes the voltage needed to produce it. The perturbation control then adds a small voltage around that bias.

The **incremental circuit** removes the DC bias and replaces the diode by $r_d$. Its source is only the added voltage $v$. The linear current from that circuit is a change $i$; add $I_Q$ back to obtain a prediction for total current.

<p className="caption">Fixed 300 K model with $n = 1$ and $I_S = 10^{-12}$ A. The tangent is exact at Q and becomes less accurate away from it. This lab does not model self-heating.</p>

### The tangent does not pass through the origin

Written in total quantities, the tangent model is $I \approx I_Q + (V - V_Q)/r_d$. Written in variations, it is simply $i \approx v/r_d$. These are the same relation in different coordinates.

A resistor alone cannot replace the diode for both DC bias and small changes. In a total-voltage representation, the tangent requires an offset source as well. In the incremental representation, subtracting the bias hides that offset cleanly.

</section>

<section id="circuit">

<div className="section-heading"><span>04</span><h2>Put the local model in a circuit</h2></div>

Consider a diode from a node to ground, with a source feeding that node through a 1 kΩ resistor. Choose the source’s DC value to establish $I_Q = 1$ mA. Using our model, the source must provide about 0.5357 V across the diode plus 1 V across the resistor: **1.5357 V**.

Now add a small signal $v_s$ to the source. For the incremental circuit, a constant ideal DC voltage source becomes zero volts, while the signal source remains. Replace the diode by its resistance at the established bias point.

<Equation tex="v_d\approx v_s\frac{r_d}{R_S+r_d},\qquad i\approx\frac{v_s}{R_S+r_d}"/>

With $r_d = 25.85\ \Omega$ and $v_s = 10$ mV, the diode-voltage change is about **0.2520 mV** and the current change is about **9.748 µA**. Most of the source’s small voltage change appears across the 1 kΩ resistor.

<IncrementalFigure/>

This is a voltage divider again, but one resistance comes from the slope of a nonlinear device. The signal is small **at the diode** even though the source perturbation is much larger.

<details className="derivation">
<summary>Check the approximation against the nonlinear circuit</summary>
<div>

<p>Subtract the original DC equation from the new circuit equation. Neglecting $I_S$ relative to $I_Q$ gives:</p>
<Equation tex="v_s=R_S i+nV_T\ln\!\left(1+\frac{i}{I_Q}\right)"/>
<p>For $i/I_Q$ much smaller than one, $\ln(1 + i/I_Q) \approx i/I_Q$. The circuit equation becomes $v_s \approx i(R_S + nV_T/I_Q)$, reproducing the incremental result.</p>
<p>For the 10 mV example, solving the nonlinear expression gives approximately 9.749 µA. The linear answer differs by about 0.012%. The series resistance limits the change at the nonlinear junction.</p>

</div>
</details>

### From a diode to an amplifier

For a bipolar transistor in an appropriate forward-active model, collector current depends exponentially on base–emitter voltage. Differentiation gives transconductance $g_m \approx I_C/V_T$.

Transconductance relates a voltage at one port to a current at another; it is **not** automatically a two-terminal resistance. Nevertheless, the same method applies: solve the bias, differentiate the device relationships, then analyze the resulting linear circuit.

Feedback can change the resistance seen at a port. A resistor-like small-signal model may describe an entire active circuit, even when no physical resistor of that value exists.



<h3 id="book-notes">A zener reference has a finite incremental stiffness</h3>

A zener may have 5 V across it while its small-signal resistance is only 20 Ω. The ratio $V/I$ describes the operating point; $r_z=dV/dI$ describes movement around it. Model a reference as its DC voltage plus this tangent resistance, supplied through a 1 kΩ resistor.

For a small supply perturbation, the ideal DC voltage source disappears from the incremental circuit. The resulting divider passes $r_z/(R+r_z)$ of the perturbation: $20/1020=0.01961$, so 100 mV of supply ripple becomes about **1.96 mV** of reference ripple. A 100 µA increase in external load current produces an additional output decrease of $(1\,\mathrm{k}\Omega\parallel20\ \Omega)\times100\ \mu\text{A}=1.96$ mV.

These predictions require the zener to remain in its intended breakdown-current range. If the load takes nearly all the resistor current, the operating point leaves the stiff part of the curve; the original 20 Ω tangent is no longer useful. Low incremental resistance also does not imply low noise or low temperature drift. Those are separate reference specifications, and a capacitor suppresses AC variation while leaving long-term drift.

<section id="limits">

<div className="section-heading"><span>05</span><h2>How small is “small”?</h2></div>

For our diode, let $x = v/(nV_T)$. The exact current change around Q is:

<Equation tex="\Delta I=(I_Q+I_S)(e^x-1)=(I_Q+I_S)\left(x+\frac{x^2}{2}+\cdots\right)"/>

The linear model keeps only $x$. The ratio of the quadratic term to the linear term has magnitude $|x|/2$. Requiring that ratio to be at most about 1% suggests $|v| \lesssim 0.02\,nV_T$, or roughly **0.52 mV** when $n = 1$ at 300 K. That is an estimate for the error in the current change, not a universal signal limit.

</div>

<LinearizationErrorFigure/>

These errors compare the linear estimate with the exact exponential current **change**. Error relative to total current is a different number. State the denominator whenever quoting an approximation error.

<DistortionFigure/>

The waveform shows what curvature does to a signal over time. The positive half-cycle grows and the negative half shrinks, so the average current rises above $I_Q$ and the shape acquires harmonics. Amplifiers built on exponential devices therefore rely on keeping the signal at the junction small, or on feedback (Chapter 42) to straighten the overall response.

### Small-signal does not mean high-frequency

Small describes amplitude. A slowly varying perturbation can be too large to linearize; a tiny high-frequency signal can require capacitive or dynamic terms. Differential resistance alone is a low-frequency or quasi-static approximation.

Temperature is another state variable. If a perturbation changes dissipation enough to heat the device, a slowly measured slope may include thermal feedback. It need not match the fixed-temperature derivative used here.

<section id="check">

<div className="section-heading"><span>06</span><h2>Check your understanding</h2></div>

<details>
<summary>1. A diode's bias current doubles. What happens to $r_d$, and to $V_Q$?</summary>
<div>

$r_d = V_T/I_Q$ halves. $V_Q$ rises by only $V_T\ln 2 \approx 18$ mV. A large relative change in current needs only a small change in voltage; that is the exponential at work, and it is why $r_d$ is small.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>2. Why can't you use $r_d$ to find the bias point?</summary>
<div>

$r_d$ depends on the bias current, so you need the bias before you can compute it. It also describes only changes around Q; it says nothing about the total voltage there. Solve the nonlinear DC circuit first, then linearize.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>3. In the incremental circuit, what happens to a 5 V DC supply? To a DC current source?</summary>
<div>

Both are constant, so their variations are zero. The voltage source becomes a short (zero volts change) and the current source becomes an open (zero current change). Only the sources that carry the signal remain.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>4. A 1 kΩ resistor feeds a diode biased at 1 mA. Is a 50 mV signal at the source "small"?</summary>
<div>

Check at the diode, not at the source. The divider puts $50 \times 25.85/1025.85 \approx 1.26$ mV across the junction, giving about 2.5% error in the current change: small enough for many purposes. The same 50 mV applied directly to the diode would be wildly nonlinear.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>5. Two different diodes (different $I_S$) are biased at the same current. Do they have the same $r_d$?</summary>
<div>

Yes, if both have $n = 1$ and the same temperature: $r_d = V_T/I_Q$ does not involve $I_S$. Their bias voltages differ, but their slopes at equal current are equal.

</div>
</details>
