<section id="slope">




<div className="section-heading">

<span>01</span><h2>The resistance of a change</h2>

</div>



For a linear resistor, the graph of current against voltage is a straight line through the origin. The ratio V/I is the same everywhere, and any change obeys ΔV = RΔI.

For a nonlinear device, the graph curves. The ratio from the origin to a point on that curve need not equal the slope of the tangent at that point.

Choose a DC operating point, or **bias point**, Q = (VQ, IQ). Separate the total quantities into their bias values and small variations:

<Equation tex="V(t)=V_Q+v(t),\qquad I(t)=I_Q+i(t)" label="01 / TOTAL = BIAS + VARIATION"/>

Here uppercase symbols identify DC values and lowercase symbols identify variations. A lowercase current can be negative: it then means less current than the bias value, not necessarily a reversal of the total current.

If I = F(V), expand around VQ:

<Equation tex="I_Q+i=F(V_Q)+F'(V_Q)v+\frac{1}{2}F''(V_Q)v^2+\cdots"/>

Because F(VQ) = IQ, the bias terms cancel. If the higher-order terms are negligible, the remaining relation is linear:

<Equation tex="i\approx g_d v,\qquad g_d=\left.\frac{dI}{dV}\right|_Q"/>
<Equation tex="r_d=\frac{1}{g_d}=\left.\frac{dV}{dI}\right|_Q" label="02 / DIFFERENTIAL CONDUCTANCE AND RESISTANCE"/>

The reciprocal relation assumes a finite nonzero slope that can be locally inverted. Conductance has units of amperes per volt, or siemens; resistance has units of volts per ampere, or ohms.



<details className="prerequisite">


<summary>If derivatives are new: think of a shrinking measurement</summary>


<div>


<p>Measure a voltage and current, move a little along the curve, and measure again. Divide the change in voltage by the change in current. This gives the slope of a short chord.</p>
<p>Make the change smaller and the chord approaches a tangent. The derivative is the limiting slope. We use it because a short section of a smooth curve can be approximated by a straight line.</p>
<p>This is a local approximation, not a claim that the entire device obeys Ohm’s law with one fixed resistor value.</p>


</div>




</details>



### Three quantities that are easy to confuse



<div className="table-wrap">

<table><thead><tr><th>Quantity</th><th>Meaning</th><th>Use</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>VQ / IQ</td><td>DC ratio from the origin</td><td>Describe one operating point</td></tr><tr><td>ΔV / ΔI</td><td>Finite chord between points</td><td>Describe a measured change</td></tr><tr><td>dV / dI at Q</td><td>Local tangent resistance</td><td>Predict sufficiently small changes</td></tr></tbody></table>

</div>



The bias must be found first. A differential resistance is a property of a device **at an operating point**, not a replacement for calculating that operating point.


</section>





<section id="diode">




<div className="section-heading">

<span>02</span><h2>Derive the diode model</h2>

</div>



Use a simplified diode with fixed temperature, negligible series resistance, and no capacitive effects. Its current is modeled by:

<Equation tex="I=I_S\left(e^{V/(nV_T)}-1\right),\qquad V_T=\frac{kT}{q}"/>

Iₛ is the saturation-current parameter; n is the ideality factor; Vₜ is the thermal voltage. At approximately 300 K, Vₜ is 25.85 mV. Here k is Boltzmann’s constant, q is the magnitude of the electron charge, and T is absolute temperature in kelvin. The thermal voltage is a physical scale, not the diode’s forward voltage.

Differentiate the exponential, then express the result in terms of current:

<Equation tex="\frac{dI}{dV}=\frac{I_S}{nV_T}e^{V/(nV_T)}=\frac{I+I_S}{nV_T}"/>

At the chosen bias point:

<Equation tex="r_d=\frac{nV_T}{I_Q+I_S}\approx\frac{nV_T}{I_Q}\quad\text{when }I_Q\gg I_S" label="03 / MORE BIAS CURRENT → LESS INCREMENTAL RESISTANCE"/>

For n = 1 and IQ = 1 mA, r_d ≈ 25.85 Ω. At 0.1 mA, it is 258.5 Ω. At 10 mA, it is 2.585 Ω—within this model.

A real diode can depart from this prediction because n changes with operating region, series resistance adds a voltage drop, temperature changes, or stored charge matters. We are building a useful model with a stated domain, not a universal description of a particular part.



<div className="worked-example">


<span className="tiny-label">WORKED EXAMPLE / TWO DIFFERENT RATIOS</span>
<h3>A 1 mA bias point</h3>
<p>Take Iₛ = 10⁻¹² A, n = 1, and Vₜ = 25.85 mV. Inverting the model gives VQ = Vₜ ln(1 + IQ/Iₛ) ≈ <strong>0.5357 V</strong>.</p>
<p>The DC ratio VQ/IQ is approximately <strong>535.7 Ω</strong>. The small-signal resistance is approximately <strong>25.85 Ω</strong>.</p>
<p>A +10 µA current change produces a linear voltage estimate of 25.85 Ω × 10 µA = <strong>0.2585 mV</strong>. The exact model gives Vₜ ln(1.01) ≈ <strong>0.2572 mV</strong>, with Iₛ negligible compared with the bias.</p>
<p>Using the DC ratio would predict 5.357 mV, over twenty times too much. It uses a line from the origin where we needed the tangent at Q.</p>


</div>




</section>





<section id="explore">




<div className="section-heading">

<span>03</span><h2>Explore the operating point</h2>

</div>



The source in this figure sets the diode voltage directly. Changing the bias-current control chooses a new IQ and computes the voltage needed to produce it. The perturbation control then adds a small voltage around that bias.

The **incremental circuit** removes the DC bias and replaces the diode by r_d. Its source is only the added voltage v. The linear current from that circuit is a change i; add IQ back to obtain a prediction for total current.

<SmallSignalLab client:load/>

<p className="caption">Figure 1. Fixed 300 K model with n = 1 and Iₛ = 10⁻¹² A. The tangent is exact at Q and becomes less accurate away from it. This lab does not model self-heating.</p>

### The tangent does not pass through the origin

Written in total quantities, the tangent model is I ≈ IQ + (V − VQ)/r_d. Written in variations, it is simply i ≈ v/r_d. These are the same relation in different coordinates.

A resistor alone cannot replace the diode for both DC bias and small changes. In a total-voltage representation, the tangent requires an offset source as well. In the incremental representation, subtracting the bias hides that offset cleanly.


</section>





<section id="circuit">




<div className="section-heading">

<span>04</span><h2>Put the local model in a circuit</h2>

</div>



Consider a diode from a node to ground, with a source feeding that node through a 1 kΩ resistor. Choose the source’s DC value to establish IQ = 1 mA. Using our model, the source must provide about 0.5357 V across the diode plus 1 V across the resistor: **1.5357 V**.

Now add a small signal vₛ to the source. For the incremental circuit, a constant ideal DC voltage source becomes zero volts, while the signal source remains. Replace the diode by its resistance at the established bias point.

<Equation tex="v_d\approx v_s\frac{r_d}{R_S+r_d},\qquad i\approx\frac{v_s}{R_S+r_d}"/>

With r_d = 25.85 Ω and vₛ = 10 mV, the diode-voltage change is about **0.2520 mV** and the current change is about **9.748 µA**. Most of the source’s small voltage change appears across the 1 kΩ resistor.

<CircuitFigure kind="incremental"/>

This is a voltage divider again, but one resistance comes from the slope of a nonlinear device. The signal is small **at the diode** even though the source perturbation is much larger.



<details className="derivation">


<summary>Check the approximation against the nonlinear circuit</summary>


<div>


<p>Subtract the original DC equation from the new circuit equation. Neglecting Iₛ relative to IQ gives:</p>
<Equation tex="v_s=R_S i+nV_T\ln\!\left(1+\frac{i}{I_Q}\right)"/>
<p>For i/IQ much smaller than one, ln(1 + i/IQ) ≈ i/IQ. The circuit equation becomes vₛ ≈ i(Rₛ + nVₜ/IQ), reproducing the incremental result.</p>
<p>For the 10 mV example, solving the nonlinear expression gives approximately 9.749 µA. The linear answer differs by about 0.012%. The series resistance limits the change at the nonlinear junction.</p>


</div>




</details>



### From a diode to an amplifier

For a bipolar transistor in an appropriate forward-active model, collector current depends exponentially on base–emitter voltage. Differentiation gives transconductance gₘ ≈ IC/Vₜ.

Transconductance relates a voltage at one port to a current at another; it is **not** automatically a two-terminal resistance. Nevertheless, the same method applies: solve the bias, differentiate the device relationships, then analyze the resulting linear circuit.

Feedback can change the resistance seen at a port. A resistor-like small-signal model may describe an entire active circuit, even when no physical resistor of that value exists.


</section>





<section id="limits">




<div className="section-heading">

<span>05</span><h2>How small is “small”?</h2>

</div>



For our diode, let x = v/(nVₜ). The exact current change around Q is:

<Equation tex="\Delta I=(I_Q+I_S)(e^x-1)=(I_Q+I_S)\left(x+\frac{x^2}{2}+\cdots\right)"/>

The linear model keeps only x. The ratio of the quadratic term to the linear term has magnitude |x|/2. Requiring that ratio to be at most about 1% suggests |v| ≲ 0.02 nVₜ, or roughly **0.52 mV** when n = 1 at 300 K. That is an estimate for the error in the current change, not a universal signal limit.



<div className="table-wrap">

<table><thead><tr><th>Positive voltage change</th><th>Approx. error in ΔI</th><th>Interpretation</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>1 mV</td><td>1.92%</td><td>Often a useful first approximation</td></tr><tr><td>5 mV</td><td>9.36%</td><td>Curvature is becoming significant</td></tr><tr><td>20 mV</td><td>33.75%</td><td>A poor linear estimate of the change</td></tr></tbody></table>

</div>



These errors compare the linear estimate with the exact exponential current **change**. Error relative to total current is a different number. State the denominator whenever quoting an approximation error.

### Small-signal does not mean high-frequency

Small describes amplitude. A slowly varying perturbation can be too large to linearize; a tiny high-frequency signal can require capacitive or dynamic terms. Differential resistance alone is a low-frequency or quasi-static approximation.

Temperature is another state variable. If a perturbation changes dissipation enough to heat the device, a slowly measured slope may include thermal feedback. It need not match the fixed-temperature derivative used here.
