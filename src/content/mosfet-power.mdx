<section id="switch">

<div className="section-heading"><span>01</span><h2>Start with the current paths</h2></div>

Connect an n-channel MOSFET source to ground, drain to the bottom of the load, and the load's top to the positive supply. A gate driver applies voltage relative to the source. A gate-source pull-down establishes an off state when the driver is disconnected; a series gate resistor controls charging current and damps some ringing.

<FetCircuit mode="switch" mos />

For an inductive load, add a freewheel path. The external flyback diode has its anode at the drain/load junction and cathode at the positive rail. While the MOSFET is on, it is reverse biased. When the MOSFET turns off, the inductor raises the drain voltage until the diode conducts, circulating current through the load and diode. The MOSFET's own body diode points from source to drain and does not provide this path for the usual low-side load.

The driver must supply a gate voltage at which the desired $R_{DS(\mathrm{on})}$ is specified. Its output-high voltage can sag under transient load, and the source can move due to wiring inductance. Measure or reason about $V_{GS}$ at the device terminals, not merely the logic pin voltage relative to a distant ground.

</section>

<section id="charge">

<div className="section-heading"><span>02</span><h2>Why $C_{iss}$ times voltage is often inadequate</h2></div>

<GateDrainChargeFigure/>

Small-signal capacitance is a local slope dQ/dV. A switching event moves through a wide range of drain and gate voltages, so gate charge is usually more useful for timing than a single input-capacitance value.

During a typical hard turn-on of an inductive load, gate voltage first rises toward the required conduction level. Current transfers to the MOSFET. While drain voltage falls, substantial gate current flows through the gate-drain capacitance. Gate voltage changes comparatively little over this interval: the **Miller plateau**. After the drain transition, further charge strengthens the channel and lowers its resistance.

For approximately constant gate current:

<Equation tex="t_{\mathrm{Miller}}\approx\frac{Q_{gd}}{I_g},\qquad I_g\approx\frac{V_{\mathrm{drive}}-V_{\mathrm{plateau}}}{R_{\mathrm{driver}}+R_g+R_{\mathrm{gate,int}}}" />

If drive is 10 V, plateau is 4 V and total resistance is 20 Ω, current is approximately 0.3 A. With $Q_{gd}$ = 12 nC, the estimated drain-voltage transition lasts 40 ns. This is a conditional estimate: plateau voltage and charge depend on current and drain voltage, and the driver may have nonlinear output impedance.

<GateChargeLab client:load />

Turn-off uses the driver's sink path and may have different resistance. A diode in parallel with part of the external gate resistance can intentionally create different turn-on and turn-off speeds. Faster edges reduce overlap loss but can increase ringing, electromagnetic interference and diode recovery stress.

</section>

<section id="loss">

<div className="section-heading"><span>03</span><h2>Derive the main power terms</h2></div>

During the on interval, an ohmic switch dissipates i²$R_{DS(\mathrm{on})}$. Average over the actual waveform:

<Equation tex="P_{\mathrm{cond}}=\frac1T\int_{\mathrm{on}}i^2(t)R_{DS(on)}(T_J)\,dt" />

For nearly constant on-state current I and duty D, this becomes I²$R_{DS(\mathrm{on})}$D. If inductor current has triangular peak-to-peak ripple ΔI around I, replace I² with I² + ΔI²/12 over that interval. Using average current squared without checking ripple can underestimate loss.

During hard switching, current and voltage overlap. Approximating each overlap interval as a triangular power pulse gives:

<Equation tex="P_{\mathrm{overlap}}\approx\frac12V_{\mathrm{bus}}I(t_r+t_f)f_s" />

For 24 V, 5 A, 30 ns rise overlap, 20 ns fall overlap and 100 kHz, this is 0.30 W. With hot $R_{DS(\mathrm{on})}$ = 24 mΩ and D = 0.5, conduction loss is 0.30 W. The estimated MOSFET total is 0.60 W before additional effects.

The gate-drive supply delivers approximately:

<Equation tex="P_{\mathrm{gate,supply}}=Q_gV_{\mathrm{drive}}f_s" />

At 30 nC, 10 V and 100 kHz, this is 30 mW. Much of it is dissipated in driver and gate resistances rather than the MOSFET channel. Report it separately when estimating MOSFET junction temperature.

Output-capacitance energy, body-diode conduction, reverse recovery and avalanche can add substantial loss. For nonlinear output capacitance, stored energy is the integral of V $C_{oss}$(V) dV, not automatically one half of a single tabulated capacitance times voltage squared. Measured switching-energy data may already include some of these mechanisms; adding them again double-counts loss.

</section>

<section id="explore">

<div className="section-heading"><span>04</span><h2>Frequency and temperature change the budget</h2></div>

<PowerFetLab client:load />

At low frequency conduction dominates; increasing frequency raises switching and drive losses. A larger transistor may reduce resistance while increasing gate and output charge. The best device depends on duty, current waveform, bus voltage and switching rate.

A steady thermal estimate is $T_J$ = $T_A$ + P RθJA, with a thermal resistance appropriate to the actual board and mounting. For the 0.60 W example and an assumed 50°C/W path at 40°C ambient, $T_J$ is about 70°C. That assumption must then be used to update on-resistance and losses. Iterate if temperature dependence materially changes power.

For a mounted heatsink, add the relevant junction-to-case, interface and sink-to-ambient terms. For short pulses, use transient thermal impedance. A quoted package thermal resistance is not a universal property independent of layout and cooling.

### Solve the temperature-resistance loop explicitly

Let $C_0=I^2D R_{25}$, fixed switching loss $P_{sw}$, and local resistance coefficient α. Combining resistance and thermal equations gives:

<Equation tex="P=\frac{C_0[1+\alpha(T_A-25)]+P_{sw}}{1-\theta\alpha C_0},\qquad T_J=T_A+\theta P"/>

For 5 A, duty 0.5, 20 mΩ at 25 °C, $C_0=0.25$ W. With α = 0.004/°C, ambient 40 °C, θ = 50 °C/W and switching loss 0.30 W, the denominator is 0.95. Total loss is 0.595 W and junction estimate 69.7 °C.

This closes the earlier iteration in a linear resistance model. The denominator must be positive for a finite stable equilibrium under its fixed-current assumptions. It does not represent every real temperature dependence, SOA mechanism or cooling configuration.

</section>

<section id="inductive">

<div className="section-heading"><span>05</span><h2>Decide where the magnetic energy goes</h2></div>

An inductor stores energy LI²/2. A 10 mH coil at 2 A contains 20 mJ. Turning off the transistor does not delete that energy; a diode, clamp, resistor, supply or the transistor must absorb or recover it.

A low-voltage flyback diode gives slow current decay. With approximately constant opposing clamp voltage VC and negligible coil resistance, di/dt ≈ −VC/L. At 0.7 V across 10 mH, decaying 2 A would take about 28.6 ms. A 24 V opposing clamp reduces the corresponding estimate to 0.833 ms. Real winding resistance changes the waveform, and the MOSFET drain sees the supply plus the opposing load clamp voltage in the usual low-side arrangement.

Avalanche is controlled breakdown of the MOSFET's drain junction. A specified single-pulse avalanche energy does not automatically permit repeated operation at the same energy. Starting temperature, current, repetition rate and device construction matter. Design a deliberate energy path and compare its voltage and energy with the selected components' ratings.

</section>

<section id="layout">

<div className="section-heading"><span>06</span><h2>Fast voltage changes can turn an off device on</h2></div>

A rising drain voltage drives current through $C_{gd}$ even when the gate command is off:

<Equation tex="i_{\mathrm{Miller}}\approx C_{gd}\frac{dV_D}{dt}" />

For an illustrative 100 pF and 5 V/ns, the instantaneous current estimate is 0.5 A. Through 5 Ω of gate-path impedance it can produce a 2.5 V disturbance. Real capacitance is voltage dependent, but the mechanism explains why a strong sink path, compact loop and sometimes a Miller clamp are useful.

Common-source inductance creates L di/dt voltage between the power source terminal and the driver's reference. Just 5 nH at 1 A/ns produces 5 V. A Kelvin-source connection separates the driver return from much of the power-current path. A gate-source resistor alone is usually too weak to control very fast injected current.

The [Infineon gate-drive application note](https://www.infineon.com/dgdl/Infineon-Gate_drive_for_power_MOSFETs_in_switchtin_applications-ApplicationNotes-v01_00-EN.pdf?fileId=8ac78c8c80027ecd0180467c871b3622) provides manufacturer guidance on interpreting switching charge and driver behavior. Use the loss equations as a first budget, then check actual device conditions and switching waveforms.



<h3 id="book-notes">Gate-driver peak current and average power answer different questions</h3>

A gate charge of 50 nC moved in 100 ns requires an average current of **0.5 A during the edge**. At 100 kHz and a 10 V drive swing, the corresponding gate-drive energy budget is $P\approx Q_GV_{drive}f=\mathbf{50\ mW}$. A small average power is therefore compatible with substantial pulse current. The driver, its local capacitor and the gate loop must carry that pulse.

Use Miller charge for the drain-voltage transition. With $Q_{GD}=15$ nC and 0.3 A available during the plateau, the approximate drain-transition time is 50 ns. A driver's advertised peak current is not necessarily the current available there: output resistance, external gate resistance and the difference between drive voltage and plateau voltage set it.

Common-source inductance couples the power path back into the gate drive. If 10 nH shared by both paths carries a 2 A change in 10 ns, its voltage is $L\,di/dt=\mathbf{2\ V}$. The driver may be producing the intended voltage relative to board ground while the transistor sees a different $V_{GS}$. A short local gate loop, separate source return where available, and individual gate resistors address this mechanism. Measure gate-to-source at the device terminals; measuring gate-to-ground can hide the cause of ringing or delayed switching.

<section id="check">

<div className="section-heading"><span>07</span><h2>Check your understanding</h2></div>

<details>
<summary>1. Why is gate charge often more useful than one $C_{iss}$ value?</summary>
<div>

A large switching excursion traverses voltage-dependent capacitances; charge counts what the driver must actually move.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>2. What approximately sets the Miller interval?</summary>
<div>

Gate-drain charge divided by available plateau gate current, under the stated charge and driver conditions.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>3. Should gate-supply power all be added to MOS channel heating?</summary>
<div>

No. Much is dissipated in the driver and gate resistances; keep it separate unless its physical partition is known.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>4. Does the nMOS body diode clamp an ordinary low-side load’s positive drain flyback?</summary>
<div>

No. It points from source to drain; the load needs its own deliberate turn-off path.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>5. Why does source inductance matter to the gate driver?</summary>
<div>

Power-current di/dt moves the source voltage relative to the driver return, altering actual gate-source drive.

</div>
</details>
