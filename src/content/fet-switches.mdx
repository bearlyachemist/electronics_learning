<section id="ideal">

<div className="section-heading"><span>01</span><h2>What a real switch adds</h2></div>

An ideal closed switch has zero resistance; an open switch has zero leakage and capacitance. A FET approximates those conditions over a restricted range of voltage, frequency and current. Its on-resistance varies with signal voltage, its off-state passes displacement current, and moving its gate can inject charge into the signal path.

If a switch with resistance $R_{ON}$ feeds a resistive load $R_L$ from a stiff voltage source, the same current flows through both, so they form a voltage divider and the attenuation is:

<Equation tex="\frac{V_o}{V_i}=\frac{R_L}{R_L+R_{ON}}" />

With $R_{ON}$ = 100 Ω and $R_L$ = 10 kΩ, the ratio is 0.9901, an error of about 0.99%. Into 1 MΩ the static error becomes only 0.01%, but the higher impedance makes leakage and charge injection more significant. There is no universally “negligible” switch resistance without a load and error budget.

A capacitively loaded switch also needs time to settle. Fast digital control does not imply immediate analog accuracy.

</section>

<section id="jfet">

<div className="section-heading"><span>02</span><h2>Turn off a normally-on JFET</h2></div>

A JFET channel conducts at gate-source voltage near zero. A sufficiently negative gate voltage depletes the channel and turns it off. For a bidirectional signal path, compare the gate voltage with **both** channel terminals: the more negative terminal can become the effective source.

Suppose a channel carries signals between −1 V and +1 V and the device's cutoff magnitude is at most 4 V under the relevant conditions. A −6 V off command gives at least −5 V gate-to-lowest-terminal voltage, providing nominal cutoff margin. A gate held at ground during the on state, however, becomes positive relative to a −1 V signal and can forward-bias a gate junction. That is not a valid symmetric analog switch arrangement.

Possible solutions include constraining signals to a compatible positive range, making the on-state gate track the signal, or using an integrated switch whose internal circuitry is designed for the specified range. The drive network must keep junctions reverse biased in both states and avoid exceeding reverse gate ratings.

A shunt mute is a useful topology: place the JFET channel between a low-amplitude signal node and a reference. On, it attenuates the signal through the upstream resistance; off, it leaves the node mostly undisturbed. With an upstream 10 kΩ resistor and a 100 Ω shunt channel, attenuation is approximately 100/10100 = 0.0099, about −40.1 dB. Loading by the next stage modifies this result. Smoothly changing the gate can reduce abrupt transients, but leakage and control feedthrough remain.

</section>

<section id="mos">

<div className="section-heading"><span>03</span><h2>Why one MOS pass transistor loses a logic level</h2></div>

An n-channel enhancement MOSFET conducts well when its gate is sufficiently above the lower-potential channel terminal. If its gate is fixed at $V_{DD}$ and it tries to charge an output capacitor toward $V_{DD}$, its available $V_{GS}$ falls as the output rises. The current becomes weak near $V_{DD}$ − $V_T$, with body effect often worsening the limitation.

<div className="derivation">

<span className="tiny-label">DERIVATION / HOW SLOWLY AN NMOS PASS DEVICE PULLS HIGH</span>

1. **Which terminal is the source.** The output $v$ is the lower-potential channel terminal, so it acts as the source: $V_{GS} = V_{DD} - v$. The drain, at $V_{DD}$, is far above $V_{GS} - V_T$, so the device is in saturation.

2. **KCL at the output capacitor.** The drain current charges $C$. Let $V_m = V_{DD} - V_T$, the voltage at which the overdrive reaches zero:
<Equation tex="C\frac{dv}{dt}=\frac{k}{2}(V_{DD}-v-V_T)^2=\frac{k}{2}(V_m-v)^2"/>

3. **Separate and integrate.** Let $w = V_m - v$, so $dw = -dv$. Then $-C\,dw/w^2 = (k/2)\,dt$, which integrates to $C/w = (k/2)t + C/V_m$ for an output starting at $v = 0$:
<Equation tex="v(t)=V_m-\frac{V_m}{1+kV_mt/2C}"/>

4. **Interpretation.** The remaining gap $V_m - v$ falls only as $1/t$, not exponentially. After ten time units of $2C/(kV_m)$, the output is still 9% short of $V_m$. In this model it never reaches $V_m$, let alone $V_{DD}$; in practice subthreshold conduction and leakage set where it stops.

</div>

The final result depends on leakage and loading; “$V_{DD}$ − $V_T$” is a useful strong-conduction boundary rather than an exact universal final voltage.

A p-channel pass device has the complementary weakness near the low rail. Put n and p devices in parallel with complementary gate controls and obtain a **CMOS transmission gate**. The n device conducts best toward the low end of the signal range; the p device helps toward the high end. The body/well connections must be arranged for the intended supply range.

This is why integrated analog switches specify signal range, supply voltages and on-resistance versus signal voltage. A digital-looking enable pin does not authorize input signals beyond the analog rails, particularly when the chip is unpowered.

A single discrete power MOSFET can conduct through its channel in either direction when enhanced, but its intrinsic body diode permits one direction even with the gate off. For bidirectional off-state blocking, two MOSFETs are commonly placed back to back.

In the common-source arrangement shown, drive the joined gates relative to the joined sources. With both off, one body diode opposes either external current direction. With both on, channel resistances add. Two 20 mΩ devices give approximately 40 mΩ; at 5 A their combined conduction loss is 1 W before temperature adjustment. Gate ratings still apply during every external voltage condition.

</section>

<section id="sample">

<div className="section-heading"><span>04</span><h2>Derive a sample-and-hold error budget</h2></div>

<SampleHoldFigure/>

During acquisition, a source resistance $R_{src}$ and the switch's on-resistance charge a hold capacitor $C_H$. For a voltage step from zero to V:

<Equation tex="V_H(t)=V\left(1-e^{-t/\tau}\right),\qquad \tau=(R_{\mathrm{src}}+R_{ON})C_H" />
<Equation tex="t_{\mathrm{settle}}\ge\tau\ln\!\left(\frac1\epsilon\right)" />

The first equation is the RC charging solution, because the source, the switch and the capacitor form one series loop with total resistance $R_{src} + R_{ON}$. The remaining fractional error is $(V - V_H)/V = e^{-t/\tau}$; requiring it to be at most $\epsilon$ and taking logarithms gives the settling time.

With $R_{src}$ = 1 kΩ, $R_{ON}$ = 100 Ω and $C_H$ = 1 nF, $\tau$ = 1.1 μs. Settling to 0.1% requires 6.91τ = 7.60 μs. For a full-scale step and a 12-bit converter's half-LSB criterion, $\varepsilon$ = 1/8192, requiring 9.01τ = 9.91 μs in this single-pole approximation. The source amplifier may impose further settling limits.

Opening the switch can place some channel or gate-coupled charge on the capacitor. Once the switch is open the capacitor is isolated, so charge is conserved on it and any delivered charge changes its voltage by $Q/C$:

<Equation tex="\Delta V_H=\frac{Q_{\mathrm{injected}}}{C_H}" />

An effective 5 pC delivered to 1 nF creates a 5 mV step. The partition of channel charge depends on impedances and timing, so use the relevant datasheet measurement or a suitable model rather than assigning all transistor channel charge to the hold node.

During hold, total leakage from the switch, capacitor, board and buffer changes the voltage. The capacitor law $i = C\,dv/dt$ gives the droop rate directly:

<Equation tex="\left|\frac{dV_H}{dt}\right|=\frac{|I_{\mathrm{leak}}|}{C_H}" />

At 10 pA and 1 nF, droop magnitude is 10 mV/s, or 100 μV over 10 ms. Increasing the capacitor helps both charge-injection error and droop, while increasing acquisition time. Thermal sampling noise adds an idealized RMS floor of √(kBT/$C_H$), approximately 2.0 μV at 300 K for 1 nF. It is a random error, unlike a repeatable offset step.

### Find whether the capacitor requirements overlap

A settling deadline provides an upper bound, while injected charge and hold leakage provide lower bounds:

<Equation tex="C_H\le\frac{t_{acq}}{(R_{src}+R_{ON})\ln(1/\varepsilon)},\qquad C_H\ge\frac{|Q_{inj}|}{\Delta V_{inj}},\qquad C_H\ge\frac{|I_{leak}|t_{hold}}{\Delta V_{hold}}"/>

For 20 µs acquisition, 1100 Ω total resistance and half-LSB settling for 12 bits, the upper bound is 2.02 nF. A 5 pC injection limited to 1 mV needs at least 5 nF. Ten picoamperes over 10 ms with at most 100 µV droop needs at least 1 nF. These three requirements do not overlap.

No capacitor choice alone fixes this example. Reduce injected charge or source resistance, extend acquisition, or alter the accuracy target. Keeping the bounds separate exposes the architectural tradeoff before selecting a component.

</section>

<section id="applications">

<div className="section-heading"><span>06</span><h2>Multiplexers, gain selection and logic</h2></div>

A multiplexer connects one of several analog sources to a common node. Break-before-make timing avoids shorting two sources together, but creates a brief unconnected interval. The next channel must settle from the previous channel's voltage; charge sharing can produce a much larger transient than the desired signal change.

A programmable-gain amplifier can switch resistors in a feedback network. Here switch resistance may directly perturb gain, and off-state capacitance can create a frequency-dependent path around a supposedly disconnected resistor. Put the switch in a low-sensitivity part of the network where possible, then derive the gain including $R_{ON}$ rather than assuming it vanishes.

CMOS logic uses a p-channel pull-up and n-channel pull-down. At a valid static input, one is off and the other restores the output to a supply rail. Dynamic power is approximately $\alpha C_L V_{DD}^2 f$, where $\alpha$ counts charging events per clock cycle. During edges, overlap conduction and internal capacitances add loss. This restores voltage levels more effectively than an isolated pass transistor.

For any switch application, follow the signal through all supply states. Off leakage, clamp conduction, diode paths and unpowered inputs are circuit behaviors, not exceptional cases outside the analysis.



<h3 id="book-notes">A multiplexer remembers the previous channel through capacitance</h3>

A multiplexer does more than select a resistor. Its output capacitor is initially charged to the previous channel's voltage. After changing channels, the new source must supply or absorb that charge through its own resistance and the switch's on-resistance.

With $R_S=100$ kΩ, $R_{ON}=500$ Ω and a 20 pF sampling node, the time constant is 2.01 µs. For a full-scale channel jump and a 12-bit target of half an LSB, the residual must fall below $1/8192$ of the step. The ideal settling requirement is $\tau\ln8192=\mathbf{18.1\ \mu s}$, before amplifier acquisition or recovery is counted. Converting immediately after selection can therefore report a mixture of the old and new channels.

Off-channel capacitance causes another error. A 3 V transition coupled through 1 pF into an otherwise floating 100 pF node gives an initial charge-sharing step of about $3\times1/(100+1)=\mathbf{29.7\ mV}$. A finite source then discharges that error; “off” resistance says little about its initial amplitude.

Break-before-make switching avoids briefly shorting two sources together, but creates an interval when the output is floating. Check that interval, source impedance, charge injection and amplifier overload recovery as a sequence. For fast or high-accuracy scanning, lower source impedance or individual channel buffers may help more than selecting a switch with a slightly smaller on-resistance.

<details>
<summary>1. Why does switch on-resistance matter even into an ideal capacitor?</summary>
<div>

It controls the charging time together with source resistance, even if final DC attenuation is negligible.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>2. Why can a single nMOS pass device weaken a HIGH?</summary>
<div>

As output rises, available gate-source overdrive falls; fixed-body effects can worsen it.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>3. What makes two back-to-back power MOSFETs block both directions?</summary>
<div>

Their opposing body-diode paths prevent an off-state forward path through both devices; gate drive must reference their common source.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>4. What three errors compete when choosing a hold capacitor?</summary>
<div>

Acquisition time, charge-injection step and leakage droop; increasing capacitance helps the latter two but slows acquisition.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>5. Does an off switch isolate every frequency?</summary>
<div>

No. Off-state capacitance and control feedthrough remain paths for changing signals.

</div>
