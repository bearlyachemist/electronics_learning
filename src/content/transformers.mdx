<section id="ratios">

<div className="section-heading"><span>01</span><h2>Derive the turns relations</h2></div>

Two windings that link a common changing magnetic flux develop voltages according to Faraday’s law. For an ideal transformer with all relevant flux linked to both windings:

<Equation tex="v_p=N_p\frac{d\Phi}{dt},\qquad v_s=N_s\frac{d\Phi}{dt}"/>
<Equation tex="\frac{v_p}{v_s}=\frac{N_p}{N_s}=a" label="01 / VOLTAGE FOLLOWS THE TURNS RATIO"/>

The physical idea is simple: both windings sit around the same changing flux, so **every turn has the same voltage induced in it**, whichever winding it belongs to. A winding's voltage is just volts-per-turn times its number of turns. Picture the core from the inductor chapter with a second winding added to the other leg.

<CoreFluxFigure/>

The terminal polarities in these equations are chosen consistently with the dot marks. Voltages measured from dotted to undotted ends have the same instantaneous polarity. Reversing one winding’s reference reverses the corresponding sign.

### Current transforms in the opposite direction

The ideal transformer stores no net energy and dissipates none. Power entering one winding leaves the other. If $i_p$ enters the primary dotted terminal and $i_{\mathrm{load}}$ leaves the secondary dotted terminal:

<Equation tex="v_p i_p=v_s i_{\mathrm{load}},\qquad i_p=\frac{i_{\mathrm{load}}}{a}"/>

If both winding currents are instead defined entering their dots, their ampere-turns sum to zero: $N_pi_p + N_si_s = 0$. The negative sign belongs to the reference directions, not to a loss of energy.

A step-up transformer provides more voltage but less available current for the same transferred power. It does not create power.

### Reflect the load

Let the secondary load be $Z_L = V_s/I_{\mathrm{load}}$. The input impedance seen at the primary is:

<Equation tex="Z_{\mathrm{in}}=\frac{V_p}{I_p}=a^2 Z_L" label="02 / IMPEDANCE TRANSFORMS AS TURNS SQUARED"/>

This applies to complex impedance as well as resistance under the ideal assumptions. It is one reason transformers are useful for matching a source to a load, not merely changing voltage. The factor is squared because the ratio acts twice: the voltage is $a$ times larger on the primary *and* the current is $a$ times smaller.

</section>

<div className="worked-example">

<span className="tiny-label">WORKED EXAMPLE / A TWO-TO-ONE TRANSFORMER</span><h3>Follow voltage, current, and power</h3>
<p>With 100 primary turns and 50 secondary turns, $a = 2$. A 12 V RMS primary produces <strong>6 V RMS</strong> across a 100 Ω load. Secondary current is <strong>60 mA RMS</strong>; primary load current is <strong>30 mA RMS</strong>.</p>
<p>Load power is $6^2/100 =$ <strong>0.36 W</strong>. Ideal input power is $12 \times 0.03 = 0.36$ W. The primary sees $a^2R_L =$ <strong>400 Ω</strong>.</p>
<p>Double secondary turns at the same primary voltage and resistance: secondary voltage doubles and load power quadruples. The source must supply the additional power; the transformer did not generate it.</p>

</div>

### Connect this to Thévenin equivalents

Suppose the 12 V RMS primary source has a 20 Ω series resistance. The reflected 400 Ω load receives $12 \times 400/(400 + 20) = 11.429$ V RMS at the primary winding, so the secondary receives **5.714 V RMS**.

The same result follows by reflecting the source to the secondary: 6 V RMS in series with $20/a^2 = 5\ \Omega$, feeding 100 Ω. The ideal ratio describes winding voltages, not necessarily the unloaded source voltage upstream of its impedance.

<ReflectionFigure/>

<div className="worked-example">

<span className="tiny-label">WORKED EXAMPLE / IMPEDANCE MATCHING</span><h3>An 8 Ω speaker on a 5 kΩ amplifier</h3>
<p>A vacuum-tube output stage works best into about 5 kΩ, but a loudspeaker is 8 Ω. Connected directly, the speaker would almost short the stage. A transformer with $a = \sqrt{5000/8} = 25$ makes the speaker look like $25^2 \times 8 = 5$ kΩ to the tube.</p>
<p>At 10 W into the speaker: $V_s = \sqrt{10 \times 8} = 8.9$ V RMS and $I_s = 1.12$ A. On the primary that becomes 224 V RMS at 44.7 mA, high voltage and low current, exactly what the tube handles well. Nothing has been amplified; the transformer has only exchanged volts for amps.</p>

</div>

<section id="flux">

<div className="section-heading"><span>03</span><h2>Voltage, frequency, and flux</h2></div>

Faraday’s law gives flux change from the time integral of winding voltage:

<Equation tex="\Delta B=\frac{1}{NA_e}\int v(t)\,dt"/>

$A_e$ is effective core cross-sectional area. Applied volt-seconds, not load power alone, set the required flux excursion in this simple picture.

For a sinusoidal voltage with RMS value $V$, integrating its sine wave gives:

<Equation tex="B_{\mathrm{peak}}=\frac{\sqrt{2}V_{\mathrm{rms}}}{2\pi fNA_e}"/>
<Equation tex="V_{\mathrm{rms}}\approx4.44fNA_eB_{\mathrm{peak}}" label="03 / THE 4.44 FACTOR IS FOR A SINE WAVE"/>

With 12 V RMS, 10 kHz, 100 turns, and 25 mm² area, the ideal flux estimate is about **0.108 T peak**. Reducing frequency to 2 kHz raises it to about 0.540 T. Whether either is acceptable depends on the actual material, temperature, waveform, and loss target.

<FluxVsFrequencyFigure/>

<div className="worked-example">

<span className="tiny-label">WORKED EXAMPLE / A VOLT-SECOND BOUND</span><h3>Estimate a minimum turn count</h3>
<p>Using the same 12 V RMS, 10 kHz, and 25 mm² area, suppose an illustrative flux target is 0.2 T peak. The sine-wave equation gives $N \ge 12/(4.44 \times 10{,}000 \times 25\times10^{-6} \times 0.2) \approx$ <strong>54.1 turns</strong>.</p>
<p>At least 55 whole turns satisfy this simplified flux inequality. That is not a completed winding design: core loss, copper area, temperature, leakage, insulation, and margins still constrain it.</p>

</div>

### Square waves use a different factor

For symmetric bipolar square voltage $\pm V$ with zero DC volt-seconds, the flux traverses from $-B_p$ to $+B_p$ in half a period:

<Equation tex="B_p=\frac{V}{4fNA_e}"/>

The factor is 4, not 4.44. Unequal positive and negative volt-seconds can make the flux operating point drift. A switching transformer needs a consistent reset mechanism or waveform balance.

An ordinary transformer is not a way to transfer a steady DC voltage. A constant applied primary voltage keeps changing flux until nonideal behavior, often saturation and resistance, dominates. Pulsed operation can work because the complete cycle manages flux; "pulsed DC" alone does not explain that management.

</section>

<section id="real">

<div className="section-heading"><span>04</span><h2>A practical equivalent</h2></div>

The ideal transformer predicts zero primary current with an open secondary. A real winding needs magnetizing current to establish flux, and core loss also draws power.

A useful equivalent adds a magnetizing inductance across the ideal primary, a core-loss branch when appropriate, winding resistances, and leakage inductances in the series paths. Winding capacitance and interwinding capacitance become important at higher frequency.

<TransformerEquivalentFigure/>

For a simple linear magnetizing branch:

<Equation tex="I_{m,\mathrm{rms}}=\frac{V_{p,\mathrm{rms}}}{2\pi fL_m}"/>

This current is approximately reactive in the lossless branch and adds vectorially to the load-related primary current. Do not simply add every RMS magnitude as though the currents were in phase.

<MagnetizingCurrentFigure/>

### Load current does not automatically imply more flux

Secondary load current produces ampere-turns opposed by an additional primary current. In a well-coupled voltage-driven model these largely cancel, while applied primary volt-seconds determine core flux.

Heavy load can still cause copper heating, voltage drop, and other limitations. But "more load current necessarily means proportionally more core flux" misses the transformer’s compensating primary current. DC imbalance and magnetizing conditions are separate concerns.

Leakage inductance represents flux that does not couple both windings. Together with capacitances it can cause ringing and imperfect high-frequency transfer. Winding resistance causes voltage regulation error and $I^2R$ heating.

<div className="worked-example">

<span className="tiny-label">WORKED EXAMPLE / REGULATION</span><h3>Why a small transformer's output sags</h3>
<p>A small mains transformer rated 12 V RMS at 1 A might measure 14 V with no load. Treat its windings, referred to the secondary, as about 2 Ω of resistance: at 1 A it loses 2 V and delivers its rated 12 V.</p>
<p>That 17% difference between no-load and full-load voltage is called regulation, and small transformers are deliberately wound this way: thinner wire is cheaper, and the resistance limits fault current. A circuit designed for "12 V" must tolerate about 14 V at light load, and the rectified peak is $14\sqrt2 \approx 19.8$ V (Chapter 21).</p>

</div>

</section>

<section id="design">

<div className="section-heading"><span>05</span><h2>Design and measurement limits</h2></div>

An isolation gap in a schematic means no intended conductive connection. It does not specify a physical insulation rating. Interwinding capacitance can still carry common-mode AC current, and a real isolation claim depends on the construction and ratings.

An autotransformer uses a shared winding connection and does not provide that same galvanic separation. A transformer used for power transfer also should not be confused with every device drawn as coupled windings; intended energy storage and operating topology matter.

When evaluating a transformer, distinguish turns ratio, no-load current, loaded regulation, magnetizing inductance, leakage, frequency band, and heating. An open-circuit ratio measurement cannot establish all of these.

The interactive model intentionally leaves out magnetizing and leakage currents. It shows ideal power transfer and a separate illustrative flux check so their different roles remain visible. If the flux estimate exceeds its displayed target, the clean ideal waveforms should not be read as a prediction of unsaturated hardware.



<h3 id="book-notes">VA describes winding stress, not DC output watts</h3>

The winding rating uses RMS voltage and RMS current because copper loss is proportional to $I_{\mathrm{rms}}^2R$. A 12 V, 1 A secondary is a 12 VA winding; it is not automatically a 12 W regulated DC supply after a bridge and reservoir capacitor.

Consider an illustrative recharge waveform that supplies 4 A for one quarter of each cycle and zero for the remainder. Its average is 1 A, but its RMS is **2 A**. A winding with 0.5 Ω resistance then dissipates 2 W, four times the 0.5 W it would dissipate at a steady 1 A. A real capacitor-input rectifier has a smoother pulse determined by transformer resistance, leakage inductance and capacitor charge, so calculate or measure its waveform rather than assigning a universal conversion factor.

Core flux is chiefly set by applied volts per turn and frequency; copper heating is chiefly set by RMS winding current. This separates two common mistakes: a larger reservoir capacitor does not directly increase the voltage-driven core flux, but it can narrow recharge pulses and increase RMS current. Conversely, using the transformer at a lower frequency with unchanged voltage raises core flux even at light load. The voltage rating, frequency rating and VA rating must therefore all fit the application.

<section id="check">

<div className="section-heading"><span>06</span><h2>Check your understanding</h2></div>

<details>
<summary>1. A 230 V RMS to 12 V RMS transformer: what is $a$, and what peak voltage appears on the secondary?</summary>
<div>

$a = 230/12 = 19.2$. The secondary peak is $12\sqrt2 = 17.0$ V (more at light load, as the regulation example shows).

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>2. A 4 Ω load is connected to the secondary of a 1 : 3 step-up transformer. What resistance does the primary source see?</summary>
<div>

$a = 1/3$, so $Z_{\mathrm{in}} = a^2 \times 4 = 0.44\ \Omega$. Stepping up the voltage makes the load look *smaller* to the source.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>3. A transformer designed for 60 Hz is run on 50 Hz mains at the same voltage. What changes?</summary>
<div>

The flux rises by $60/50 = 1.2$. If the core was already near saturation, the magnetizing current increases sharply and the transformer runs hot. The turns ratio is unchanged.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>4. Why can't a transformer pass DC?</summary>
<div>

Induced voltage requires changing flux. A steady primary voltage would demand flux increasing forever; in reality the current rises until the winding resistance limits it and the core saturates, and nothing useful appears on the secondary.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>5. With the secondary open, a transformer still draws primary current. Is that a fault?</summary>
<div>

No. That is magnetizing current, needed to establish the flux, plus a little core-loss current. It is mostly reactive, so it consumes little real power.

</div>
</details>
