<div><span className="tiny-label">THE QUESTION</span><p className="opening-question">Is a silicon diode "a 0.7 V drop"?</p></div>

<div>

Sometimes that is exactly the right model, and sometimes it is badly wrong. At 1 µA the same diode might drop 0.4 V; at 1 A, more than 1 V; when hot, less. And "off" does not mean no current: a reverse-biased diode still leaks, and it still behaves like a small capacitor.

This chapter turns the junction physics of Chapter 19 into a family of circuit models, from the ideal switch to the exponential law with charge storage, and shows how to choose among them.

<section id="models">

<div className="section-heading"><span>01</span><h2>Direction and diode models</h2></div>

Call the terminals **anode A** and **cathode K**. Define diode voltage $V_D = V_A - V_K$ and positive current from anode to cathode. The bar in the circuit symbol marks the cathode. A package stripe often identifies it too, but verify the actual pinout.

<DiodeSymbolFigure/>

A diode model is a deliberate choice about what matters:

<div className="table-wrap"><table><thead><tr><th>Model</th><th>Forward behavior</th><th>Reverse behavior</th><th>Useful question</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>Ideal switch</td><td>Zero voltage while conducting</td><td>Zero current while blocking</td><td>Which paths conduct?</td></tr><tr><td>Fixed drop</td><td>A chosen $V_F$ while conducting</td><td>Open circuit below breakdown</td><td>Rough current and headroom</td></tr><tr><td>Exponential</td><td>Current varies continuously with voltage</td><td>Small saturation current in its domain</td><td>Operating point and local slope</td></tr><tr><td>Practical dynamic model</td><td>Adds series resistance, charge and capacitance</td><td>Adds leakage and breakdown</td><td>Switching, loss, overshoot</td></tr></tbody></table></div>

<DiodeModelsFigure/>

For the ideal model, conducting requires $V_D = 0$ and $I_D \ge 0$; blocking requires $I_D = 0$ and $V_D \le 0$. These are consistency conditions. Guess a state, solve the remaining linear circuit, then check the result. A negative current through an assumed conducting ideal diode means that assumption was wrong.

With the fixed-drop model, replace zero forward voltage by $V_F$. It is not a hard physical threshold. "0.7 V for silicon" is a convenient estimate at some currents and temperatures, not a law.

**Worked example.** A 5 V source, 1 kΩ resistor and forward diode give $(5 - 0.7)\text{ V}/1\text{ k}\Omega = 4.3$ mA in the fixed-drop model. Reversing the diode gives approximately zero current below breakdown, with almost the entire 5 V across the diode in reverse.

<DiodeOrFigure/>

<div className="worked-example">

<span className="tiny-label">WORKED EXAMPLE / GUESS, SOLVE, CHECK</span><h3>Which source powers the load?</h3>
<p>Use the 0.7 V fixed-drop model. <strong>Guess</strong> that $D_1$ conducts and $D_2$ is off. Then the node sits at $5 - 0.7 = 4.3$ V and the load draws 4.3 mA, all through $D_1$.</p>
<p><strong>Check $D_1$:</strong> its current, 4.3 mA, is positive, so "on" is consistent. <strong>Check $D_2$:</strong> its anode is at 3 V and its cathode at 4.3 V, so $V_D = -1.3$ V, reverse-biased, consistent with "off." Both assumptions survive; this is the answer.</p>
<p>Had we guessed both on, the node would have to be at 4.3 V and at 2.3 V simultaneously, a contradiction. Unplug the adapter and the guess flips: $D_2$ conducts, the load gets 2.3 V, and $D_1$ blocks any current from flowing back into the adapter's output.</p>

</div>

</section>

<section id="exponential">

<div className="section-heading"><span>02</span><h2>Derive a useful current law</h2></div>

The physics chapter found that forward bias increases minority-carrier concentration at a junction edge exponentially. On the p side, the excess electron concentration at the edge is approximately:

<Equation tex="\Delta n(0)=n_{p0}\left(e^{V_D/V_T}-1\right)" />

In a long neutral region with diffusion and recombination, that excess decays as $\Delta n(x) = \Delta n(0)\,e^{-x/L_n}$. Its gradient at the junction therefore has magnitude $\Delta n(0)/L_n$. Multiplying by $qD_n$ gives the electron diffusion current density. Repeat for holes on the other side, add both contributions, and multiply by junction area:

<Equation tex="I_D=I_S\left(e^{V_D/V_T}-1\right),\qquad I_S=qA n_i^2\left(\frac{D_n}{L_nN_A}+\frac{D_p}{L_pN_D}\right)" />

This derivation assumes low-level injection, negligible neutral-region voltage drop, steady state, and suitable neutral-region lengths. Real devices include other mechanisms; a useful fitted version introduces ideality factor $n$:

<Equation tex="I_D=I_S\left(e^{V_D/(nV_T)}-1\right)" />

Do not confuse ideality factor $n$ with the electron concentration $n$ used in the preceding physics chapter. Here it is a dimensionless fitting parameter. It may change with operating regime.

At forward current much larger than $I_S$, invert the law:

<Equation tex="V_D\approx nV_T\ln(I_D/I_S),\qquad \Delta V_D=nV_T\ln(I_2/I_1)" />

At 300 K with $n = 1.5$, a tenfold current increase requires about 89.3 mV more forward voltage in this model. Doubling current needs about 26.9 mV. Current can change dramatically while the displayed voltage seems almost unchanged.

<DiodeCurveFigure/>

The two plots resolve the opening question. The "0.7 V turn-on" is an artifact of looking at a linear current axis scaled in milliamps. On a logarithmic axis the curve has no corner; it is a straight line. A diode's forward voltage is better described as *about 0.6–0.7 V at the milliamp currents we usually use, changing by about 60–90 mV per decade of current*.

Differentiating gives the small-signal resistance:

<Equation tex="r_d=\left(\frac{dI_D}{dV_D}\right)^{-1}=\frac{nV_T}{I_D+I_S}\approx\frac{nV_T}{I_D}" />

At 1 mA and $n = 1.5$ this is 38.8 Ω. It is not $V_D/I_D$ and it applies only to small variations around that bias point. At high current, bulk and contact resistance add to the local slope.

</section>

<section id="explore">

<div className="section-heading"><span>03</span><h2>Find the operating point</h2></div>

The diode cannot choose its current independently of the circuit. For a source $V_S$ and series resistor $R$:

<Equation tex="I_D=\frac{V_S-V_D}{R}" />

This descending load line intersects the rising diode curve. The intersection satisfies both device physics and Kirchhoff's voltage law. The lab finds it by bracketed numerical solution, not by assigning a fixed forward drop.

<DiodeLab client:load />

For the default 5 V, 1 kΩ, $I_S = 1$ pA and $n = 1.5$, expect roughly 0.86 V and 4.14 mA. This illustrative model deliberately need not match the 0.7 V estimate. Compare the percentage error in current with the percentage error in diode voltage: a large resistor can make current relatively insensitive to the precise forward drop.

<div className="worked-example">

<span className="tiny-label">WORKED EXAMPLE / HOW MUCH DOES THE MODEL MATTER?</span><h3>Fixed drop versus exponential</h3>
<p>The fixed 0.7 V model predicts $(5 - 0.7)/1\text{ k}\Omega = 4.30$ mA; the exponential model gives 4.14 mA. The voltage estimate was off by 0.16 V (19%), but the current only by 4%, because the resistor absorbs most of the supply.</p>
<p>Now drop the supply to 1.2 V. The fixed-drop model predicts $(1.2 - 0.7)/1\text{ k}\Omega = 0.50$ mA. Solving the exponential model gives $V_D = 0.77$ V and 0.43 mA, 14% lower, because the true drop is now a large fraction of the supply. <strong>The smaller the headroom above the diode drop, the more the diode model matters.</strong></p>

</div>

**Design example.** A 3.3 V source drives an LED specified near 2.0 V at 5 mA. The first estimate is $R = (3.3 - 2.0)\text{ V}/5\text{ mA} = 260\ \Omega$; 270 Ω gives about 4.81 mA at that nominal drop. At a 3.6 V supply and 1.8 V LED drop, that same resistor gives 6.67 mA. Design around the allowed current over source and device variation. An LED is not accurately represented by the silicon example's saturation current.

</section>

<section id="dynamic">

<div className="section-heading"><span>04</span><h2>Charge, temperature, and speed</h2></div>

Temperature changes more than $V_T$. Saturation current changes strongly with temperature too. Holding $I_S$ artificially fixed while increasing $V_T$ would produce a misleading temperature experiment. At fixed current, many silicon PN diodes show a decreasing forward drop with temperature over useful ranges, but the coefficient is device- and current-dependent.

<DiodeTempFigure/>

Reverse leakage also depends on voltage and temperature. The simple exponential model's $-I_S$ reverse plateau omits surface leakage, depletion-region generation and breakdown. Avalanche and tunneling can make reverse current rise sharply; safe operation then requires current and power control. A useful rule of thumb: leakage roughly doubles for every 10 °C rise.

Reverse bias stores depletion charge, creating junction capacitance. Forward injection stores excess minority carriers. When drive reverses, that stored charge must be removed or recombine. Current may temporarily flow in the reverse direction before the device recovers its blocking state.

<Equation tex="Q_{rr}=\int_{\mathrm{recovery}} |i_R(t)|\,dt" />

<RecoveryFigure/>

For a triangular 0.2 A reverse-current pulse lasting 50 ns, the charge is approximately 5 nC. Time alone does not specify recovery loss: current, voltage, temperature and switching slope matter. A Schottky diode uses a metal–semiconductor barrier and avoids the same minority-carrier storage mechanism, but still has capacitance and displacement current.

**Why circuit context matters.** A fast voltage edge across 20 pF produces $i = C\,dv/dt$. At 100 V/µs that is 2 mA even without forward conduction. A diode can be "off" in the DC sense and still couple a transient.

</section>

<section id="selection">

<div className="section-heading"><span>05</span><h2>Read a diode specification</h2></div>

Read maximum ratings separately from typical curves. Check repetitive reverse voltage, average forward current under its stated thermal conditions, surge waveform and duty cycle, forward drop at the intended current, leakage at the intended temperature, and junction temperature. For switching, inspect recovery charge/time and capacitance with their test conditions.

Approximate conduction loss by the average of $v_D(t)\,i_D(t)$. A constant 0.8 V at 0.5 A gives 0.4 W; a pulsed rectifier requires averaging its actual current waveform. Package thermal resistance and copper layout then determine temperature rise.

<div className="table-wrap"><table><thead><tr><th>Family</th><th>Typical strength</th><th>Typical weakness</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>Small-signal PN (1N4148 type)</td><td>Fast, low capacitance, low leakage</td><td>Low current</td></tr><tr><td>Rectifier (1N400x type)</td><td>Amps of current, high voltage</td><td>Slow recovery, larger capacitance</td></tr><tr><td>Schottky</td><td>Low drop (0.2–0.5 V), almost no recovery</td><td>Higher leakage, lower voltage, hot-sensitive</td></tr><tr><td>Zener</td><td>Controlled reverse breakdown</td><td>Dissipation, noise, soft knee at low voltage</td></tr><tr><td>TVS</td><td>Absorbs specified surge energy</td><td>Capacitance, leakage</td></tr><tr><td>LED</td><td>Emits light</td><td>Low reverse voltage, drop 1.8–3.3 V by color</td></tr></tbody></table></div>

No family wins every combination of leakage, capacitance, voltage, loss and cost.



<h3 id="book-notes">The fastest diode can still transfer a capacitive current pulse</h3>

Reverse recovery and junction capacitance are different mechanisms. A minority-carrier diode can continue conducting while stored charge is extracted after reversal. A Schottky junction largely avoids that minority-carrier storage mechanism, but its voltage-dependent capacitance still draws current when voltage changes.

With a local capacitance of 100 pF and a voltage edge of 10 V in 20 ns, $i=C\,dv/dt$ gives a **50 mA** displacement-current scale. That pulse can upset a high-impedance detector or flow through a switch even when a recovery-time specification seems negligible. For a large voltage change, use the charge integral $\Delta Q=\int C(V)\,dV$ rather than multiplying the initial capacitance by the full step.

Select a diode by its job: a low-drop power rectifier emphasizes conduction loss and leakage; a peak-hold diode emphasizes leakage and capacitance; a fast commutation diode emphasizes recovery charge at the relevant current and edge conditions. Breakdown rating alone addresses none of those. A low forward drop can come with greater leakage, and reverse leakage increases with temperature, so a diode that improves power efficiency may worsen a long-duration hold measurement.

<div className="section-heading"><span>06</span><h2>Check your understanding</h2></div>

<details>
<summary>1. A diode drops 0.62 V at 1 mA with $n = 1$. Estimate its drop at 10 mA and at 10 µA.</summary>
<div>

60 mV per decade: about 0.68 V at 10 mA and 0.50 V at 10 µA (ignoring series resistance).

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>2. You assume an ideal diode conducts and find its current is −2 mA. What do you conclude?</summary>
<div>

The assumption is wrong: an ideal conducting diode cannot carry reverse current. Redo the analysis with the diode off, then check that its voltage is negative.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>3. Why might a Schottky diode be a poor choice for a sample-and-hold switch at 85 °C?</summary>
<div>

Its reverse leakage is much higher than a PN diode's and rises steeply with temperature, so it would discharge the hold capacitor. Its low drop and speed don't help there.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>4. Two identical diodes are connected in parallel to share 2 A. Why might one take most of the current?</summary>
<div>

Small differences in $I_S$ or temperature shift their curves; at a common voltage the slightly "leakier" or warmer one carries more current, heats more, and its forward drop falls further, a positive feedback that can concentrate the current. Small series resistors help balance them (Chapter 40).

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>5. A rectifier diode switches off from 1 A. Its datasheet quotes $t_{rr}$ = 2 µs. Why might this matter in a 100 kHz converter?</summary>
<div>

The 10 µs period leaves only 5 µs per half-cycle, and for up to 2 µs of each the diode conducts backward, shorting the circuit through itself and wasting energy at every edge. A fast-recovery or Schottky diode is needed.

</div>
</details>
