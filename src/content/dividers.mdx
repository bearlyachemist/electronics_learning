<section id="divider">




<div className="section-heading">

<span>01</span><h2>Two resistors, one ratio</h2>

</div>



A voltage is a difference in electric potential between two points. We choose the bottom wire as our **0 V reference**, then describe the supply and output voltages relative to it. Ground here is a reference node; it does not mean current disappears into the earth.

The source maintains a voltage across two resistors in series. With no external connection drawing current from the midpoint, the same current flows through both resistors. That condition is what makes the familiar divider equation possible.



<details className="prerequisite">

<summary>A quick bridge: voltage, current, and resistance</summary>

<div>


<p><strong>Current</strong> is the rate at which charge passes a point, measured in amperes. <strong>Voltage</strong> is energy transferred per unit charge, measured in volts. A resistor relates its voltage drop to its current through Ohm’s law: V = IR.</p>
<p>Kirchhoff’s current law says that currents entering a node equal currents leaving it, for this lumped DC model. Kirchhoff’s voltage law says that the signed voltage changes around a loop sum to zero. We use conventional current, flowing from the source’s positive terminal through the resistors toward the negative terminal.</p>
<p>A resistance of 10 kΩ is 10,000 Ω. A current of 0.5 mA is 0.0005 A. Multiplying them gives a voltage drop of 5 V.</p>


</div>



</details>



### Follow the current first

The total series resistance is R₁ + R₂. Applying Ohm’s law around the loop gives the current. The output is the voltage drop across the lower resistor:

<Equation tex={String.raw`I = \frac{V_{\mathrm{in}}}{R_1+R_2}, \qquad V_{\mathrm{out}} = I R_2`} label="01 / CURRENT → VOLTAGE"/>

Substitute the current into the second expression. This is the unloaded divider equation:

<Equation tex={String.raw`\boxed{V_{\mathrm{out}} = V_{\mathrm{in}}\frac{R_2}{R_1+R_2}}`} label="02 / THE DIVIDER RATIO"/>

The numerator contains R₂ because we measure the output **across R₂**. A larger fraction of the total resistance below the midpoint means a larger fraction of the supply voltage appears there.

<aside className="margin-note"><strong>Check the limits.</strong> If R₂ approaches zero, the output approaches ground. If R₁ approaches zero, the output approaches the supply. If they are equal, the ratio is one half. These checks are often better than memorizing the numerator.</aside>

<DividerLab client:load simple={true}/>

<p className="caption">Figure 1. The ratio sets the unloaded voltage; the absolute resistance sets the current and dissipation. Values in this figure are saved locally in your browser.</p>

### A first calculation

With a 10 V source and two 10 kΩ resistors, the current is 10 V / 20 kΩ = **0.5 mA**. Each resistor drops 5 V. Each dissipates I²R = **2.5 mW**, so the source delivers 5 mW in total.

Replace both with 100 kΩ resistors: the output is still 5 V, but current falls to 0.05 mA and total dissipation to 0.5 mW. This looks like an easy improvement. We have not yet asked the output to supply anything.


</section>





<section id="loading">




<div className="section-heading">

<span>02</span><h2>When a load joins in</h2>

</div>



A **load** is whatever you connect to receive the output: a measuring instrument, another amplifier, a sensor interface. Start with a simple model—a resistor Rₗ connected between output and ground.

Rₗ and R₂ now share the same two nodes, so they are in parallel. Their equivalent resistance is smaller than either resistance alone. Replace R₂ in the original divider equation by this parallel combination:

<Equation tex={String.raw`R_{\mathrm{bottom}} = R_2 \parallel R_L = \frac{R_2R_L}{R_2+R_L}`}/>
<Equation tex={String.raw`V_{\mathrm{out}} = V_{\mathrm{in}}\frac{R_2\parallel R_L}{R_1+(R_2\parallel R_L)}`} label="03 / THE LOADED DIVIDER"/>

With R₁ = R₂ = Rₗ = 10 kΩ, the lower combination is 5 kΩ. The output becomes 10 × 5 / (10 + 5) = **3.333 V**, not 5 V.

### Why does the output fall?

The extra branch draws current from the midpoint. Current through R₁ now splits between R₂ and the load. The effective total resistance seen by the source falls, so the source supplies more current. More current through R₁ means a larger voltage drop across it, leaving less voltage at the output.

Current is not “used up” by R₁. Charge keeps moving, and current divides between branches. The source supplies energy; the resistors turn electrical energy into heat.

<DividerLab client:visible/>
<p className="caption">Figure 2. The curve sweeps the possible load resistance; the orange point is the currently selected load. Each equal horizontal interval is a factor of ten in resistance.</p>



<details className="derivation">

<summary>Derive the same result directly from the node equation</summary>

<div>


<p>At the output node, current arriving through R₁ equals the sum leaving through R₂ and Rₗ:</p>
<Equation tex={String.raw`\frac{V_{\mathrm{in}}-V_{\mathrm{out}}}{R_1} = \frac{V_{\mathrm{out}}}{R_2} + \frac{V_{\mathrm{out}}}{R_L}`}/>
<p>Collect every term containing the unknown output voltage on the right:</p>
<Equation tex={String.raw`\frac{V_{\mathrm{in}}}{R_1} = V_{\mathrm{out}}\left(\frac1{R_1}+\frac1{R_2}+\frac1{R_L}\right)`}/>
<p>Divide by the quantity in parentheses, then multiply numerator and denominator by R₁R₂Rₗ:</p>
<Equation tex={String.raw`V_{\mathrm{out}} = \frac{V_{\mathrm{in}}R_2R_L}{R_1R_2+R_1R_L+R_2R_L}`}/>
<p>This is algebraically identical to the parallel-resistance expression. The node method will remain useful when a circuit no longer looks like a recognizable divider.</p>


</div>



</details>





<div className="worked-example">

<span className="tiny-label">ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE / MEASURING CHANGES THE CIRCUIT</span><h3>A meter is also a load</h3>
<p>Suppose two 1 MΩ resistors divide 10 V. A voltmeter modeled as a 10 MΩ input resistance measures the midpoint. The unloaded voltage is 5 V, but the lower effective resistance is 1 MΩ ∥ 10 MΩ = 0.9091 MΩ.</p>
<p>The meter therefore reads <strong>4.7619 V</strong>: about 4.76% below the unloaded value. Even an accurate meter can report a voltage that differs from the voltage present before it was connected.</p>
<p>Using two 10 kΩ divider resistors with the same meter reduces the loading error to about <strong>0.050%</strong>, at the cost of 100 times the unloaded divider current.</p>

</div>




</section>

<div className="section-heading">

<span>03</span><h2>A simpler way to see it</h2>

</div>



Instead of carrying three resistors through every calculation, describe the original divider as an ideal voltage source with a resistance in series. This is its **Thévenin equivalent**, seen between the output terminal and ground.

The equivalent voltage Vₜₕ is the voltage measured with no load. The equivalent resistance Rₜₕ tells us how strongly the terminal voltage changes when current is drawn.

<Equation tex={String.raw`V_{\mathrm{th}} = V_{\mathrm{in}}\frac{R_2}{R_1+R_2}, \qquad R_{\mathrm{th}} = R_1\parallel R_2`} label="04 / THE OUTPUT AS A SOURCE"/>

### Why are the two resistors in parallel here?

To find the output resistance of this linear circuit, set the **independent ideal voltage source to zero volts**. An ideal zero-volt source is a short circuit. The top of R₁ is now connected to ground, just like the bottom of R₂. Looking into the output, both resistors lead to the same reference node; they are in parallel.

This source-deactivation rule applies to finding the equivalent resistance. We have not physically shorted a powered supply. For networks with dependent sources, keep those sources active and use a test source when necessary.

### Loading becomes a second divider

The external load now forms a divider with Rₜₕ:

<Equation tex={String.raw`V_{\mathrm{out}} = V_{\mathrm{th}}\frac{R_L}{R_{\mathrm{th}}+R_L}`}/>

Equivalently, Vₒᵤₜ = Vₜₕ − IₗRₜₕ. The output resistance is the amount of voltage lost per ampere drawn. It is a property of the source circuit as seen from its terminals, not necessarily a single physical resistor.

Define fractional loading error relative to the unloaded voltage. For positive resistances and a nonzero supply:

<Equation tex={String.raw`\epsilon = \frac{V_{\mathrm{th}}-V_{\mathrm{out}}}{V_{\mathrm{th}}} = \frac{R_{\mathrm{th}}}{R_{\mathrm{th}}+R_L}`} label="05 / HOW MUCH ERROR?"/>

Solving this expression for the load requirement gives a useful design inequality:

<Equation tex={String.raw`R_L \geq R_{\mathrm{th}}\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon_{\max}}-1\right)`}/>



<div className="table-wrap">

<table><thead><tr><th>Maximum loading error</th><th>Required load</th><th>What it means</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>10%</td><td>Rₗ ≥ 9 Rₜₕ</td><td>A visible disturbance</td></tr><tr><td>1%</td><td>Rₗ ≥ 99 Rₜₕ</td><td>A much lighter load</td></tr><tr><td>0.1%</td><td>Rₗ ≥ 999 Rₜₕ</td><td>Precision needs margin</td></tr></tbody></table>

</div>



Notice that “the load is ten times the source resistance” means roughly **9.09% error**, not 1%. A rule of thumb is only useful when its implied error is acceptable.


</section>





<section id="design">




<div className="section-heading">

<span>04</span><h2>From calculation to design</h2>

</div>



A ratio tells us which voltage we obtain unloaded. Absolute resistance tells us how much current we waste and how easily a load can move that voltage. Design means choosing both.



<div className="worked-example">

<span className="tiny-label">WORKED DESIGN / A 1% LOADING BUDGET</span><h3>Choose a divider for a known input resistance</h3>
<p>We want an unloaded 5 V midpoint from 10 V. The following circuit is modeled as a 100 kΩ resistance to ground. Allow at most 1% error from loading alone.</p>
<p><strong>1. Set the ratio.</strong> Equal resistors give the desired half-supply voltage. Call each resistance R; their Thévenin resistance is R/2.</p>
<p><strong>2. Apply the error constraint.</strong> Rₜₕ ≤ Rₗ/99 = 1.0101 kΩ, so each resistor must be no larger than 2.0202 kΩ.</p>
<p><strong>3. Choose values.</strong> Two nominal 2 kΩ resistors give Rₜₕ = 1 kΩ. The loaded output is 5 × 100/101 = <strong>4.9505 V</strong>. Loading error is 0.9901%.</p>
<p><strong>4. Check dissipation.</strong> Unloaded current is 2.5 mA, giving 12.5 mW per resistor. With the specified load connected, R₁ dissipates about 12.75 mW and R₂ about 12.25 mW.</p>
<p><strong>5. Interpret the result.</strong> The nominal loading target is met with almost no margin. This is not yet a 1%-accurate output design: resistor tolerances and supply variation are still outside the budget. A lower resistance can buy loading margin, but costs current.</p>

</div>



### What resistor tolerance actually changes

Two resistors marked “10 kΩ, 1%” do not necessarily have the same actual resistance. If they both shift upward by the same percentage, the unloaded ratio is unchanged. The troublesome case is **ratio mismatch**.

For two nominally equal resistors and an exact 10 V source, the lowest unloaded output occurs when R₁ is 1% high and R₂ is 1% low:

<Equation tex={String.raw`V_{\min}=10\frac{9.9}{10.1+9.9}=4.95\ \mathrm{V},\qquad V_{\max}=5.05\ \mathrm{V}`}/>

The resistances in this expression are in kΩ; their units cancel in the ratio. These are worst-case bounds, not a probability statement. They also exclude loading. To combine tolerances with a load, evaluate the loaded equation at the relevant extremes of resistor, load, and supply values.



<details className="derivation">

<summary>Go deeper: sensitivity explains ratio matching</summary>

<div>


<p>Let k = R₂/(R₁ + R₂). For small changes, logarithmic differentiation gives:</p>
<Equation tex={String.raw`\frac{\Delta k}{k}\approx\frac{R_1}{R_1+R_2}\left(\frac{\Delta R_2}{R_2}-\frac{\Delta R_1}{R_1}\right)`}/>
<p>For equal resistors the coefficient is one half. Opposite 1% changes produce approximately 1% ratio error. Equal fractional changes cancel. This is why tracking between resistors can matter more than either resistor’s absolute accuracy.</p>
<p>The approximation is a first-order local result. Use the exact divider expression for final corner calculations or large changes.</p>


</div>



</details>



### Compensate for a known load—or reduce its effect?

If a load is fixed, we can design the divider around it. For a 10 V supply, a 10 kΩ lower resistor and a fixed 10 kΩ load combine to 5 kΩ. Choosing R₁ = 5 kΩ gives exactly 5 V at that particular load.

Disconnect that load, however, and the output rises to **6.667 V**. Compensation has corrected one operating condition; it has not made a stiff voltage source.

A buffer is another option: its input draws little current from the divider, while its output supplies the receiving circuit. Real buffers bring their own offset, input current, output-current limits, and supply-range constraints. We will develop those ideas in the amplifier chapters.


</section>

<div className="section-heading">

<span>05</span><h2>Where the model ends</h2>

</div>



The calculations here assume an ideal DC supply, linear resistors, steady conditions, and a load described by a resistance to ground. Those assumptions are useful—and specific.



<div className="limit-grid">




<div>

<span className="tiny-label">SUPPLY VARIATION</span><h3>A divider is not a regulator</h3><p>With fixed resistances, output is proportional to input. A 5% supply increase causes a 5% output increase. The divider does not correct it.</p>

</div>




<div>

<span className="tiny-label">TIME & FREQUENCY</span><h3>An input can store charge</h3><p>Input capacitance interacts with output resistance. In a simple model, Rₜₕ and an input capacitance create a time constant τ = RₜₕC. A correct DC voltage may still settle too slowly.</p>

</div>




<div>

<span className="tiny-label">HEAT & NOISE</span><h3>Resistance has other costs</h3><p>Lower resistance increases source current and dissipation. Higher resistance can increase thermal voltage noise and sensitivity to leakage. Loading is only one part of the tradeoff.</p>

</div>




<div>

<span className="tiny-label">REAL LOADS</span><h3>Not everything is a resistor</h3><p>A current sink, diode input, or switched sampling capacitor needs a different load model. Keep the node equation; replace the load relationship with one appropriate to the device.</p>

</div>
