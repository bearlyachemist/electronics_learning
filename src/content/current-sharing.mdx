<div className="opening-grid">

<div><span className="tiny-label">A PRACTICAL QUESTION</span><p className="opening-question">One transistor can safely carry 1.5 A. You need 2 A, so you wire two in parallel. Why might the first one still fail?</p></div>

<div>

Paralleling power devices is the natural way to handle more current or more dissipation than one package allows. Resistors in parallel share current according to their resistances. Bipolar transistors in parallel share according to their $V_{BE}$ values, through an exponential law, and their $V_{BE}$ values depend on their temperatures, which depend on their currents.

The result can be a stable, nearly even split, or a runaway in which one transistor takes almost everything. This chapter quantifies both effects and shows the standard fix: a small resistor in each emitter.

</div>

</div>

<section id="why">

<div className="section-heading"><span>01</span><h2>Why parallel transistors</h2></div>

A single power transistor is limited by its maximum collector current, by its junction temperature (set by dissipation and thermal resistance), and by its safe operating area. Two transistors in parallel, each on its own share of heat sink, double the possible current and halve each device’s dissipation—*if* they share equally.

Tie the bases together, the collectors together and the emitters together: the two transistors see identical $V_{BE}$ and $V_{CE}$. They share equally only if their $I_S$ values are identical and they are at the same temperature. Neither is ever exactly true.

</section>

<section id="mismatch">

<div className="section-heading"><span>02</span><h2>Mismatch and the exponential</h2></div>

Two transistors of the same type may need $V_{BE}$ values differing by several millivolts, sometimes tens of millivolts, at the same current. Ebers–Moll converts a voltage difference into a current ratio:

<Equation tex="\frac{I_1}{I_2}=e^{\Delta V_{BE}/V_T}"/>

A 1 mV difference gives a 4% imbalance; 5 mV, 21%; 10 mV, 47%; 25 mV, a factor of 2.6. In the lab, a 5 mV mismatch with no ballast and a generous heat sink (0.5 °C/W) splits 2 A as **1.16 A and 0.84 A**: one transistor carries 38% more than the other, and the electrical mismatch has been amplified somewhat by the thermal effect described next.

</section>

<section id="thermal">

<div className="section-heading"><span>03</span><h2>The electro-thermal loop</h2></div>

Suppose one transistor carries slightly more current. It dissipates more, $V_{CE}$ × I, and runs hotter by $\theta$ × $V_{CE}$ × I, where $\theta$ is its junction-to-ambient thermal resistance. A hotter junction needs about 2.1 mV less $V_{BE}$ per °C for the same current; at a fixed shared $V_{BE}$, its current therefore rises further. This is **positive feedback**.

Quantify one trip around the loop.

<div className="derivation">

<span className="tiny-label">DERIVATION / THE ELECTRO-THERMAL LOOP GAIN</span>

1. **Electrical to thermal.** A small current increase $\Delta I$ raises the dissipation by $V_{CE}\,\Delta I$ (holding $V_{CE}$ fixed). Through the thermal resistance, the junction temperature rises by
<Equation tex="\Delta T=\theta\,V_{CE}\,\Delta I"/>

2. **Thermal to electrical.** At constant current the junction would need $k\,\Delta T$ less base–emitter voltage, with $k$ ≈ 2.1 mV/°C. With the base voltage held fixed, that is equivalent to an extra drive voltage $\Delta V = k\,\Delta T$ applied in series with the junction.

3. **Voltage to current.** KVL from the fixed base node through the junction and $R_E$: the extra drive must be absorbed by a junction-voltage change $V_T\,\Delta I'/I$ (the junction's small-signal resistance $r_e = V_T/I$) plus a resistor drop $R_E\,\Delta I'$:
<Equation tex="\Delta I'=\frac{\Delta V}{r_e+R_E}=\frac{k\,\theta\,V_{CE}\,\Delta I}{V_T/I+R_E}"/>

4. **Loop gain** is the ratio of the returned current change to the original. Multiply numerator and denominator by $I$:
<Equation tex="G_{\mathrm{loop}}=\frac{\Delta I'}{\Delta I}=\frac{\theta\,V_{CE}\,I\,k}{V_T+IR_E}" label="ELECTRO-THERMAL LOOP GAIN"/>

5. **Stability.** Going around the loop repeatedly gives a total response $\Delta I(1 + G + G^2 + \cdots)$, a geometric series that converges to $\Delta I/(1 - G)$ only if $G < 1$. For $G \ge 1$ the disturbance grows.

</div>

If $G_{\mathrm{loop}}$ is below 1, a disturbance dies out and the pair settles at a slightly uneven split. If it exceeds 1, any imbalance grows. With a fixed total current, as here, the outcome is **current hogging**: one transistor takes almost everything. With a fixed voltage drive, the outcome is **thermal runaway**: the current of the whole stage rises until something fails.

<div className="worked-example">

<span className="tiny-label">WORKED EXAMPLE / THE OPENING QUESTION</span><h3>2 A shared at $V_{CE}$ = 10 V, 2 °C/W per device</h3>

<p>Without ballast, at 1 A each: $G_{\mathrm{loop}}$ = 2 × 10 × 1 × 0.0021/0.0259 ≈ <strong>1.6</strong>. The equal split is unstable. The lab settles with <strong>1.92 A and 0.08 A</strong>: the first transistor carries nearly all of the 2 A—more than its 1.5 A rating—and runs at 63 °C while its partner idles near 27 °C.</p>

<p>With 0.1 Ω in each emitter: $G_{\mathrm{loop}}$ = 0.042/(0.026 + 0.1) ≈ <strong>0.33</strong>. The split becomes 1.03 A and 0.97 A with the same 5 mV mismatch.</p>

</div>

The loop gain grows with $\theta$, with $V_{CE}$ and with current. High-voltage, high-current linear operation on a small heat sink is the dangerous corner—exactly where linear amplifiers and regulators operate.

</section>

<section id="ballast">

<div className="section-heading"><span>05</span><h2>Designing ballast resistors</h2></div>

An emitter resistor converts a current difference into a voltage that opposes it. Without temperature effects, KVL from the shared base node through each junction and ballast resistor to the shared emitter rail gives the same linearized result as the degenerated mirror (Chapter 35): a mismatch $\Delta V_{BE}$ must be absorbed by $V_T\,\Delta I/I + R_E\,\Delta I$. The current imbalance becomes

<Equation tex="\frac{\Delta I}{I}\approx\frac{\Delta V_{BE}}{I R_E+V_T}"/>

The quantity I·$R_E$—the voltage dropped across each ballast resistor at its share of the current—is what matters. With 5 mV mismatch and I·$R_E$ = 0.1 V, the imbalance is about 4%; with 0.5 V, about 1%. The same I·$R_E$ term appears in the denominator of the loop gain, so the same resistor also defeats the thermal feedback.

<SharingFigure/>

A practical recipe:

1. Estimate the worst $V_{BE}$ mismatch for the devices (tens of millivolts for unselected parts unless matched).
2. Choose I·$R_E$ of 0.1–0.5 V at each device’s nominal current, making the static imbalance acceptable.
3. Check $G_{\mathrm{loop}}$ at the worst combination of $V_{CE}$, current and thermal resistance, and keep it well below 1 (0.5 or less).
4. Account for the cost: each resistor dissipates I²$R_E$ (0.1 W at 1 A and 0.1 Ω) and costs I·$R_E$ of headroom.

Typical values in audio output stages are 0.1–0.47 Ω per device (Chapter 37), which also stabilizes the quiescent current. Mounting all devices on one heat sink, close together, adds thermal coupling that pulls their temperatures together and helps further.

</section>

<section id="soa">

<div className="section-heading"><span>06</span><h2>Hot spots, SOA and MOSFETs</h2></div>

**The same problem inside one transistor.** A large power transistor is effectively many small transistors in parallel across its chip. If one region runs hotter, it draws more current and heats further, concentrating current into a hot spot. This **second breakdown** can destroy a transistor at a power well below its nominal rating when both voltage and current are high. It is why datasheets give a **safe operating area (SOA)** graph that falls off more steeply at high $V_{CE}$ than a simple constant-power limit would. Manufacturers fight it with distributed emitter ballast resistance on the chip.

**MOSFETs share differently.** At high gate drive, a MOSFET’s on-resistance rises with temperature, so a hotter device carries *less* current: paralleled MOSFETs in switching service share naturally. At low gate drive, in linear operation below a crossover current, their temperature coefficient reverses and they can hog current just as BJTs do. Linear MOSFET stages still need source resistors or individual control.

**Active sharing.** When precision matters, each device can have its own sense resistor and control loop, forcing equal currents regardless of mismatch—at the cost of more parts.

### Solve the thermal requirement for ballast

With $k=|dV_{BE}/dT|$ and a chosen maximum local loop gain $L$, require $\theta V_{CE}Ik/(V_T + IR_E) \le L$. Multiply out to get $V_T + IR_E \ge \theta V_{CE}Ik/L$, then divide by $I$:

<Equation tex="R_E\ge\frac{\theta V_{CE}k}{L}-\frac{V_T}{I}"/>

Use zero if the right side is negative, then impose the independent matching and voltage-drop requirements. At 1 A each, 30 V, 2 °C/W and $k=2.1$ mV/°C, keeping the local estimate below 0.5 requires $R_E\ge0.226$ Ω. A 0.1 Ω resistor that works well at 10 V does not retain the same thermal margin at 30 V.

<span className="tiny-label">WORKED EXAMPLE / MATCHING, POWER AND STABILITY</span><h3>Two devices, 1 A nominal each</h3>

<p>Ignoring self-heating, a 5 mV mismatch with 0.1 Ω ballast gives a relative current difference of $0.005/(0.02585+0.1)=3.97\%$, so the split is approximately 1.020 A and 0.980 A. Including separate heat paths at 10 V and 2 °C/W increases the difference to about 6%, as the lab shows.</p>

<p>Each resistor dissipates roughly 0.10 W at 1 A. A 0.25 W part has only a nominal power margin: check ambient derating and overload pulses. A larger 0.33 Ω resistor gives stronger thermal control but loses 0.33 V and dissipates 0.33 W at 1 A.</p>

<p>The model assumes each device’s heat path is independent. A common heat sink creates thermal coupling; equal sink temperature can improve sharing, while unequal junction-to-case paths still matter. No terminal ballast resistor can guarantee against local hot spots inside a transistor beyond its safe operating area.</p>

</div>

<section id="check">

<div className="section-heading"><span>07</span><h2>Check your understanding</h2></div>

<details>
<summary>1. Why can a few millivolts of mismatch cause a large current error?</summary>
<div>

BJT collector current depends exponentially on base–emitter voltage; a shared base voltage does not force equal currents.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>2. Where must the ballast resistors go?</summary>
<div>

One in each emitter before the emitters join. A shared resistor after the junction senses only total current.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>3. Why does increasing collector–emitter voltage worsen thermal sharing?</summary>
<div>

It increases power per ampere, so a current increase causes more heating and a stronger junction-voltage change.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>4. Does an unchanged total current exclude a thermal problem?</summary>
<div>

No. One device can take almost all the fixed total and exceed its own current or dissipation rating.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>5. Can a steady-state sharing calculation replace the SOA check?</summary>
<div>

No. It does not model local hot spots, second breakdown, pulse limits or transient heat flow.

</div>
</details>
