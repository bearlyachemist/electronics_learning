<section id="derive">

<div className="section-heading"><span>01</span><h2>From low-pass to integral</h2></div>

A capacitor integrates its current exactly in the ideal constant-$C$ model:

<Equation tex="v_C(t)=v_C(t_0)+\frac{1}{C}\int_{t_0}^{t}i(u)\,du"/>

To integrate an input **voltage**, we need a circuit that converts that voltage to a suitable current. A resistor feeding a capacitor is the simplest attempt, but its current depends on both input and output:

<Equation tex="i=\frac{v_{\mathrm{in}}-v_{\mathrm{out}}}{R},\qquad RC\frac{dv_{\mathrm{out}}}{dt}+v_{\mathrm{out}}=v_{\mathrm{in}}"/>

If the output term is small compared with the relevant input variation, the derivative term dominates:

<Equation tex="\frac{dv_{\mathrm{out}}}{dt}\approx\frac{v_{\mathrm{in}}}{RC}"/>
<Equation tex="v_{\mathrm{out}}(t)\approx K+\frac{1}{RC}\int v_{\mathrm{in}}(t)\,dt" label="01 / A PASSIVE APPROXIMATION"/>

$K$ records the integration constant. Neglecting it does not make stored charge disappear. For periodic zero-mean steady-state examples we choose the corresponding periodic solution; a startup problem requires its own initial condition.

**The physical picture** is the mirror image of the differentiator's. If the output stays small, the resistor sees almost the whole input voltage, so it acts as a voltage-to-current converter, $i \approx v_{\mathrm{in}}/R$. The capacitor then does what it always does: accumulates that current as charge. The integrator works while its output is small enough not to disturb the resistor current.

### Use the frequency condition

The exact RC low-pass transfer function is:

<Equation tex="H(j\omega)=\frac{1}{1+j\omega RC}"/>

When $x = \omega RC = f/f_c$ is much greater than one, the constant one in the denominator is small:

<Equation tex="H(j\omega)\approx\frac{1}{j\omega RC}"/>

The **high-frequency region of a low-pass circuit** approximates integration. At those frequencies output is strongly attenuated and approaches a −90° phase shift. This is not an unlimited integrator extending down to DC.

A constant DC input eventually appears at the capacitor unchanged, while an ideal integrator driven by a nonzero constant would ramp indefinitely. Those are fundamentally different long-term behaviors.

<div className="section-heading"><span>02</span><h2>Compare exact and approximate</h2></div>

For input $A\sin(\omega t)$, a zero-mean integral is $-A\cos(\omega t)/\omega$. Dividing by $RC$ gives the passive approximation’s amplitude $A/(\omega RC)$ and phase −90°.

The plot compares that approximation with the exact low-pass response. Changing frequency also changes the time represented by one horizontal period.

<FilterLab client:load kind="lowpass"/>

<p className="caption">Exact sinusoidal steady state and an integral approximation. This plot does not include a startup transient or an arbitrary integration constant.</p>

The relative complex-response error is:

<Equation tex="\frac{H_{\mathrm{approx}}-H}{H}=\frac{1}{jx},\qquad \left|\frac{H_{\mathrm{approx}}-H}{H}\right|=\frac{1}{x}"/>

At $f/f_c = 10$, the complex error is 10%; at 100, it is 1%. These include phase as well as amplitude. At a ratio of 10, exact phase is −84.29°, not yet −90°.

Better approximation comes with smaller passive output. If the output becomes comparable to offsets, noise, or loading effects, mathematical accuracy of the ideal approximation is not enough.

</section>

<section id="waveforms">

<div className="section-heading"><span>03</span><h2>Ramps and triangles</h2></div>

During a short constant-current interval, capacitor voltage is a ramp. For a passive RC circuit driven by a square wave, the current is only approximately constant because output voltage also appears across the resistor.

With an input alternating between $+A$ and $-A$, and a half-period short compared with $RC$, the output has nearly constant slopes $\pm A/(RC)$. A periodic output centered around zero is approximately triangular.

<SquareToTriangleFigure/>

<div className="worked-example">

<span className="tiny-label">WORKED EXAMPLE / SQUARE TO NEAR-TRIANGLE</span><h3>How large is the triangle?</h3>
<p>Drive a circuit with $RC = 1$ ms using a ±1 V square wave at 10 kHz. Its period is 100 µs. Approximate slope magnitude is $1\text{ V}/1\text{ ms} =$ <strong>1,000 V/s</strong>.</p>
<p>Over the 50 µs positive half-cycle, output rises by about 50 mV. Thus the periodic waveform is approximately <strong>50 mV peak-to-peak</strong>, or 25 mV peak around zero.</p>
<p>The exact RC waveform has gently curved segments, not perfectly straight lines. Its peak magnitude is $A\tanh[T/(4RC)]$, which here is <strong>24.995 mV</strong>.</p>

</div>

<details className="derivation">
<summary>Derive the exact square-wave peak</summary>
<div>

<p>In symmetric steady state, the positive half-cycle starts at $-V_p$ and ends at $+V_p$. During that half-cycle, the source is $+A$ and the duration is $T/2$:</p>
<Equation tex="V_p=A+(-V_p-A)e^{-T/(2RC)}"/>
<p>Collect the $V_p$ terms and solve:</p>
<Equation tex="V_p=A\frac{1-e^{-T/(2RC)}}{1+e^{-T/(2RC)}}=A\tanh\!\left(\frac{T}{4RC}\right)"/>
<p>For a small argument, $\tanh z \approx z$, giving $V_p \approx AT/(4RC)$. A unipolar 0-to-$A$ square wave has a DC component and a different midpoint; it must not be silently treated as $\pm A$.</p>

</div>
</details>

### A pulse represents area

For a current pulse, voltage change depends on total charge, not merely its peak. A 10 µA current lasting 20 µs adds 200 pC. On 1 nF, that changes voltage by **0.2 V**.

This makes integration useful in charge measurement and averaging. It also makes unwanted bias currents and offsets accumulate. Radiation detectors, for example, deliver each particle's charge as a brief, irregular current spike; integrating it onto a capacitor turns that spike into a clean voltage step whose height measures the particle's energy.

</section>

<section id="active">

<div className="section-heading"><span>04</span><h2>An active integrator</h2></div>

An ideal inverting op-amp integrator has an input resistor and a feedback capacitor. With the summing node held approximately at zero by negative feedback, input current is $v_{\mathrm{in}}/R$. Applying current continuity at that node gives:

<Equation tex="\frac{dv_{\mathrm{out}}}{dt}=-\frac{v_{\mathrm{in}}}{RC}"/>
<Equation tex="v_{\mathrm{out}}(t)=v_{\mathrm{out}}(t_0)-\frac{1}{RC}\int_{t_0}^{t}v_{\mathrm{in}}(u)\,du"/>

The amplifier supplies the inversion and holds the input resistor’s far end near a fixed potential. This removes the passive integrator’s dependence on output voltage within the amplifier’s valid operating range.

<ActiveIntegratorFigure/>

<div className="worked-example">

<span className="tiny-label">WORKED EXAMPLE / AN OUTPUT RAMP</span><h3>How long before the output runs out of room?</h3>
<p>With $R = 10$ kΩ and $C = 100$ nF, $RC = 1$ ms. A +0.1 V input makes the ideal output fall at <strong>100 V/s</strong>.</p>
<p>Starting at 0 V, reaching an assumed −10 V output boundary takes <strong>100 ms</strong>. The integral equation is no longer valid beyond that boundary if the amplifier saturates.</p>
<p>Even an unwanted equivalent DC input of +1 mV would produce a −1 V/s ramp. "Small" does not mean harmless when its effect accumulates.</p>

</div>

### Give DC a path

A feedback resistor $R_f$ in parallel with the capacitor makes a leaky integrator. For an ideal op-amp:

<Equation tex="H(s)=-\frac{R_f/R}{1+sR_fC}"/>

At DC it has finite gain $-R_f/R$. Well above $1/(2\pi R_fC)$, it approaches $-1/(sRC)$, while the amplifier must still have enough loop gain and bandwidth.

For $R_f = 1$ MΩ and $C = 100$ nF, that transition is about 1.59 Hz. This provides a controlled DC behavior, not a perfect integral down to zero frequency.

<DualSlopeFigure/>

<div className="worked-example">

<span className="tiny-label">WORKED EXAMPLE / WHY MULTIMETERS INTEGRATE</span><h3>A converter whose accuracy ignores $R$ and $C$</h3>
<p><strong>Phase 1.</strong> Integrate the unknown $V_{\mathrm{in}}$ for a fixed time $T_1$. The output reaches $V_{\mathrm{in}}T_1/RC$ in magnitude.</p>
<p><strong>Phase 2.</strong> Switch the input to a reference $-V_{\mathrm{ref}}$ and count the time $t_2$ for the output to return to zero: $V_{\mathrm{ref}}t_2/RC = V_{\mathrm{in}}T_1/RC$, so</p>
<Equation tex="V_{\mathrm{in}}=V_{\mathrm{ref}}\,\frac{t_2}{T_1}"/>
<p>$RC$ appears on both sides and cancels. So does the clock frequency, if one clock times both phases. Only the reference voltage must be accurate. Choose $T_1$ = 100 ms, a whole number of cycles of both 50 Hz and 60 Hz mains, and any hum on the input integrates to zero. With $V_{\mathrm{ref}}$ = 1 V and a 100 kHz clock, an input of 0.5 V gives $t_2$ = 50 ms, or 5,000 counts.</p>
<p>This is why so many digital multimeters use this method: integration turns a hard analog problem (an accurate, stable RC) into an easy one (counting).</p>

</div>

</section>

<section id="drift">

<div className="section-heading"><span>05</span><h2>Initial conditions, drift, and reset</h2></div>

A capacitor’s starting voltage contributes directly to the output. A reset mechanism establishes an initial condition; a switch that resets a real capacitor can inject charge, leak, and require settling time. The mathematical act of setting an integral to zero has a physical implementation cost.

Capacitor leakage, dielectric absorption, input bias current, and amplifier offset produce errors that may grow with time. For example, 10 nA of unwanted current into 100 nF changes voltage at **0.1 V/s**. Over a 200 ms hold, that is 20 mV.

An integrator can suppress high-frequency content, but it does not automatically solve all noise problems. Low-frequency offsets and drift are especially important because of the long accumulation time.

For an active circuit, check output swing, slew rate, input common-mode range, capacitor polarity and voltage, and closed-loop stability. These determine the interval and bandwidth over which the ideal derivation is useful.

</section>

<section id="check">

<div className="section-heading"><span>06</span><h2>Check your understanding</h2></div>

<details>
<summary>1. An RC low-pass with $f_c$ = 100 Hz is used to integrate a 5 kHz signal. What are the complex error and the output amplitude for a 1 V input?</summary>
<div>

$x = 50$, so the error is $1/x = 2\%$, and the output is about $1/x = 20$ mV. Accuracy and output amplitude are bought together.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>2. An active integrator ($RC$ = 10 ms) has 0.2 V at its input for 30 ms, starting from 0 V. Where does the output end up?</summary>
<div>

$-0.2 \times 30/10 = -0.6$ V, assuming the op-amp stays within its range.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>3. Why does a passive RC integrator not ramp forever when given DC?</summary>
<div>

As the output rises, the resistor voltage $v_{\mathrm{in}} - v_{\mathrm{out}}$ falls, so the charging current shrinks. The output settles at the input voltage. The active version holds the resistor's far end at 0 V, so the current never shrinks.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>4. What does $R_f$ cost in a leaky integrator?</summary>
<div>

Below $f = 1/(2\pi R_fC)$ the circuit stops integrating and becomes a fixed-gain amplifier. It trades exact low-frequency integration for bounded DC behavior. Choose $R_f$ so that transition lies below the band of interest.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>5. A 100 pF integrating capacitor receives a 2 pC charge pulse. What output step results? What if the pulse arrives as 1 µA for 2 µs instead of 10 µA for 0.2 µs?</summary>
<div>

$Q/C = 2\text{ pC}/100\text{ pF} = 20$ mV in both cases. The integrator responds to the area, not the shape.

</div>
</details>
