<div><span className="tiny-label">A PRACTICAL QUESTION</span><p className="opening-question">Two diodes back to back have the same junctions as a transistor. Why don’t they amplify?</p></div>

<div>

Chapter 19 built a PN junction and Chapter 20 derived the diode law from carriers that diffuse away from it. A bipolar transistor adds one idea: put a second junction so close to the first that the carriers injected by one are collected by the other before they can recombine.

The consequences are the transistor rules used in every later chapter. Collector current is set by the base–emitter voltage, almost independent of the collector voltage. Only a small base current is needed to supply the losses, so the current gain $\beta$ is large. And the same physics predicts the transistor’s imperfections: a slight dependence on collector voltage, a speed limit, and a strong temperature dependence. This chapter derives them in a deliberately simple one-dimensional model.

</div>

<section id="diffusion">

<div className="section-heading"><span>01</span><h2>Minority carriers diffuse and recombine</h2></div>

Inject extra electrons into p-type silicon, where they are minority carriers. Two things happen. They spread out by diffusion, because random thermal motion carries more of them from crowded regions to sparse ones. And they disappear by recombination with the plentiful holes, on average after a **lifetime** $\tau$.

Both effects can be written as one equation by counting electrons in a thin slice of the material.

<div className="derivation">

<span className="tiny-label">DERIVATION / THE MINORITY-CARRIER CONTINUITY EQUATION</span>

1. **Set up a control volume.** Take a slice of cross-sectional area $A$ between $x$ and $x + dx$. Let $\Delta n(x,t)$ be the excess electron density (electrons per cm³ above equilibrium). The slice contains $\Delta n\,A\,dx$ excess electrons.

2. **Diffusion flux.** Fick's law (Chapter 19) says the particle flux, electrons per cm² per second, flows down the density gradient:
<Equation tex="F(x)=-D_n\frac{\partial\,\Delta n}{\partial x}"/>

3. **Net inflow.** Electrons enter through the face at $x$ at the rate $A\,F(x)$ and leave through the face at $x + dx$ at the rate $A\,F(x+dx)$. For a thin slice, $F(x+dx) \approx F(x) + \frac{\partial F}{\partial x}dx$, so the net inflow is
<Equation tex="A\,[F(x)-F(x+dx)]=-A\frac{\partial F}{\partial x}dx=A\,D_n\frac{\partial^2\Delta n}{\partial x^2}dx"/>

4. **Recombination.** Each excess electron survives an average time $\tau_n$, so the slice loses excess electrons at the rate $\Delta n\,A\,dx/\tau_n$.

5. **Balance.** The rate of change of the slice's contents equals inflow minus loss. Dividing every term by $A\,dx$:
<Equation tex="\frac{\partial\,\Delta n}{\partial t}=D_n\frac{\partial^2\Delta n}{\partial x^2}-\frac{\Delta n}{\tau_n}" label="MINORITY-CARRIER CONTINUITY"/>

6. **Steady state.** With the bias held constant, $\partial\Delta n/\partial t = 0$. Dividing by $D_n$ and defining $L_n^2 = D_n\tau_n$:
<Equation tex="\frac{d^2\Delta n}{dx^2}=\frac{\Delta n}{L_n^2},\qquad L_n=\sqrt{D_n\tau_n}"/>
   This is a linear second-order equation whose solutions are $e^{x/L_n}$ and $e^{-x/L_n}$, or any combination of them.

<p className="assume">Assumed: one-dimensional flow, no electric field in the region (so no drift current), and low-level injection (the excess electrons are few compared with the holes, so $\tau_n$ is constant).</p>

</div>

The **diffusion length** $L_n$ is how far a typical carrier wanders before recombining. For electrons in moderately doped p-type silicon, $D_n$ ≈ 20 cm²/s and $\tau_n$ ≈ 0.1 µs give $L_n$ ≈ 14 µm.

### Long and short regions

If the p region is much longer than $L_n$, the excess decays as $e^{-x/L_n}$: almost every injected electron recombines inside it. That was the “long diode” of Chapter 20.

If the region is much **shorter** than $L_n$ (say 1 µm) and its far end is held at Δn = 0 by a contact or by a collecting junction, recombination hardly has time to act. Then the right side $\Delta n/L_n^2$ is negligible compared with the curvature the boundary conditions force, $d^2\Delta n/dx^2 \approx 0$, and the profile is a straight line from its edge value Δn(0) to zero. The electron current density is $q$ times the diffusion flux, $J_n = qD_n\,|d\Delta n/dx|$, and for a straight line the slope is $\Delta n(0)/W$:

<Equation tex="\Delta n(x)\approx\Delta n(0)\left(1-\frac{x}{W}\right),\qquad J_n=qD_n\frac{\Delta n(0)}{W}"/>

The current is set by the slope of that line. A shorter region gives a steeper slope and therefore more current for the same edge density. This short-region case is the heart of the transistor.

### What the straight-line approximation leaves out

Solve the steady equation exactly, without dropping the recombination term, and the approximation's error becomes visible.

<div className="derivation">

<span className="tiny-label">DERIVATION / EXACT PROFILE AND BASE TRANSPORT FACTOR</span>

1. **General solution.** Any combination of $e^{\pm x/L_n}$ solves the steady equation. Writing it with hyperbolic functions of the distance from the far end makes the boundary conditions easy:
<Equation tex="\Delta n(x)=a\,\sinh\frac{W-x}{L_n}+b\,\cosh\frac{W-x}{L_n}"/>

2. **Collecting boundary,** $\Delta n(W) = 0$: at $x = W$, $\sinh 0 = 0$ and $\cosh 0 = 1$, so $b = 0$.

3. **Injecting boundary,** $\Delta n(0) = n_0$: $a\,\sinh(W/L_n) = n_0$, so
<Equation tex="\Delta n(x)=n_0\,\frac{\sinh\left((W-x)/L_n\right)}{\sinh(W/L_n)}"/>

4. **Current at each end.** The electron current density is $J(x) = qD_n|d\Delta n/dx|$. Differentiating, $d\sinh(u)/dx = \cosh(u)\cdot(-1/L_n)$ with $u = (W-x)/L_n$:
<Equation tex="J(x)=\frac{qD_nn_0}{L_n}\,\frac{\cosh\left((W-x)/L_n\right)}{\sinh(W/L_n)}"/>
   At the emitter edge ($x = 0$) the cosh is $\cosh(W/L_n)$; at the collector edge ($x = W$) it is 1.

5. **Base transport factor,** the fraction of injected current that arrives at the collector:
<Equation tex="\alpha_T=\frac{J(W)}{J(0)}=\frac{1}{\cosh(W/L_n)}"/>
   The missing fraction is the electrons that recombined on the way.

6. **Thin-base limit.** For $u = W/L_n \ll 1$, use $\sinh u \approx u$ and $\cosh u \approx 1 + u^2/2$. The profile becomes $n_0(W-x)/W$, the straight line, and
<Equation tex="\alpha_T\approx\frac{1}{1+W^2/2L_n^2}\approx1-\frac{W^2}{2L_n^2}"/>

</div>

 At $W/L_n=0.1$, the exact survival fraction is 0.9950; at 1 it is only 0.648. A "thin" base means thin relative to the diffusion length, not merely small on a ruler. In forward active operation, reverse bias changes the far-edge equilibrium condition slightly; at substantial injection it is negligible beside the injected density.

<section id="structure">

<div className="section-heading"><span>02</span><h2>Two junctions, very close together</h2></div>

An NPN transistor is a thin p-type **base** sandwiched between an n-type **emitter** and an n-type **collector**. The three regions are not symmetric:

- The **emitter** is very heavily doped (around 10¹⁹ cm⁻³), so that when its junction is forward-biased it injects many electrons into the base and receives few holes back.
- The **base** is thin—well under a micrometre in modern devices—and only moderately doped, so that injected electrons cross it before recombining.
- The **collector** is more lightly doped and large. Its junction with the base is reverse-biased in normal operation, so its depletion region has a strong field that sweeps any electron arriving at its edge into the collector.

Two discrete diodes wired back to back share the junction types but not the geometry. Their metal interconnection does not preserve a minority-carrier distribution across a shared thin semiconductor base. Each junction behaves independently rather than handing injected carriers to the other.

<section id="transport">

<div className="section-heading"><span>03</span><h2>Follow an electron through the base</h2></div>

Forward-bias the base–emitter junction by $V_{BE}$ and derive the collector current in two steps: first the electron density the junction sets at the base edge, then the current that density drives across the base.

<div className="derivation">

<span className="tiny-label">DERIVATION / THE COLLECTOR CURRENT LAW</span>

1. **Equilibrium across the junction.** With no bias, electrons on the emitter side (density $n_{n0} \approx N_D$) face a potential barrier $V_{bi}$ to reach the base. In thermal equilibrium the Boltzmann relation links the densities on the two sides:
<Equation tex="n_{p0}=n_{n0}\,e^{-V_{bi}/V_T},\qquad n_{p0}=\frac{n_i^2}{N_A}"/>
   The second form is the mass-action law $np = n_i^2$ in the p-type base, where $p \approx N_A$.

2. **Apply forward bias.** A forward voltage $V_{BE}$ lowers the barrier to $V_{bi} - V_{BE}$. Assume the junction stays close to equilibrium internally (low-level injection: the injected electrons are far fewer than the base's holes), so the same Boltzmann relation holds across the reduced barrier:
<Equation tex="n(0)=n_{n0}\,e^{-(V_{bi}-V_{BE})/V_T}=n_{p0}\,e^{V_{BE}/V_T}=\frac{n_i^2}{N_A}e^{V_{BE}/V_T}"/>
   This is the **law of the junction**: each $V_T \ln 10$ ≈ 60 mV of forward bias multiplies the edge density by ten.

3. **Collector boundary.** The reverse-biased collector junction's field sweeps away every electron that reaches it, so $n(W) \approx 0$.

4. **Current across a thin base.** With $W \ll L_n$ the profile is the straight line of section 01, from $n(0)$ to 0. The current is $qA$ times the diffusion flux $D_n\,n(0)/W$, and since almost no electrons recombine on the way ($\alpha_T \approx 1$), this is the collector current:
<Equation tex="I_C=qAD_n\frac{n(0)}{W}=\underbrace{\frac{qAD_nn_i^2}{N_AW}}_{I_S}\,e^{V_{BE}/V_T}" label="COLLECTOR CURRENT"/>

<p className="assume">The "−1" of the exact diode law, $e^{V_{BE}/V_T} - 1$, is dropped because $e^{V_{BE}/V_T}$ exceeds $10^{10}$ at any useful forward bias.</p>

</div>

Three conclusions follow, each one a later rule of thumb.

1. **$V_{BE}$ controls $I_C$ exponentially**: every factor of ten in current needs $V_T$ ln 10 ≈ 60 mV more base–emitter voltage at room temperature.
2. **The collector voltage barely appears.** As long as the collector junction is reverse-biased, it collects everything regardless of how strongly. This is why the collector behaves like a current source.
3. **$I_S$ depends on geometry and doping**: area A, base width W and base doping $N_A$. It also contains $n_i$², which depends very strongly on temperature (section 06).

<div className="worked-example">

<span className="tiny-label">WORKED EXAMPLE / SIZE THE NUMBERS</span><h3>A small NPN in this model</h3>

<p>Base doping 10¹⁷ cm⁻³ makes $n_{p0}$ = (10¹⁰)²/10¹⁷ = 10³ electrons per cm³. At $V_{BE}$ = 0.65 V the edge density is 10³ × $e^{0.65/0.02585}$ ≈ <strong>8.3 × 10¹³ cm⁻³</strong>, about 1200 times smaller than the hole density, so “low-level injection” holds.</p>

<p>With A = 1000 µm², $D_n$ = 20 cm²/s and a neutral base 0.655 µm wide, $I_S$ = $q A D_n n_i^2/(N_A W)$ ≈ <strong>4.9 × 10⁻¹⁶ A</strong>, and $I_C$ = 40.6 µA at 0.65 V. At 0.70 V it is 279 µA, almost seven times more for 50 mV.</p>

</div>

</section>

<section id="beta">

<div className="section-heading"><span>04</span><h2>Where $\beta$ comes from</h2></div>

If the emitter injected only electrons and none recombined, there would be no base current at all. Two loss mechanisms require the base terminal to supply holes:

**Back-injection into the emitter.** The forward-biased junction also injects holes from the base into the emitter, exactly as in a diode. Their current has the same form as the electron current, with the emitter’s doping and width. The **emitter injection efficiency** $\gamma$ is the fraction of emitter current carried by electrons.

**Recombination in the base.** The stored electrons in the base, a triangle of total charge $Q_B$ = qA n(0) W/2, recombine at rate $Q_B$/$\tau_n$. The **base transport factor** $\alpha_T$ is the fraction of injected electrons that survive the crossing.

Each loss is a current that the base terminal must supply. Express both as fractions of $I_C$.

<div className="derivation">

<span className="tiny-label">DERIVATION / THE CURRENT GAIN FROM TWO LOSSES</span>

1. **Back-injected hole current.** The emitter is also a short region (width $W_E$, doping $N_E$, hole diffusion constant $D_p$). The same junction law and straight-line argument, with the roles of electrons and holes exchanged, give the hole current injected from the base into the emitter:
<Equation tex="I_{pE}=qAD_p\frac{p_{n0}}{W_E}e^{V_{BE}/V_T},\qquad p_{n0}=\frac{n_i^2}{N_E}"/>

2. **Ratio to the collector current.** Divide by $I_C = qAD_n(n_i^2/N_A)(1/W)e^{V_{BE}/V_T}$. The factors $qA$, $n_i^2$ and the exponential cancel:
<Equation tex="\frac{I_{pE}}{I_C}=\frac{D_p\,N_A\,W}{D_n\,N_E\,W_E}"/>

3. **Recombination current.** The excess electrons stored in the base form a triangle of height $n(0)$ and base $W$, so the stored charge is $Q_B = qA\,n(0)W/2$. They recombine at the rate $Q_B/\tau_n$, and every recombination needs one hole from the base terminal:
<Equation tex="I_{rec}=\frac{Q_B}{\tau_n}=\frac{qA\,n(0)\,W}{2\tau_n}"/>

4. **Ratio to the collector current.** Divide by $I_C = qAD_n\,n(0)/W$:
<Equation tex="\frac{I_{rec}}{I_C}=\frac{W^2}{2D_n\tau_n}=\frac{W^2}{2L_n^2}"/>

5. **Add the losses.** The base current is the sum, $I_B \approx I_{pE} + I_{rec}$, so
<Equation tex="\frac{1}{\beta}=\frac{I_B}{I_C}\approx\underbrace{\frac{D_pN_AW}{D_nN_EW_E}}_{\text{back-injection}}+\underbrace{\frac{W^2}{2L_n^2}}_{\text{recombination}}" label="CURRENT GAIN"/>

6. **Relate $\beta$ and $\alpha$.** KCL at the transistor gives $I_E = I_C + I_B$, so
<Equation tex="\alpha=\frac{I_C}{I_E}=\frac{I_C}{I_C+I_C/\beta}=\frac{\beta}{\beta+1},\qquad\beta=\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}"/>
   Equivalently $\alpha = \gamma\,\alpha_T$, the product of the emitter efficiency $\gamma = I_{nE}/(I_{nE}+I_{pE})$ and the transport factor of section 01.

</div>

<div className="worked-example">

<span className="tiny-label">WORKED EXAMPLE / THE TWO LOSSES</span><h3>Emitter 10¹⁹ cm⁻³ and 0.3 µm, base 10¹⁷ cm⁻³ and 0.655 µm</h3>

<p>Back-injection: (2.6 × 10¹⁷ × 0.655)/(20 × 10¹⁹ × 0.3) = <strong>0.00284</strong>. Recombination: 0.655²/(2 × 14²) = <strong>0.00110</strong>. Their sum is 0.00394, so $\beta$ ≈ <strong>254</strong>.</p>

<p>The fractions are $\gamma$ = 0.99717 and $\alpha_T$ = 0.99890, giving $\alpha$ = 0.99607. A collector current that is 99.6% of the emitter current becomes a gain of 254 because $\beta$ = $\alpha$/(1 − $\alpha$) magnifies the small remainder.</p>

</div>

The formula shows the designer’s levers and their costs. A heavier, thicker emitter improves $\gamma$. A thinner base improves both terms: narrowing the metallurgical base from 0.8 µm to 0.5 µm more than doubles $\beta$ in this model (to about 540). Lighter base doping improves $\gamma$—but a lightly doped, thin base has high lateral resistance and is easily “punched through” by the collector depletion region. Doping it three times more heavily drops $\beta$ to about 92. Real devices also use a narrower bandgap in the base or emitter engineering to beat these tradeoffs.

<BaseWidthFigure/>

Because $\beta$ is a ratio of two small loss terms, it varies a great deal between transistors of the same type, with temperature (often rising over part of the operating range), and with current (it falls at low current, where depletion-region recombination adds base current, and at high current, where the injected electrons become comparable to the base doping). **$\beta$ is a poor quantity to design around.** The next chapters build circuits that work whatever $\beta$ is, provided it is large.



<h3 id="book-notes">Why beta measured at one current does not define the whole device</h3>

The transport model explains gain around a chosen carrier density. At very low collector current, leakage and recombination can consume a larger fraction of base drive. At high current density, high-level injection and internal resistive drops disturb the assumptions that made current proportional to a single exponential. Beta often has a useful middle-current region rather than remaining constant over decades.

This matters when scaling a transistor. At the same collector current, a larger die works at lower current density and generally has more junction capacitance. At equal current density, scaling area and current together leaves the required junction voltage approximately unchanged, while absolute transconductance and capacitance both grow. The gain-speed tradeoff therefore cannot be inferred from package size alone.

A practical bias design uses a guaranteed beta range at the intended current and voltage, then checks how the circuit behaves outside the typical value. In a switch, use the specified saturation test current ratio instead of active-region beta. In a low-noise input stage, look at base resistance, current noise and junction capacitance as well as gain. A device chosen to carry amperes can be a poor microamp input transistor even if its printed current gain looks impressive.

<section id="early">

<div className="section-heading"><span>06</span><h2>Early effect, transit time and temperature</h2></div>

### Base-width modulation

The collector depletion region extends partly into the base. Raising the collector–base reverse voltage widens it, which narrows the neutral base W. The edge density n(0) is unchanged, so the profile gets steeper and $I_C$ rises. In the model, the neutral base shrinks from 0.708 µm at $V_{CB}$ = 0 to 0.655 µm at 5 V and 0.582 µm at 20 V, raising $I_C$ from 37.6 µA to 40.6 µA and 45.8 µA.

<div className="derivation">

<span className="tiny-label">DERIVATION / THE EARLY VOLTAGE</span>

1. **Current depends on width.** From the collector-current law, at fixed $V_{BE}$ the only quantity that changes with collector voltage is the neutral base width: $I_C \propto 1/W$.

2. **Differentiate.** Since $V_{BE}$ is fixed, a change in $V_{CE}$ is a change in $V_{CB}$:
<Equation tex="\frac{dI_C}{dV_{CE}}=-\frac{I_C}{W}\,\frac{dW}{dV_{CB}}"/>
   $dW/dV_{CB}$ is negative (more reverse bias, narrower base), so the slope is positive.

3. **Define the Early voltage** as the ratio of the current to its slope, a voltage set by the device's geometry and doping:
<Equation tex="V_A\equiv\frac{I_C}{dI_C/dV_{CE}}=-\frac{W}{dW/dV_{CB}}"/>
   Geometrically, a straight line with this slope through the operating point reaches zero current at $V_{CE} = -V_A$: every curve, whatever its current, extrapolates back to about the same point.

4. **Linear model.** To first order about $V_{CE} = 0$, $I_C$ grows by its slope times $V_{CE}$, and the small-signal output resistance is the reciprocal of that slope:
<Equation tex="I_C\approx I_Se^{V_{BE}/V_T}\left(1+\frac{V_{CE}}{V_A}\right),\qquad r_o=\left(\frac{dI_C}{dV_{CE}}\right)^{-1}=\frac{V_A}{I_C}"/>
   Check against the model: between 0 and 5 V the current rises from 37.6 µA to 40.6 µA, a chord slope of 0.6 µA/V, so $V_A \approx 40.6/0.6$ ≈ 68 V. Between 5 and 20 V the chord gives about 117 V, because depletion widening slows as reverse bias grows. The tangent at the 5 V operating point lies between these, about 87 V: $V_A$ is only approximately constant.

</div>

The collector’s small-signal output resistance $r_o$ is finite. It limits the gain of a single amplifier stage and the quality of a current source (Chapters 31 and 34). If the collector voltage rises far enough that the depletion region reaches the emitter, the base vanishes: **punch-through**, a breakdown mechanism.

### Stored charge and speed

The electron triangle is stored charge, and changing the current means changing that charge.

<div className="derivation">

<span className="tiny-label">DERIVATION / TRANSIT TIME AND THE SPEED LIMIT IT SETS</span>

1. **Stored charge.** Integrate the excess density over the base: a triangle of height $n(0)$ and width $W$, so $Q_B = qA\,n(0)\,W/2$.

2. **Transit time.** Charge divided by the current that carries it is the average time a carrier spends in the base:
<Equation tex="\tau_F=\frac{Q_B}{I_C}=\frac{qA\,n(0)W/2}{qAD_n\,n(0)/W}=\frac{W^2}{2D_n}"/>

3. **Diffusion capacitance.** Since $Q_B = \tau_FI_C$ and $dI_C/dV_{BE} = g_m$, a small change in $V_{BE}$ changes the stored charge by $dQ_B = \tau_F\,g_m\,dV_{BE}$. That is a capacitance $C_{diff} = g_m\tau_F$ between base and emitter.

4. **The current-gain limit.** The small-signal base current must supply both $r_\pi$ (Chapter 30) and this capacitance: $i_b = v_{be}(1/r_\pi + j\omega C_{diff})$, while $i_c = g_mv_{be}$. The current gain is
<Equation tex="\beta(j\omega)=\frac{g_m}{1/r_\pi+j\omega g_m\tau_F}"/>
   At frequencies where the capacitive term dominates, $|\beta| \approx 1/(\omega\tau_F)$, which equals 1 at the **transition frequency**:
<Equation tex="f_T=\frac{1}{2\pi\tau_F}"/>
   Depletion capacitances add to $C_{diff}$, so a real device's $f_T$ is lower: $f_T \lesssim 1/2\pi\tau_F$.

</div>

For W = 0.655 µm, $\tau_F$ ≈ 0.11 ns, which limits this structure to a transition frequency of no more than about 1.5 GHz; junction capacitances lower it further in practice. Changing $I_C$ requires changing $Q_B$, which is why a change in $V_{BE}$ acts as if it charges a capacitance $g_m\tau_F$ (the “diffusion capacitance”) in addition to the depletion capacitances. In saturation the collector junction is forward-biased too and injects its own carriers; the extra stored charge must be removed before the transistor can turn off, which is why saturated switches are slow to release.

### Temperature

$I_S$ contains $n_i^2$, which rises steeply with temperature. Holding $I_C$ fixed, $V_{BE}$ must fall to compensate.

<div className="derivation">

<span className="tiny-label">DERIVATION / THE TEMPERATURE DEPENDENCE OF V<sub>BE</sub></span>

1. **Temperature dependence of $I_S$.** The intrinsic density obeys $n_i^2 \propto T^3e^{-E_g/kT}$. Absorbing the geometry and any weak dependence of $D_n$ into a constant $K$, and writing $E_g/k$ in terms of the extrapolated bandgap voltage $V_{G0}$ ≈ 1.21 V (so $E_g/kT = V_{G0}/V_T$):
<Equation tex="I_S=K\,T^3\,e^{-V_{G0}/V_T}"/>

2. **Invert the diode law** at a fixed collector current $I_C$:
<Equation tex="V_{BE}=V_T\ln\frac{I_C}{I_S}=V_T\ln\frac{I_C}{K}-3V_T\ln T+V_{G0}"/>

3. **Eliminate the unknown constant.** At a reference temperature $T_0$, where $V_{BE} = V_{BE0}$ and $V_T = V_{T0}$, the same equation gives $\ln(I_C/K) = (V_{BE0} - V_{G0})/V_{T0} + 3\ln T_0$.

4. **Substitute back,** using $V_T/V_{T0} = T/T_0$:
<Equation tex="V_{BE}(T)=V_{G0}+\frac{T}{T_0}\left(V_{BE0}-V_{G0}\right)+3V_T\ln\frac{T_0}{T}"/>
   At $T = T_0$ the last term is zero and the expression returns $V_{BE0}$, as it must.

5. **Differentiate and evaluate at $T_0$.** The derivative of $3(kT/q)\ln(T_0/T)$ is $3(k/q)[\ln(T_0/T) - 1]$, which is $-3V_{T0}/T_0$ at $T = T_0$:
<Equation tex="\frac{dV_{BE}}{dT}\Big|_{T_0}=\frac{V_{BE0}-V_{G0}-3V_{T0}}{T_0}"/>

6. **At constant $V_{BE}$ instead.** From $I_C = I_Se^{V_{BE}/V_T}$, a temperature change that would lower $V_{BE}$ by 2.1 mV at constant current instead raises the current by the factor $e^{2.1/25.85}$, about 8.5% per degree.

</div>

With $V_{BE0}$ = 0.65 V at 300 K this gives **−2.1 mV/°C**: the famous rule that a forward-biased silicon junction’s voltage falls about 2 mV per degree at constant current. Conversely, at constant $V_{BE}$ the collector current rises about 8% per degree—a factor of about 64 from 300 K to 360 K in this temperature law. Chapter 29 designs bias circuits that survive this, and Chapter 34 collects these results as the Ebers–Moll rules of thumb.

<span className="tiny-label">WORKED EXAMPLE / WIDTH, CHARGE AND SPEED</span><h3>Halve the neutral base width at fixed injection</h3>

<p>In the short-base model, $I_C\propto1/W$, stored base charge $Q_B\propto W$, and transit time $τ_F\propto W^2$. Halving W therefore doubles collector current, halves stored charge, and quarters transit time. These are simultaneous consequences of the same density triangle.</p>

<p>For $W=0.655$ µm and $D_n=20$ cm²/s, convert units before calculating: $D_n=0.002$ m²/s and $τ_F=(0.655\times10^{-6})^2/(2\times0.002)=0.107$ ns. The transit-only frequency bound is 1.48 GHz. With a 40.6 µA collector current, stored charge is $I_Cτ_F=4.35$ fC.</p>

<p>This is a device-physics estimate, not a guarantee of circuit bandwidth. Junction capacitance, base resistance, wiring and the amplifier’s gain can impose much lower poles.</p>

</div>

<div className="takeaway"><span className="tiny-label">THE IDEA TO KEEP</span><p>The emitter junction sets the electron density at one end of a thin base; the collector removes it at the other. The <em>slope of that line</em> is the collector current, and every transistor rule follows from it.</p></div>

<details>
<summary>1. Why do two separate diodes fail to behave as a transistor?</summary>
<div>

They do not share a thin neutral semiconductor base in which minority carriers injected at one junction can diffuse to the other. A metal interconnection supplies ordinary terminal current but does not provide that transport path.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>2. What does making the base shorter do to current and transit time?</summary>
<div>

At fixed injected edge density, $I_C\propto1/W$ and $τ_F\propto W^2$. A shorter base gives more current and shorter transit time, at the cost of reduced punch-through margin.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>3. Why is β large even though collector current is slightly less than emitter current?</summary>
<div>

$\beta=\alpha/(1-\alpha)$. With α = 0.996, the small difference becomes base current and β is about 249.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>4. Does increasing collector voltage normally increase the emitter-edge density?</summary>
<div>

At fixed base–emitter voltage and temperature, not appreciably in this model. It narrows the neutral base, steepens the profile and increases collector current.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>5. Does −2.1 mV/°C describe fixed voltage or fixed current?</summary>
<div>

Fixed current. If base–emitter voltage is held fixed instead, the increasing saturation current causes a steep rise in collector current.
