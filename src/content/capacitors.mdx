<section id="charge">

<div className="section-heading"><span>01</span><h2>Charge and electric fields</h2></div>

A capacitor has two conductors separated by an insulating region. Moving charge through the external circuit leaves one conductor with an excess of electrons and the other with a corresponding deficit. The component can remain neutral overall while its two plates carry equal and opposite charges.

<ParallelPlateFigure/>

Charge does not have to cross the ideal dielectric for current to flow in the external wires. The changing electric field and dielectric polarization accompany charge redistribution on the conductors. Leakage through a real dielectric is a separate effect.

For a linear capacitor, the signed charge on the terminal we call positive is proportional to its voltage relative to the other terminal:

<Equation tex="Q=CV,\qquad C=\frac{Q}{V}" label="01 / CAPACITANCE IS CHARGE PER VOLT"/>

One farad is one coulomb per volt. A 100 µF capacitor at 5 V has 500 µC of charge on its positive plate and −500 µC on the other. It has not created a net 1,000 µC of charge.

<aside className="margin-note"><strong>A water analogy, used carefully.</strong> Picture a tank divided by a flexible rubber membrane. Pumping water into one side stretches the membrane and pushes the same amount out of the other side; no water crosses. The pressure difference grows with the volume moved (that is $V = Q/C$), and a stiffer membrane means a smaller capacitance. Like all analogies it breaks eventually, but it gets the essentials right: current flows in both leads, nothing crosses the barrier, and "full" means "pushing back hard."</aside>

### Geometry explains the first model

For large parallel plates separated by a uniform dielectric, neglecting fringing fields:

<Equation tex="C=\frac{\varepsilon A}{d},\qquad E\approx\frac{V}{d}"/>

$A$ is the overlapping area, $d$ the spacing, and $\varepsilon$ the dielectric permittivity. More area or greater permittivity increases capacitance; greater separation decreases it. Reducing $d$ also increases electric field for a given voltage, so obtaining more capacitance is not independent of breakdown limits.

<div className="worked-example">

<span className="tiny-label">WORKED EXAMPLE / HOW BIG IS A FARAD?</span><h3>Plates in air</h3>
<p>Two plates 10 cm × 10 cm, 1 mm apart in air ($\varepsilon \approx \varepsilon_0 = 8.85$ pF/m): $C = 8.85\times10^{-12} \times 0.01/0.001 = 88.5$ pF. A farad would need plates about 10 km on a side at the same spacing.</p>
<p>Practical capacitors get their large values three ways: enormous area rolled or stacked into a small volume, dielectrics with permittivity hundreds to thousands of times $\varepsilon_0$, and spacing of micrometres or less. That last point is why a large capacitance in a small package usually comes with a low voltage rating: the field $V/d$ across a thin dielectric grows quickly.</p>

</div>

The familiar constant $C$ assumes fixed geometry and a sufficiently linear dielectric. If charge is a nonlinear function of voltage, the local capacitance is $dQ/dV$. That distinction returns in the real-capacitor chapter.

</section>

<section id="current">

<div className="section-heading"><span>02</span><h2>The current–voltage law</h2></div>

Current is the rate at which charge enters the chosen terminal. Differentiate $Q = CV$, keeping $C$ constant:

<Equation tex="i=\frac{dQ}{dt}=C\frac{dv}{dt}" label="02 / CURRENT CONTROLS THE SLOPE OF VOLTAGE"/>

The sign convention matters: positive current enters the terminal used as the positive voltage reference. Positive current makes that voltage rise; negative current makes it fall.

The same relationship can be written as an integral:

<Equation tex="v(t)=v(t_0)+\frac{1}{C}\int_{t_0}^{t}i(u)\,du"/>

The initial voltage is part of the answer. A capacitor remembers accumulated charge; its present voltage is not determined by the present current alone.

<details className="prerequisite">
<summary>A short bridge to derivatives and integrals</summary>
<div>

<p>The derivative $dv/dt$ is the voltage change per unit time at a particular instant. On a voltage-versus-time graph, it is the tangent slope.</p><p>The integral of current is the signed area beneath the current-versus-time curve. Its units are amperes × seconds = coulombs. Divide that accumulated charge by farads to obtain a voltage change.</p><p>For constant current, the area is a rectangle and no calculus machinery is needed: $\Delta V = I\,\Delta t/C$.</p>

</div>
</details>

### "Open circuit at DC" needs a condition

At steady constant voltage, $dv/dt = 0$, so an ideal capacitor draws zero current. That is the origin of the DC open-circuit rule. During startup or while a current source charges it, the voltage is not constant and the rule does not apply.

A finite current cannot cause an instantaneous voltage jump. A mathematical ideal voltage step directly across an ideal capacitor would require an impulse of current. Actual circuits contain resistance and inductance that determine what happens during the edge.

These two facts are the capacitor's whole personality in circuits: **it resists sudden changes in voltage, and it ignores steady voltages.** The first makes it a reservoir and a smoothing element; the second makes it a DC blocker. Almost every capacitor application in this book is one or the other.

<div className="worked-example">

<span className="tiny-label">WORKED EXAMPLE / A CURRENT PULSE</span><h3>From two volts to three</h3>
<p>A 10 µF capacitor starts at 2 V. A current of +2 mA enters its positive terminal for 5 ms. Added charge is $2\text{ mA}\times5\text{ ms} =$ <strong>10 µC</strong>, so voltage rises by <strong>1 V</strong> to 3 V.</p>
<p>The result depends on the integral of current. A 4 mA pulse lasting 2.5 ms adds the same charge. A subsequent −2 mA pulse lasting 5 ms returns the ideal capacitor to 2 V.</p>

</div>

<section id="energy">

<div className="section-heading"><span>03</span><h2>Where the energy goes</h2></div>

Adding a little charge $dQ$ to a capacitor at voltage $V$ requires energy $dW = V\,dQ$. Because voltage rises as charge accumulates, the energy is not simply the final voltage times the final charge.

<Equation tex="W=\int_0^Q \frac{q}{C}\,dq=\frac{Q^2}{2C}=\frac{1}{2}CV^2" label="03 / ENERGY IN THE ELECTRIC FIELD"/>

Stored energy is nonnegative even when the chosen voltage polarity is negative. Reversing the reference labels changes $Q$ and $V$ signs, not the physical energy.

For the 10 µF example, moving from 2 V to 3 V increases energy by $\tfrac12 \times 10\ \mu\text{F} \times (3^2 - 2^2)\text{ V}^2 =$ **25 µJ**. Using $\tfrac12C(\Delta V)^2$ would give 5 µJ and would be wrong: the capacitor already held energy at the initial voltage.

### Why charging through a resistor loses energy

Connect an initially uncharged capacitor to a constant supply $V_s$ through a resistor and wait for settling. The source transfers total charge $CV_s$ while staying at voltage $V_s$, so it supplies $CV_s^2$. Only $\tfrac12CV_s^2$ ends up in the capacitor. The other half becomes heat in the series resistance.

For 100 µF charged to 5 V, source energy is **2.5 mJ**, stored energy **1.25 mJ**, and dissipated energy **1.25 mJ**. Changing resistance changes the peak current and charging time, but not these final energies under the stated ideal step-charging assumptions.

<ChargingEnergyFigure/>

This is not a universal 50% limit for every charging method. A varying source or an energy-converting circuit can follow a different energy path. Charging from a source that ramps up gradually, so the resistor never sees much voltage, loses far less; switching converters (Chapter 25) exploit this.

<div className="worked-example">

<span className="tiny-label">WORKED EXAMPLE / A SUPERCAPACITOR BACKUP</span><h3>How long will 1 F keep a clock running?</h3>
<p>A real-time clock draws 5 mA and works down to 3 V. A 1 F supercapacitor is charged to 5 V. At constant current, $dv/dt = I/C = 5$ mV/s, so the time to fall 2 V is $C\,\Delta V/I = 1 \times 2/0.005 =$ <strong>400 s</strong>, under seven minutes.</p>
<p>Energy gives the same picture from another side: the capacitor releases $\tfrac12 \times 1 \times (5^2 - 3^2) = 8$ J between 5 V and 3 V, and the load uses about $4\text{ V}\times5\text{ mA} = 20$ mW on average, so $8/0.02 = 400$ s. Note that 4.5 J, 36% of the original 12.5 J, is still stranded below 3 V. Capacitors deliver their energy over a wide voltage range, which is why a converter that works down to low input voltages extracts much more from them.</p>

</div>

<section id="explore">

<div className="section-heading"><span>04</span><h2>Charge at a constant rate</h2></div>

An ideal current source is especially revealing: it fixes the charge-transfer rate and lets capacitor voltage respond. Unlike resistor charging from a fixed voltage source, the slope stays constant.

<CapacitorLab client:load/>

<p className="caption">Constant-current charging from zero volts. The source is ideal and imposes no voltage-compliance limit; the plot is a model rather than a component rating.</p>

At the default 1 mA and 100 µF, the slope is 10 V/s. After 100 ms, voltage is 1 V, plate charge is 100 µC, and energy is 50 µJ. Notice that doubling the time doubles charge but quadruples energy: later charge is delivered at a higher voltage.

Constant-current charging is how many real circuits make linear ramps: the sawtooth sweep of an oscilloscope, the timing ramp of a 555-style timer with a current source, and the integrating analog-to-digital converter all rely on $v = It/C$.

</section>

<section id="connections">

<div className="section-heading"><span>05</span><h2>Series, parallel, and sharing</h2></div>

Capacitors in parallel share the same voltage. Their charges add, so their capacitances add:

<Equation tex="C_{\parallel}=C_1+C_2+\cdots"/>

For ideal series capacitors with no initial net charge on the isolated middle node, equal charge magnitudes appear on each capacitor. Their voltages add:

<Equation tex="\frac{1}{C_{\mathrm{series}}}=\frac{1}{C_1}+\frac{1}{C_2}+\cdots"/>

These are the resistor rules turned around. Parallel plates simply add area; series capacitors add spacing.

<SeriesParallelCapFigure/>

With 10 µF and 22 µF in series, equivalent capacitance is **6.875 µF**. Across 12 V, equal charge gives 8.25 V on the smaller capacitor and 3.75 V on the larger. Initial charge and real leakage can change the voltage division; series connection does not automatically guarantee equal sharing or a usable sum of voltage ratings. Supercapacitor stacks use balancing resistors across each cell for exactly this reason.

### Charge sharing is an energy problem too

<ChargeSharingFigure/>

Connect an isolated 100 µF capacitor at 5 V in parallel, with matching polarity, to another 100 µF capacitor initially at 0 V. Charge conservation gives a final voltage of 2.5 V.

Initial energy is 1.25 mJ; final total energy is 0.625 mJ. The remaining 0.625 mJ leaves the ideal capacitor storage, typically becoming heat during the transient in a real interconnect. Zero resistance and zero inductance would make the instantaneous connection model singular; they do not make energy conservation optional.

### A useful application: short-term supply support

<HoldUpFigure/>

If a load draws an extra 20 mA for 50 µs and allowable ideal capacitive droop is 0.1 V, the minimum capacitance from charge balance is $I\,\Delta t/\Delta V =$ **10 µF**. This is only the capacitive part of the answer. ESR causes an additional current-dependent drop, ESL matters during the edge, and effective capacitance can differ from its marked value (Chapter 9).



<h3 id="book-notes">Read the marking before using the number</h3>

On a capacitor using the common three-digit picofarad code, the first two digits are significant figures and the third is a decimal multiplier: **104** means $10\times10^4$ pF = 100 nF; **473** means 47 nF. A printed decimal, a legacy direct-picofarad marking and a manufacturer-specific code can mean something different. Many small ceramic chips are unmarked, so a photograph or package size is insufficient to identify their value.

Capacitance, tolerance, voltage rating and polarity are separate pieces of information. “104” does not specify any of the last three. The schematic value may omit units, but the parts list should not. Measure an unknown part with an appropriate test frequency and retain its provenance before assigning it to a precision or power role.

A useful assembly check is to ask what failure the wrong decade would cause. If a 10 kΩ timing resistor was intended to charge 100 nF, the time constant is 1 ms. Substituting 10 nF gives 0.1 ms; substituting 1 µF gives 10 ms. The circuit can appear to work while its timing is wrong by tenfold. This is also why measuring capacitance after assembly may be misleading: other parallel capacitors and semiconductor junctions contribute to the reading.

<section id="check">

<div className="section-heading"><span>06</span><h2>Check your understanding</h2></div>

<details>
<summary>1. A capacitor's current is zero at some instant. Must its voltage be zero?</summary>
<div>

No. Zero current means zero *slope*: the voltage is momentarily not changing. It can be holding any value it accumulated earlier, as at 7 ms in the pulse example.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>2. You double the voltage on a capacitor. By what factor do its charge and energy change?</summary>
<div>

Charge doubles ($Q = CV$); energy quadruples ($W = \tfrac12CV^2$). The second half of the charge is pushed in against twice the voltage.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>3. Two 10 µF, 16 V capacitors in series: what capacitance, and is the combination safe on 30 V?</summary>
<div>

5 µF. Ideally each takes 15 V, under its rating. In practice unequal leakage and tolerance can push one well above 16 V; without balancing resistors the combination is not reliably safe on 30 V.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>4. Why does a capacitor charged through 1 Ω waste the same energy as one charged through 1 kΩ?</summary>
<div>

The total charge moved and the source voltage are the same, so the source delivers $CV_s^2$ and the capacitor stores half regardless. The smaller resistor carries a thousand times the peak current for a thousandth of the time; the integral of $I^2R$ comes out the same.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>5. A 100 mA load must survive a 2 ms supply dropout with no more than 0.5 V of droop. How much capacitance?</summary>
<div>

$C = I\,\Delta t/\Delta V = 0.1 \times 0.002/0.5 = 400$ µF, before allowing for tolerance, ESR and aging. A 470 µF part, or larger, would be the practical choice.

</div>
</details>
