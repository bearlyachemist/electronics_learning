<div className="opening-grid">

<div><span className="tiny-label">A PRACTICAL QUESTION</span><p className="opening-question">An emitter follower can offer three-quarters of a megohm at its base, but its bias divider drags the input impedance down to 64 kΩ. Can the divider be made to vanish for signals while still setting the DC bias?</p></div>

<div>

The current through a resistor depends on the voltage *across* it. If the voltage at its far end rises and falls with the near end, little current flows, however small the resistor is. The resistor then behaves, for signals, as if it were much larger.

Arranging this deliberately—driving the far end of an impedance with a copy of the signal—is called **bootstrapping**, after pulling oneself up by one’s own bootstraps. It is the positive-gain counterpart of the Miller effect in Chapter 41, and it reappears in amplifiers, output stages, guarded cables and instrumentation.

</div>

</div>

<section id="problem">

<div className="section-heading"><span>01</span><h2>The divider problem</h2></div>

Chapter 29 biased an audio follower with $R_1$ = 130 kΩ and $R_2$ = 150 kΩ on 15 V, with $R_E$ = 7.5 kΩ at about 1 mA. Looking into the base, the transistor offers ($\beta$ + 1)($r_e$ + $R_E$) ≈ 760 kΩ, but the divider sits directly across the input, so the source sees 69.6 kΩ ∥ 760 kΩ ≈ **64 kΩ**. The stiffness rule forced the divider to be small, and the divider then set the input impedance.

The divider must stay small for DC. The question is whether it can be made large for signals.

</section>

<section id="principle">

<div className="section-heading"><span>02</span><h2>The principle: 1/(1 − G)</h2></div>

Take an impedance Z between a node A, which carries the signal v, and a node B. If node B is driven with a scaled copy of the same signal, $v_B$ = G v, then by Ohm's law the current through Z depends only on the voltage *across* Z, which is $v - Gv$. The source driving node A must supply that current, so the impedance it sees is $v$ divided by it:

<Equation tex="i=\frac{v-Gv}{Z}=\frac{v(1-G)}{Z}\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad Z_{\mathrm{eff}}=\frac{v}{i}=\frac{Z}{1-G}" label="BOOTSTRAPPED IMPEDANCE"/>

- **G between 0 and 1** (a follower’s output): Z looks larger. With G = 0.99, a hundred times larger; with G = 0.997, about 300 times.
- **G = 1**: no current at all; Z is invisible.
- **G greater than 1**: the current reverses—a negative resistance, and the recipe for an oscillator.
- **G negative** (an inverting amplifier): Z looks *smaller*, Z/(1 + |G|). For a capacitor, that is the Miller effect.

Applied to a capacitance, the result reads $C_{\mathrm{eff}}$ = C(1 − G): a bootstrapped capacitance shrinks.

</section>

<section id="follower">

<div className="section-heading"><span>03</span><h2>The bootstrapped follower</h2></div>

<BootstrapFigure/>

Split the bias path: the divider $R_1$, $R_2$ sets a DC voltage at a node M, and a resistor $R_3$ carries that voltage from M to the base. For DC, nothing has changed except a small extra drop across $R_3$ from the base current. Now add a capacitor $C_B$ from the emitter to M.

For signals, M now follows the emitter, which follows the base with gain G = $R_E$/($R_E$ + $r_e$) ≈ 0.997 (Chapter 28's follower gain; a large $C_B$ makes $v_M = v_e$). $R_3$ runs from the base, at $v$, to M, at $Gv$, which is exactly the bootstrap configuration with $Z = R_3$:

<Equation tex="Z_{R_3,\mathrm{eff}}=\frac{R_3}{1-G}\approx\frac{47\ \mathrm{k\Omega}}{0.0034}\approx14\ \mathrm{M\Omega}"/>

The divider has effectively disappeared from the input. What remains is the transistor itself. But the divider has not vanished from the circuit: through $C_B$ it now loads the *emitter*, so for signals the emitter sees $R_E$ ∥ $R_1$ ∥ $R_2$ = 7.5 kΩ ∥ 69.6 kΩ = 6.8 kΩ, and the base sees ($\beta$ + 1) times that. KCL at the base node: the source current divides between $R_3$ and the transistor's base, two parallel paths:

<Equation tex="Z_{\mathrm{in}}\approx\frac{R_3}{1-G}\ \Big\|\ (\beta+1)\left(r_e+R_E\parallel R_1\parallel R_2\right)\approx14\ \mathrm{M\Omega}\parallel0.69\ \mathrm{M\Omega}\approx0.65\ \mathrm{M\Omega}"/>

The lab’s nodal solution gives **651 kΩ**, ten times the plain divider’s 64 kΩ. The input impedance is now limited by the transistor’s $\beta$, as in an unbiased follower, not by the bias network. A Darlington follower would push it into the megohms.

Bootstrapping fails at low frequencies, where $C_B$’s impedance becomes comparable to the resistances around node M. With 10 µF, the improvement fades below a few hertz.

</section>

<section id="others">

<div className="section-heading"><span>05</span><h2>Other bootstraps</h2></div>

### A collector load that acts like a current source

A common-emitter stage’s gain is limited by $R_C$ (Chapter 32). Split $R_C$ into two resistors and connect a capacitor from their junction to the output of a follower that the stage drives. The junction then moves with the collector, so the upper resistor carries almost no signal current and looks enormous. The stage’s effective load becomes the follower’s input impedance, and the gain rises toward $g_m$ times that. Classic discrete audio amplifiers used exactly this bootstrapped collector load in the driver stage of their push–pull outputs, which also let the output swing closer to the supply.

### Guards and driven shields

A coaxial cable between a high-impedance sensor and an amplifier adds capacitance: 2 m at 100 pF/m is 200 pF, which with a 1 MΩ source gives a low-pass corner of only 800 Hz. Drive the cable’s shield with a follower copy of the signal (G ≈ 0.99), and the capacitance between center conductor and shield carries 1% of the current it would otherwise: effectively 2 pF, raising the corner to about 80 kHz. The same idea, a **guard ring** driven at the signal voltage, surrounds sensitive inputs on circuit boards so that leakage across the board surface finds no voltage difference to act on.

### Inside a transistor

A follower’s own $C_π$ sits between base and emitter, both of which move together, so it too is bootstrapped: the input capacitance of a follower is $C_μ$ plus a small fraction of $C_π$. Chapter 41 contrasts this with the common-emitter stage, where $C_μ$ is multiplied instead.

</section>

<section id="limits">

<div className="section-heading"><span>06</span><h2>Limits</h2></div>

**Positive feedback.** A bootstrap is positive feedback around a gain just under 1. If G reaches or exceeds 1—through added gain, or phase shifts at high frequency that make the copy arrive late—the effective impedance becomes negative and the circuit can oscillate. Bootstrapped stages need gain margin and sometimes small damping resistors.

**Frequency range.** The capacitor sets a lower limit, as in the lab. At high frequencies, the follower’s gain falls and acquires phase shift, so the improvement fades there too.

**Noise.** A bootstrapped resistor still generates the thermal noise current of its real value. A resistor's noise current density is $\sqrt{4kT/R}$, set by its real value. A 14 MΩ *apparent* impedance built from a 47 kΩ resistor therefore injects the noise current of 47 kΩ, $\sqrt{14\text{ M}/47\text{ k}}$ ≈ 17 times more than a genuine 14 MΩ resistor would. Bootstrapping fixes loading, not noise.

**What remains.** Bootstrapping removes one limit and exposes the next. In the follower that limit is $\beta$ times the emitter load; in a guarded cable it is the follower’s gain accuracy; in a collector load it is the next stage’s input impedance.

### Phase is part of the bootstrap gain

Use the complex follower transfer $G(jω)$, rather than only its magnitude. For $G=|G|e^{-jφ}$, the bootstrap result $Z_{eff}/Z = 1/(1-G)$ still holds with phasors, so the multiplier's magnitude is $1/|1 - G|$. Write $1 - G = 1 - |G|\cos φ + j|G|\sin φ$ and take its magnitude, using $\cos^2φ + \sin^2φ = 1$:

<Equation tex="|1-G|^2=(1-|G|\cos φ)^2+|G|^2\sin^2φ=1+|G|^2-2|G|\cos φ"/>

Therefore

<Equation tex="\left|\frac{Z_{eff}}{Z}\right|=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+|G|^2-2|G|\cos φ}}"/>

At $|G|=0.99$ and zero phase lag, the multiplier is 100. At 5° it is only 11.5; at 20° it is 2.89. A follower that has almost unity magnitude can therefore stop bootstrapping effectively once its phase changes. The impedance acquires a reactive component, which matters when it interacts with other capacitances or a feedback loop.

<BootstrapPhaseFigure/>

### Check the capacitor at both ends of the band

The capacitor must couple the emitter to the divider midpoint at the lowest intended frequency. A quick conservative comparison is $|X_C|\ll R_1\parallel R_2$, though an accurate corner also includes the base-feed resistor, transistor input impedance and emitter loading. For 130 kΩ and 150 kΩ, the divider resistance is 69.6 kΩ. A 10 µF capacitor has 796 Ω reactance at 20 Hz, about 1.1% of that value.

At DC, the capacitor is open and cannot supply bias current. At high frequency, follower phase and parasitic capacitance limit the boost. The useful result is a high impedance over a specified band, not an infinite resistance at every frequency.

<div className="takeaway"><span className="tiny-label">THE IDEA TO KEEP</span><p>Drive the far end of an impedance with a copy of the signal and it looks <em>1/(1 − G) times larger</em>. With G close to 1, resistors vanish and capacitances shrink; with G negative, the same algebra gives the Miller effect.</p></div>

<section id="check">

<div className="section-heading"><span>07</span><h2>Check your understanding</h2></div>

<details>
<summary>1. What voltage determines current through the bootstrapped resistor?</summary>
<div>

The difference between its two moving end voltages. If both move nearly together, that difference and the signal current are small.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>2. Does the bootstrap capacitor change the DC divider ratio?</summary>
<div>

An ideal capacitor is open at DC. The divider, base-feed resistor and base current still determine bias.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>3. Is gain magnitude 0.99 enough to claim a 100× impedance boost?</summary>
<div>

Only with negligible phase error. The denominator is the complex quantity 1 − G; phase lag can reduce the boost sharply.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>4. Why does a heavy emitter load reduce the input impedance?</summary>
<div>

It reduces the load transformed through the transistor and can reduce follower gain, increasing the signal voltage across the bootstrapped resistor.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>5. Can bootstrapping cancel leakage and protection-diode current?</summary>
<div>

No. Those are additional current paths whose dependence on voltage, temperature and frequency must be included separately.

</div>
</details>
