<div className="opening-grid">

<div><span className="tiny-label">A PRACTICAL QUESTION</span><p className="opening-question">A sensor has 10 kΩ of output resistance. Connected straight to a 1 kΩ load, it delivers one eleventh of its voltage. Can a single transistor fix that without changing the voltage?</p></div>

<div>

Connect the signal to a transistor’s base, take the output from its emitter, and tie the collector to the supply. The output then sits one diode drop below the input and follows it. That sounds useless—there is no voltage gain—until you look at the currents. The load’s current comes from the supply through the collector; the source only provides the base current, $\beta$ + 1 times smaller.

The emitter follower is therefore an **impedance converter**: it presents a high impedance to its source and a low impedance to its load. It is the most common transistor stage after the switch.

</div>

</div>

<section id="follow">

<div className="section-heading"><span>01</span><h2>The output follows the input</h2></div>

With the base–emitter junction conducting, rule 2 of Chapter 27 gives:

<Equation tex="V_E=V_B-V_{BE}\approx V_B-0.65\ \mathrm V"/>

Changes are what matter for signals. As long as the transistor stays active, $V_{BE}$ barely changes (Chapter 34 makes “barely” precise), so

<Equation tex="\Delta V_E\approx\Delta V_B,\qquad G=\frac{\Delta V_E}{\Delta V_B}\approx1"/>

The voltage gain is slightly less than 1 but the **current** gain is $\beta$ + 1, so the power gain is large. The follower needs some resistance or current source from the emitter to a lower voltage, so that a current flows and the base–emitter junction stays forward-biased.

</section>

<section id="impedance">

<div className="section-heading"><span>02</span><h2>Impedances looking in and looking back</h2></div>

### Input impedance

Let the emitter drive a total resistance R (the emitter resistor in parallel with the load, for signals). A change $\Delta V_B$ moves the emitter by the same amount, so the emitter current changes by $\Delta V_B$/R. The base current changes $\beta$ + 1 times less:

<Equation tex="\Delta I_B=\frac{\Delta I_E}{\beta+1}=\frac{\Delta V_B}{(\beta+1)R}\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad Z_{\mathrm{in}}=(\beta+1)R" label="LOOKING INTO THE BASE"/>

The load appears magnified by $\beta$ + 1 when seen from the base.

### Output impedance

Now drive the base from a source with Thévenin resistance $R_s$ and ask how the emitter voltage responds to a change in load current. A change $\Delta I_E$ needs a base-current change $\Delta I_E$/($\beta$ + 1), which flows through $R_s$ and lowers the base (and emitter) by $\Delta I_E$ $R_s$/($\beta$ + 1):

<Equation tex="Z_{\mathrm{out}}=\frac{R_s}{\beta+1}" label="LOOKING INTO THE EMITTER"/>

The source impedance appears divided by $\beta$ + 1 when seen from the emitter. The transistor’s own intrinsic emitter resistance, $r_e$ = $V_T$/$I_E$ ≈ 25 Ω at 1 mA, adds in series (Chapter 30); for now it is small compared with the effect of $R_s$.

<div className="worked-example">

<span className="tiny-label">WORKED EXAMPLE / THE OPENING QUESTION</span><h3>10 kΩ source, 1 kΩ load, $\beta$ = 100</h3>

<p>Connected directly, the load receives 1/(1 + 10) = <strong>9.1%</strong> of the source voltage.</p>

<p>With a follower, the base sees ($\beta$ + 1) × 1 kΩ = 101 kΩ, so the base receives 101/(101 + 10) = <strong>91%</strong> of the source voltage. The emitter, looking back, has an output impedance of 10 kΩ/101 ≈ 99 Ω (plus $r_e$), which the 1 kΩ load hardly loads. One transistor turned a 9% connection into a roughly 80–90% one, depending on $r_e$ and bias.</p>

</div>

Both results are the same fact seen from opposite sides: currents in the emitter circuit are $\beta$ + 1 times the currents in the base circuit, while voltage changes are equal. Impedance is voltage over current, so it scales by $\beta$ + 1.

### Keep the two gains separate

The base-to-emitter gain and the source-to-emitter gain differ when the source has resistance. With $R=R_E\parallel R_L$ and negligible Early effect, the hybrid-π model gives:

<Equation tex="Z_{in}=r_π+(\beta+1)R,\qquad A_{BE}=\frac{(\beta+1)R}{r_π+(\beta+1)R},\qquad A_{SE}=A_{BE}\frac{Z_{in}}{R_s+Z_{in}}" />

The first factor describes following; the second describes source loading. Count each once. Using the input divider and then applying a second output divider based on the same source resistance would double-count one effect.

<section id="explore">

<div className="section-heading"><span>03</span><h2>Explore swing and clipping</h2></div>

An NPN follower can **source** as much current as the transistor allows: when the input rises, the transistor simply conducts harder. It cannot **sink** current. When the input falls, the transistor turns off and only the emitter resistor pulls the output down. The lab drives a follower from ±10 V supplies with the emitter resistor returned to −10 V and a load to ground.

<FollowerLab client:load/>

<p className="caption">Figure 1. A DC-coupled follower. Negative peaks flatten at the voltage where the emitter resistor and load form a divider from −10 V with the transistor off.</p>

With the transistor off, the emitter is pulled down to the divider voltage

<Equation tex="V_{\mathrm{floor}}=-V_{EE}\,\frac{R_L}{R_E+R_L}"/>

With $R_E$ = 3.3 kΩ and a 1 kΩ load on ±12 V supplies, that is −12 × 1/4.3 = −2.79 V, however far the input goes. To swing to −6 V into 1 kΩ, $R_E$ must carry the load’s 6 mA from the remaining 6 V: $R_E$ ≤ 1 kΩ.

That fix has a cost. With $R_E$ = 1 kΩ to −10 V and the output resting at 0 V, 10 mA flows continuously: 0.1 W in the transistor and 0.1 W in $R_E$, to deliver a signal whose average power may be far smaller. The **push–pull** stage (a later chapter) pairs an NPN follower that sources with a PNP follower that sinks, so that neither has to idle at the peak load current.

</section>

<section id="practice-followers">

<div className="section-heading"><span>04</span><h2>Followers in practice</h2></div>

**AC coupling.** A follower driving a capacitively coupled load must be biased so that the emitter sits near the middle of the available swing. Biasing is the subject of Chapter 29.

**Reverse base–emitter breakdown.** Suppose a follower charges a large capacitor to +8 V, and then its base is suddenly pulled to −5 V. The capacitor holds the emitter at +8 V, so the base–emitter junction is reverse-biased by 13 V, well past a typical $V_{EBO}$ of 6 V. A diode across the base–emitter junction (cathode to base) prevents this.

**Followers inside other circuits.** A follower is placed after a high-impedance stage to drive a load, before a stage whose input would load a source, and as the output stage of regulators and amplifiers. Its high input impedance and low output impedance are why it appears so often.

**Stability.** A follower driving a capacitive load from a source with inductance can oscillate at high frequency. A small resistor (tens of ohms) in series with the base, or with the load, usually cures it. The cause—phase shift inside a feedback loop—is a later topic.

</section>

<section id="regulator">

<div className="section-heading"><span>05</span><h2>The follower as a voltage regulator</h2></div>

Chapter 15’s zener regulator wastes current: the resistor must supply the full load current plus a minimum zener current at the lowest input voltage. Put a follower after the zener and the zener only has to supply base current.

<FollowerRegulatorFigure/>

<div className="worked-example">

<span className="tiny-label">WORKED EXAMPLE / 100 mA FROM AN UNREGULATED 15 V</span><h3>A 10 V zener and a follower</h3>

<p>The output is about 10 − 0.65 = <strong>9.35 V</strong>. At 100 mA and a worst-case $\beta$ of 50, the base needs 0.1 A/51 = 1.96 mA.</p>

<p>Allowing 5 mA of minimum zener current at the input’s ripple trough of 13 V: R ≤ (13 − 10)/(5 + 1.96) mA = 431 Ω; choose <strong>390 Ω</strong>. Without the follower, the same resistor would have to pass 105 mA, about 29 Ω, and with no load at a 15 V peak the zener would dissipate (15 − 10)/29 Ω × 10 V ≈ 1.7 W.</p>

<p>The pass transistor now dissipates (15 − 9.35) V × 0.1 A ≈ <strong>0.57 W</strong> on average, needing a suitable package. A capacitor across the zener reduces its noise and the ripple on the base.</p>

</div>

The output still changes with load current through the transistor’s $r_e$ and the zener’s dynamic resistance divided by $\beta$, and with temperature through $V_{BE}$. Feedback regulators (Chapter 15’s preview, and later chapters) compare the output with a reference and correct both.

</section>

<section id="limits">

<div className="section-heading"><span>06</span><h2>PNP followers and limits</h2></div>

A PNP follower is the mirror image: emitter one $V_{BE}$ above the base, collector to the negative supply, able to sink current but not source it. Pairing an NPN and a PNP follower gives push–pull operation, with a crossover region near zero where neither conducts strongly—another later topic.

The first-model results above omit three effects that matter for precise work:

- **$r_e$.** The emitter has an intrinsic resistance $V_T$/$I_E$ in series with $R_s$/($\beta$ + 1). At 1 mA it is about 26 Ω; at 10 µA it is 2.6 kΩ. A follower biased at very low current is a poor driver.
- **$V_{BE}$ changes with current.** By about 60 mV per decade of current (Chapter 34). For large swings into a load, the emitter does not follow the base exactly; the gain is R/(R + $r_e$), slightly less than 1, and slightly signal-dependent.
- **$V_{BE}$ changes with temperature.** By about −2.1 mV/°C, so a DC-coupled follower’s output drifts upward as it warms.

<div className="worked-example">

<span className="tiny-label">WORKED DESIGN / BUDGET THE NEGATIVE PEAK</span><h3>±4 V into 1 kΩ from ±10 V</h3>

<p>At the −4 V output peak the grounded load injects 4 mA into the output node. A 1 kΩ pull-down resistor to −10 V removes 6 mA, leaving 2 mA to flow through the NPN. The transistor therefore remains conducting. At the +4 V peak it must supply 14 mA through the pull-down plus 4 mA to the load: 18 mA total.</p>

<p>Those peak currents set base-drive requirements. At β = 50, the positive peak needs about $18/51=0.353$ mA of base current. A 10 kΩ signal source would lose 3.53 V in delivering it, so a large ideal-source swing does not prove that a weak sensor can drive the circuit.</p>

<p>At zero output, the 1 kΩ pull-down dissipates 100 mW and the transistor roughly another 100 mW. Check both standing power and peak current; the follower’s current gain is supplied by its power rails.</p>

</div>

<div className="takeaway"><span className="tiny-label">THE IDEA TO KEEP</span><p>A follower copies a voltage while multiplying impedance by $\beta$ + 1 looking in and dividing it by $\beta$ + 1 looking back. It <em>sources easily but sinks only through its emitter resistor</em>.</p></div>

<p className="reference-note">Coverage: The Art of Electronics §§2.2.3–2.2.4. Biasing a follower for AC signals is in Chapter 29; the $r_e$ correction is in Chapters 30 and 34.</p>

</section>

<section id="check">

<div className="section-heading"><span>07</span><h2>Check your understanding</h2></div>

<details>
<summary>1. Does a follower have unity gain from the original signal source?</summary>
<div>

Not necessarily. Its base-to-emitter gain is near unity, but finite source resistance and base current create an additional input divider.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>2. Why can its negative swing clip before its positive swing?</summary>
<div>

The NPN supplies current readily, but the pull-down must remove current at negative peaks. Once the required transistor current would be negative, it turns off and the passive divider sets the output.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>3. What can a capacitor held above the base do to the base–emitter junction?</summary>
<div>

It can reverse-bias that junction when the base falls. A protection diode with anode at emitter and cathode at base clamps the reverse voltage, if the surrounding circuit can carry its current.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>4. Is the zener follower output exactly the zener voltage?</summary>
<div>

No. It is lower by the operating base–emitter voltage and varies with current, temperature and reference impedance.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>5. Why specify bias current when quoting output impedance?</summary>
<div>

The intrinsic contribution is approximately $V_T/I_E$. At low bias it can be much larger than the transformed source resistance.

</div>
</details>
