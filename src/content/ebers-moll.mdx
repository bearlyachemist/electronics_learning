<div><span className="tiny-label">A PRACTICAL QUESTION</span><p className="opening-question">Two identical transistors share the same base–emitter voltage. Why do they carry the same current—and why might one of them carry 9% more?</p></div>

<div>

The first model (Chapter 27) said $V_{BE}$ ≈ 0.65 V and $I_C$ = $\beta I_B$. That was enough to bias circuits and design switches. It could not explain $r_e$, the distortion of a bypassed amplifier, the temperature drift of a bias point, or why a current source is not perfect.

The Ebers–Moll model replaces those approximations with the physics of Chapter 26 in compact form. It is the model behind the small-signal parameters, the source of the designer’s rules of thumb, and the basis of the current mirror, the first circuit in this course that *depends* on the exponential law rather than tolerating it.

</div>

<section id="law">

<div className="section-heading"><span>01</span><h2>The exponential law</h2></div>

In the active region:

<Equation tex="I_C=I_S\left(e^{V_{BE}/V_T}-1\right)\left(1+\frac{V_{CE}}{V_A}\right),\qquad I_B=\frac{I_C}{\beta},\qquad V_T=\frac{kT}{q}\approx25.3\ \mathrm{mV}\ (20\,^\circ\mathrm C)" label="EBERS–MOLL, ACTIVE REGION"/>

$I_S$ is the saturation current (around 10⁻¹⁴ A for a small-signal transistor, strongly temperature-dependent); $V_A$ is the Early voltage. At normal operating currents the −1 is negligible. The collector current is controlled by $V_{BE}$; the base current is a fraction of it. That is the transconductance view of Chapter 33, now quantitative.

### All four regions

The full model treats the transistor as two interacting diodes. A forward “transport” current flows from collector to emitter when the base–emitter junction is forward-biased, a reverse one when the base–collector junction is, and each junction draws its own base current:

<Equation tex="I_C=I_S\left(e^{V_{BE}/V_T}-e^{V_{BC}/V_T}\right)-\frac{I_S}{\beta_R}\left(e^{V_{BC}/V_T}-1\right)"/>

<Equation tex="I_B=\frac{I_S}{\beta_F}\left(e^{V_{BE}/V_T}-1\right)+\frac{I_S}{\beta_R}\left(e^{V_{BC}/V_T}-1\right)"/>

$\beta_F$ is the normal current gain; $\beta_R$, the gain with emitter and collector interchanged, is small (often 1–10) because the transistor is built asymmetrically (Chapter 26). With the collector junction reverse-biased, the equations reduce to the active-region law. With both junctions forward-biased—saturation—the collector junction injects too, and solving for $V_{CE}$ at a forced ratio $\beta_{\mathrm{forced}}$ = $I_C$/$I_B$ gives

<Equation tex="V_{CE(\mathrm{sat})}=V_T\ln\frac{1/\alpha_R+\beta_{\mathrm{forced}}/\beta_R}{1-\beta_{\mathrm{forced}}/\beta_F},\qquad \alpha_R=\frac{\beta_R}{1+\beta_R}"/>

With $\beta_F$ = 150, $\beta_R$ = 2 and a forced $\beta$ of 10, $V_{CE(\mathrm{sat})}$ ≈ 50 mV, independent of the collector current in this ideal model. Real transistors add the collector’s ohmic resistance, which is why datasheets quote 0.1–0.3 V at substantial currents.

They interlock. At constant $V_{BE}$, a 1 °C rise is equivalent to raising $V_{BE}$ by 2.1 mV, so $I_C$ rises by 2.1/25.85 ≈ 8%. A factor of two in current needs 18 mV ($V_T$ ln 2). Two transistors whose $V_{BE}$ differ by 1 mV carry currents differing by 4%. With $I_S$ = 10⁻¹⁴ A, 1 mA needs $V_{BE}$ = 0.655 V and 10 mA needs 0.714 V—so “0.6 to 0.7 V” is a statement about typical currents, not a law.

<section id="revisited">

<div className="section-heading"><span>04</span><h2>Follower and common-emitter revisited</h2></div>

### The emitter follower

Because $V_{BE}$ changes by 60 mV per decade, an emitter follower’s output does not follow its input exactly. Its small-signal output impedance contains $r_e$, so the gain into a total emitter resistance R is R/(R + $r_e$). A follower at 1 mA driving 1 kΩ has gain 0.975; at 100 µA it would have 0.80. A follower intended to drive low impedances must run at enough current that $r_e$ is small compared with the load. Its distortion is small for the same reason: the transistor’s varying $v_{be}$ is only a small part of the output when R ≫ $r_e$.

### The common-emitter stage

The maximum gain of a bypassed stage is −$g_mR$_C = −$V_{RC}$/$V_T$ (Chapter 32): it depends only on the DC voltage across the collector resistor. A stage running from 15 V with 7.5 V across $R_C$ cannot have a gain above about 290, whatever transistor or current is chosen. Doubling the current while halving $R_C$ keeps the same gain.

The exponential also predicts the distortion. A base–emitter signal of 5 mV peak multiplies the collector current by $e^{±5/25.85}$: up 21.3%, down 17.6%. The output is asymmetric even though the input is a pure sine; the asymmetry is second-harmonic distortion. Emitter degeneration fixes it by making $v_{be}$ a small fraction of the input. With $R_E$ = 1 kΩ and $r_e$ = 29 Ω, a 100 mV input puts only about 3 mV across the junction itself.

### Bias drift

At a fixed base voltage, $V_{BE}$ falls 2.1 mV/°C, so a common-emitter stage with $V_E$ = 1 V drifts by 0.21%/°C, as found in Chapter 29. A stage with its emitter grounded and a fixed base voltage would drift by 8%/°C. That is the quantitative reason for emitter resistors in bias circuits.

</section>

<section id="perfect">

<div className="section-heading"><span>05</span><h2>An aside: the perfect transistor</h2></div>

What would an ideal three-terminal amplifying device look like? Ebers–Moll suggests an answer: a perfect transconductance device. Its control terminal would draw no current, its output current would depend only on the control voltage (no Early effect), its characteristic would not depend on temperature, and its transconductance would be as large as possible.

The ideal BJT gives high transconductance per unit current: $g_m/I_C=1/V_T$. Compared with a long-channel MOSFET in strong inversion at typical overdrive, that ratio is usually larger; weak-inversion MOS operation requires a separate comparison. Its imperfections are the base current (a finite $\beta$), the Early effect, and its strong temperature dependence. The field-effect transistors of the next chapters have essentially no gate current—better in that respect—but much lower transconductance at the same current and larger variation between parts.

Circuit design is largely the art of building near-perfect behavior from imperfect devices: emitter degeneration for predictable gain, stiff dividers for $\beta$-independent bias, matched pairs for temperature-independent behavior, and, most powerfully, negative feedback.



<h3 id="book-notes">Static saturation and charge-storage recovery are different models</h3>

The static Ebers–Moll equations can say that both junctions are forward biased, but they do not say how quickly stored carriers disappear. A useful first dynamic estimate treats saturation as excess charge $Q_S$ that must be removed by reverse base current or recombination.

If a switch has an illustrative 20 nC of excess charge and the driver initially extracts a nearly constant 2 mA, the extraction timescale is $Q_S/I_{B,reverse}=\mathbf{10\ \mu s}$. Removing forward base drive is not the same as providing that reverse current. A base-emitter resistor can help, but its discharge current falls as the base voltage changes; the constant-current estimate then ceases to apply.

A **Baker clamp** diverts excess base drive as collector voltage falls, limiting deep collector-base forward bias. It can reduce stored charge at the cost of a larger on-state voltage and a more complicated drive path. A Schottky clamp is attractive because its lower forward drop can act before the silicon collector-base junction becomes strongly forward biased. Actual switching delay still depends on drive current, load, charge distribution and temperature.

This distinction explains why stronger forward base drive can improve a switch's LOW voltage yet worsen turn-off, and why an amplifier driven into a rail can take longer to recover than its small-signal bandwidth suggests. A DC operating-point solution and a charge-aware transient model answer different questions.

<section id="mirrors">

<div className="section-heading"><span>06</span><h2>Current mirrors</h2></div>

Two transistors made on the same chip, at the same temperature, have the same $I_S$. If they also have the same $V_{BE}$, they carry the same collector current. The current mirror exploits this directly.

<MirrorFigure/>

$Q_1$ is **diode-connected**: its collector is tied to its base. A reference current $I_{\mathrm{ref}}$ fed into it develops exactly the $V_{BE}$ that $Q_1$ needs to carry that current. $Q_2$ shares the same $V_{BE}$, so its collector current copies $I_{\mathrm{ref}}$. Here $I_{\mathrm{ref}}$ ≈ ($V_{CC}$ − $V_{BE}$)/R; on 15 V, R = 14.3 kΩ gives about 1 mA.

The mirror is useful because the output current is copied without a resistor in the output path: it is a current source whose compliance extends to within a few tenths of a volt of the emitter. It is also programmable: change $I_{\mathrm{ref}}$ and the output follows.

### Its errors

**Base current.** $I_{\mathrm{ref}}$ supplies the collector current of $Q_1$ plus both base currents. With matched transistors:

<Equation tex="I_{\mathrm{out}}=\frac{I_{\mathrm{ref}}}{1+2/\beta}"/>

With $\beta$ = 100, $I_{\mathrm{out}}$ = 0.980 $I_{\mathrm{ref}}$, a 2% error.

**Early effect.** $Q_1$ always sits at $V_{CE}$ = $V_{BE}$ ≈ 0.65 V. If $Q_2$’s collector is at 10 V, its current is larger by about (10 − 0.65)/$V_A$. For $V_A$ = 100 V that is **9.3%**—the answer to the opening question—and the output current changes as the load changes the collector voltage.

**Mismatch and temperature.** A 1 mV difference in $V_{BE}$ between the two transistors (from geometry or a temperature difference of half a degree) means a 4% current difference. Discrete transistors in separate packages are poorly matched; matched pairs in one package, or transistors on one chip, are far better.

### Improvements

Adding equal emitter resistors to both transistors, with a few tenths of a volt across them, degenerates the mirror: $V_{BE}$ mismatch now has a smaller effect and the output resistance rises, just as in Chapter 31. Three-transistor variants—the Wilson mirror, and a mirror with a follower supplying the bases—reduce both the base-current and Early-effect errors. Mirrors are everywhere inside integrated circuits, where matched transistors are free; they are the active loads and current sources of the differential amplifiers that come next.

### Separate a model prediction from a device specification

The transport equations account for two coupled junctions. The added Early factor is a useful low-frequency extension; the simple model still omits base spreading resistance, emitter and collector series resistance, high-level injection, breakdown and charge-storage dynamics. Its static saturation voltage therefore cannot predict switching delay or qualify a power transistor.

<span className="tiny-label">WORKED CHECK / MATCHED DOES NOT MEAN IDENTICAL CURRENT</span><h3>A basic mirror at different collector voltages</h3>

<p>Let β = 100, $V_A=100$ V, $V_{CE1}=0.65$ V and $V_{CE2}=10$ V. The Early-effect current ratio at common base voltage is $k=(1+10/100)/(1+0.65/100)=1.0929$.</p>

<p>The reference supplies $I_{C1}$ and both base currents, so with equal β the output-to-reference ratio is $k/[1+(1+k)/\beta]=1.0705$. Base-current loss and Early-effect increase partly oppose each other here; the output is about 7.1% above the supplied reference current, rather than independently adding "2%" and "9.3%" as exact errors.</p>

<p>Equalize collector voltages and the ratio returns to $1/(1+2/\beta)=0.9804$. Change temperature or matching and another error enters. Identify the reference current and each terminal current before comparing percentages.</p>

</div>

<section id="check">

<div className="section-heading"><span>07</span><h2>Check your understanding</h2></div>

<details>
<summary>1. When does the forward-active exponential law cease to be the full answer?</summary>
<div>

When the collector junction conducts forward, reverse operation matters, high-level injection occurs, or breakdown and resistive effects become important. Use the appropriate region and model.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>2. Does low static saturation voltage imply fast turn-off?</summary>
<div>

No. Strong saturation can store substantial minority-carrier charge. Static Ebers–Moll equations do not calculate its removal time.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>3. How much extra junction voltage doubles collector current at 300 K?</summary>
<div>

$V_T\ln2\approx17.9$ mV in the ideal forward-active model.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>4. Why does a basic mirror draw less output than its supplied reference current when Early effect is absent?</summary>
<div>

The reference must supply the first collector plus both transistor base currents, so some current is diverted from the mirrored collectors.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>5. Does matched $V_{BE}$ alone ensure exact current matching?</summary>
<div>

No. Collector voltage, temperature, device geometry, series resistance and operating region also matter.

</div>
</details>
