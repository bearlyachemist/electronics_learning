<h1>Band-limited differentiators<br/><em>A derivative needs a bandwidth.</em></h1>

<p className="dek">Swap the integrator's resistor and capacitor and the op amp differentiates, until its rising gain meets the falling open-loop gain and the circuit rings. Derive the resonance, bound the derivative with two components, and see why every useful differentiator has a defined bandwidth.</p>

<div className="chapter-meta"><span>40–55 min</span><span>RC differentiators (Ch. 7) · Integrators (Ch. 83) · Stability (Ch. 74)</span></div>

</div>

<div className="opening-grid">

<div><span className="tiny-label">PREDICT BEFORE CALCULATING</span><p className="opening-question">An op-amp differentiator is made from one capacitor and one resistor. Why does it ring, even with a clean input?</p></div>

<div>

Put a capacitor at the input of an inverting amplifier and a resistor in its feedback, and the input current is $C\,dV/dt$: the output is the input's derivative. It is the mirror image of the integrator, and in a sense its opposite in temperament. The integrator ignores high frequencies and accumulates low ones; the differentiator amplifies high frequencies without limit.

That has two consequences. Noise, which is mostly fast, is amplified far more than any slow signal. And the circuit's noise gain rises with frequency, exactly as in the transimpedance amplifier of Chapter 82, which makes the bare circuit nearly unstable. Both are fixed by the same two components.

</div>

</div>

<div className="learning-map">

<span className="tiny-label">THE PATH THROUGH THIS CHAPTER</span><p>Bound the derivative at both ends → Use impedances before approximating → Find the resonance → Compare three input slopes → Noise and a defined bandwidth → Check your understanding.</p>

</div>

<section id="model">

<div className="section-heading"><span>01</span><h2>Derive the ideal differentiator from KCL</h2></div>

<OpAmpFigure mode="differentiator"/>

Begin with the simplest version: a capacitor $C_{IN}$ from the input to the inverting node, a resistor $R_F$ from that node to the output, and the noninverting input grounded. Call the inverting node $X$. Two assumptions, each justified separately, make the analysis short.

<div className="derivation">

<span className="tiny-label">DERIVATION / THE IDEAL DIFFERENTIATOR</span>

1. **No current enters the op amp's inputs.** The input bias current is picoamps to nanoamps, negligible beside the capacitor and resistor currents. So whatever current arrives at node $X$ through $C_{IN}$ must leave through $R_F$.

2. **Node $X$ sits at 0 V.** The op amp's output obeys $v_O = A(v_+ - v_X) = -Av_X$. If the output is finite (a few volts) and $A$ is very large, then $v_X = -v_O/A$ is essentially zero. This "virtual ground" is a consequence of the negative feedback through $R_F$ holding the output in range, not a wire to ground; Section 03 checks it with finite $A$.

3. **Current through the capacitor.** A capacitor's current is $C$ times the rate of change of the voltage across it. That voltage is $v_I - v_X = v_I$, so
<Equation tex={String.raw`i_C=C_{IN}\frac{d(v_I-v_X)}{dt}=C_{IN}\frac{dv_I}{dt}`}/>

4. **Current through the resistor,** flowing from $X$ to the output, by Ohm's law:
<Equation tex={String.raw`i_F=\frac{v_X-v_O}{R_F}=-\frac{v_O}{R_F}`}/>

5. **KCL at $X$:** current in equals current out, $i_C = i_F$:
<Equation tex={String.raw`C_{IN}\frac{dv_I}{dt}=-\frac{v_O}{R_F}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad v_O=-R_FC_{IN}\frac{dv_I}{dt}`} label="IDEAL OP-AMP DIFFERENTIATOR"/>

6. **Check the units and sign.** $R_FC_{IN}$ is a time (ohms × farads = seconds), and $dv_I/dt$ is volts per second, so their product is volts. The minus sign is the inverting configuration: a rising input pushes current into $X$, and the output must go negative to pull that current away through $R_F$.

<p className="assume">Valid while: the op amp's loop gain is large at the signal frequency, the output stays within its swing, and the output can supply the current $v_O/R_F$.</p>

</div>

The same result in the frequency domain follows from the impedances: $Z_{IN} = 1/(sC_{IN})$, $Z_F = R_F$, and the inverting gain $-Z_F/Z_{IN} = -sR_FC_{IN}$. Multiplying by $s$ is differentiation, so the two forms agree. Its magnitude, $\omega R_FC_{IN}$, rises without limit as frequency increases, and that single fact causes both problems this chapter solves: noise amplification and instability.

The practical circuit adds two components: $R_{IN}$ in series with $C_{IN}$, which limits the input current at high frequency, and $C_F$ across $R_F$, which rolls off the feedback. Section 02 derives what they do.

</section>

<section id="derive">

<div className="section-heading"><span>02</span><h2>Derive the band-limited transfer function</h2></div>

With $R_{IN}$ and $C_F$ added, the input branch and feedback branch are both impedances. Repeat the KCL step with phasors.

<div className="derivation">

<span className="tiny-label">DERIVATION / THE PRACTICAL DIFFERENTIATOR</span>

1. **Input-branch impedance.** $R_{IN}$ and $C_{IN}$ in series:
<Equation tex={String.raw`Z_{IN}=R_{IN}+\frac{1}{sC_{IN}}=\frac{1+sR_{IN}C_{IN}}{sC_{IN}}`}/>

2. **Feedback impedance.** $R_F$ and $C_F$ in parallel, using $Z_1Z_2/(Z_1+Z_2)$:
<Equation tex={String.raw`Z_F=\frac{R_F\cdot\frac{1}{sC_F}}{R_F+\frac{1}{sC_F}}=\frac{R_F}{1+sR_FC_F}`}/>

3. **KCL at $X$, with $V_X = 0$ and no op-amp input current:**
<Equation tex={String.raw`\frac{V_I-0}{Z_{IN}}=\frac{0-V_O}{Z_F}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad\frac{V_O}{V_I}=-\frac{Z_F}{Z_{IN}}`}/>

4. **Substitute and simplify.** Dividing $Z_F$ by $Z_{IN}$ inverts the second fraction:
<Equation tex={String.raw`H(s)=-\frac{R_F}{1+sR_FC_F}\cdot\frac{sC_{IN}}{1+sR_{IN}C_{IN}}=-\frac{sR_FC_{IN}}{(1+sR_{IN}C_{IN})(1+sR_FC_F)}`} label="BAND-LIMITED DIFFERENTIATOR"/>

5. **Read off the regions.** Define $\tau_1 = R_{IN}C_{IN}$ and $\tau_2 = R_FC_F$, with corner frequencies $f_1 = 1/2\pi\tau_1$ and $f_2 = 1/2\pi\tau_2$.
   - For $f \ll f_1, f_2$, both denominator factors are about 1 and $H \approx -sR_FC_{IN}$: the ideal derivative.
   - If $f_1 < f_2$, then for $f_1 \ll f \ll f_2$ the first factor is about $s\tau_1$, so $H \approx -sR_FC_{IN}/(sR_{IN}C_{IN}) = -R_F/R_{IN}$: a flat gain, because $C_{IN}$ has become a short and the circuit is an ordinary inverting amplifier.
   - For $f \gg f_1, f_2$, both factors are proportional to $s$, so $H \approx -R_FC_{IN}/(s\tau_1\tau_2)$: the gain falls at 20 dB per decade.

</div>

If the two corners are made equal, $\tau_1 = \tau_2 = \tau$, the middle region vanishes: the circuit differentiates up to $f_c = 1/2\pi\tau$ and then rolls off. That is the usual design choice, because it gives the widest differentiating band for a given high-frequency gain limit.

</section>

<section id="resonance">

<div className="section-heading"><span>03</span><h2>Derive the resonance with a finite-gain op amp</h2></div>

Section 01 assumed $v_X = 0$. Now let the op amp have a finite, frequency-dependent gain and see what that assumption was hiding. A compensated op amp's open-loop gain above a few hertz is well described by $A(s) = \omega_t/s$, where $\omega_t = 2\pi f_t$ is its unity-gain angular frequency (Chapter 74).

<div className="derivation">

<span className="tiny-label">DERIVATION / THE BARE DIFFERENTIATOR WITH FINITE GAIN</span>

1. **The op amp relation.** With the plus input grounded, $V_O = -A V_X$, so $V_X = -V_O/A$. Node $X$ is no longer exactly zero.

2. **KCL at $X$** (still no input current), with $C_{IN}$ from $V_I$ and $R_F$ to $V_O$:
<Equation tex={String.raw`sC_{IN}(V_I-V_X)=\frac{V_X-V_O}{R_F}`}/>

3. **Substitute $V_X = -V_O/A$** and multiply both sides by $R_F$:
<Equation tex={String.raw`sR_FC_{IN}V_I+sR_FC_{IN}\frac{V_O}{A}=-\frac{V_O}{A}-V_O`}/>

4. **Collect the $V_O$ terms** on one side:
<Equation tex={String.raw`sR_FC_{IN}V_I=-V_O\left(1+\frac{1}{A}+\frac{sR_FC_{IN}}{A}\right)\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad\frac{V_O}{V_I}=\frac{-sR_FC_{IN}}{1+\dfrac{1+sR_FC_{IN}}{A}}`}/>
   The numerator $1 + sR_FC_{IN}$ inside the correction term is the **noise gain** $1 + Z_F/Z_{IN}$: the gain the circuit applies to an error at the op amp's input. As $A \to \infty$ the correction vanishes and the ideal result returns.

5. **Insert $A = \omega_t/s$,** so $1/A = s/\omega_t$:
<Equation tex={String.raw`\frac{V_O}{V_I}=\frac{-sR_FC_{IN}}{1+\dfrac{s}{\omega_t}+\dfrac{s^2R_FC_{IN}}{\omega_t}}`}/>

6. **Match to the standard second-order form** $1 + s/(Q\omega_0) + s^2/\omega_0^2$, term by term:
<Equation tex={String.raw`\frac{1}{\omega_0^2}=\frac{R_FC_{IN}}{\omega_t}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\omega_0=\sqrt{\frac{\omega_t}{R_FC_{IN}}}`}/>
<Equation tex={String.raw`\frac{1}{Q\omega_0}=\frac{1}{\omega_t}\quad\Rightarrow\quad Q=\frac{\omega_t}{\omega_0}=\sqrt{\omega_tR_FC_{IN}}`} label="RESONANCE OF THE BARE DIFFERENTIATOR"/>

7. **How long it rings.** A second-order system with $Q \gg 1$ responds to a disturbance with a sinusoid at $\omega_0$ whose envelope decays as $e^{-\omega_0t/2Q}$. The envelope time constant is $2Q/\omega_0 = Q/(\pi f_0)$.

<p className="assume">The loop view gives the same answer: the noise gain $1 + sR_FC_{IN}$ rises at 20 dB per decade, $A$ falls at 20 dB per decade, and they meet with a 40 dB-per-decade closing rate, about 0° of phase margin. A large $Q$ is what "almost no phase margin" looks like in the closed-loop response.</p>

</div>


<DiffBodeFigure/>

<div className="worked-example">

<span className="tiny-label">WORKED EXAMPLE / A RESONATOR YOU DID NOT ASK FOR</span><h3>$R_F$ = 100 kΩ, $C_{IN}$ = 1 nF, $f_t$ = 1 MHz</h3>

<p>$R_FC_{IN}$ = 100 µs and $\omega_t = 2\pi × 10^6$ s⁻¹. So $f_0 = \sqrt{6.28 \times 10^6/10^{-4}}/2\pi$ = <strong>40 kHz</strong> and $Q = \sqrt{628}$ = <strong>25</strong>. Any disturbance near 40 kHz is amplified 25 times and rings for about $Q/\pi f_0$ ≈ 200 µs. The input does not have to contain 40 kHz: every edge, and the op amp's own noise, excites it.</p>

<p><strong>The fix.</strong> Add $R_{IN}$ = 10 kΩ and $C_F$ = 100 pF. Both time constants are 10 µs, both corners 16 kHz. The noise gain now levels off at $1 + R_F/R_{IN}$ = 11 well below the crossing, the loop has ample phase margin, and the circuit differentiates cleanly up to about 16 kHz.</p>

</div>

<section id="cases">

<div className="section-heading"><span>04</span><h2>Compare three input slopes</h2></div>

<div className="worked-example">

<span className="tiny-label">WORKED EXAMPLE / RAMPS, SINES AND EDGES</span><h3>The compensated differentiator: $R_FC_{IN}$ = 100 µs, corners at 16 kHz</h3>

<p><strong>Ramps.</strong> Slopes of 100, 1000 and 10 000 V/s give outputs of −10 mV, −0.1 V and −1 V, once the 10 µs transients have settled, as long as the ramp itself stays within the input range.</p>

<p><strong>Sines.</strong> At 1 kHz the exact gain is 0.626, against the ideal derivative's 0.628. At 10 kHz it is 4.5, against 6.28: both poles already matter. At 100 kHz it is 1.55 and falling. The circuit is a differentiator below about 3 kHz, within 3%.</p>

<p><strong>Edges.</strong> A 1 V edge rising in 1 µs has a derivative of $10^6$ V/s, which "should" give −100 V. It is far outside the differentiating band; the actual output is a pulse limited by the 16 kHz poles and by the output swing. A differentiator measures the slopes of slow signals; it does not measure fast edges.</p>

</div>

</section>

<section id="limits">

<div className="section-heading"><span>05</span><h2>Noise and a defined bandwidth</h2></div>

<DiffNoiseFigure/>

Noise contains energy at all frequencies, and a differentiator's gain is proportional to frequency, so it weights the fastest noise the most. A 1% noise floor on a sensor can become a derivative far larger than the signal's. The fix is always the same: decide the bandwidth over which the derivative is needed, and limit the circuit to it. The compensation components above do exactly that, and the filtered derivative appears with a small, predictable delay.

**Where differentiators are useful:** edge detection and pulse shaping (deliberately), the D term of a PID controller (always with a roll-off, "filtered derivative"), rate feedback in a servo, and pre-emphasis. For estimating the rate of change of a noisy measurement, a band-limited analog derivative or a properly filtered numerical estimate after sampling is preferable to a bare differentiator.

**Practical points.** The source's resistance adds to $R_{IN}$; stray capacitance adds to $C_F$. A large transient can overload the op amp, and its recovery is unrelated to the intended response. Verify with a sine sweep and with a pulse of realistic rise time, against the full transfer function including the op amp's finite gain.

<div className="takeaway"><span className="tiny-label">THE IDEA TO KEEP</span><p>A bare $C_{IN}$–$R_F$ differentiator is a resonator with $Q = \sqrt{\omega_tR_FC_{IN}}$. Add $R_{IN}$ and $C_F$ to <em>give the derivative a defined bandwidth</em>; it then stays stable and stops amplifying noise it was never meant to measure.</p></div>

</section>

<section id="precision-depth">

<div className="section-heading"><span>06</span><h2>Specify derivative accuracy, pulse range and noise explicitly</h2></div>

<h3>State how accurately the circuit should differentiate</h3>

For the practical transfer already derived, divide by the ideal derivative transfer $-sR_FC_{in}$. The remaining factor is $1/[(1+s\tau_1)(1+s\tau_2)]$, where $\tau_1=R_{in}C_{in}$ and $\tau_2=R_FC_F$. At equal corners $f_c$, the magnitude relative to an ideal differentiator is $1/[1+(f/f_c)^2]$ and the added phase lag is $-2\arctan(f/f_c)$.

At $f=0.1f_c$, magnitude is 0.9901 of ideal, a 0.99% error, but phase error is already 11.42°. For less than 1° extra phase, the frequency must be below about $0.00873f_c$. A derivative used for damping in a control loop may therefore require far more corner-frequency separation than an amplitude-only specification suggests.

<h3>A finite edge produces a finite pulse</h3>

An input ramp of 1 V in 100 µs has slope 10000 V/s. With $R_FC_{in}=100$ µs, the ideal derivative output is −1 V during that ramp. The practical circuit rounds the beginning and end over its two time constants. A 1 V edge in 1 µs would request −100 V under the same ideal derivative model, far beyond ordinary output range. The band-limiting resistors and capacitors bound the linear high-frequency response, but a sufficiently large fast edge can still overload the amplifier.

For equal time constants $\tau$, the step response follows from the transfer function in three lines. A unit step has Laplace transform $1/s$, so the output is
<Equation tex={String.raw`V_O(s)=\frac1s\cdot\frac{-sR_FC_{in}}{(1+s\tau)^2}=-\frac{R_FC_{in}}{\tau^2}\cdot\frac{1}{(s+1/\tau)^2}`}/>
The standard transform pair $t\,e^{-at} \leftrightarrow 1/(s+a)^2$ then gives the time response directly; setting its derivative to zero shows the peak occurs at $t = \tau$:

<Equation tex={String.raw`v_o(t)=-\frac{R_FC_{in}}{\tau^2}\,t\,e^{-t/\tau},\qquad |v_{o,peak}|=\frac{R_FC_{in}}{e\tau}\quad(t=\tau)`}/>

With $R_FC_{in}=100$ µs and $\tau=10$ µs, that peak is 3.68 V. The pulse width is controlled by the bandwidth, not by an infinite mathematical derivative. Choose an output limit and input-edge specification before trusting this waveform.

<h3>Compare with a finite-difference derivative</h3>

A sampled estimate $[x(t)-x(t-\Delta t)]/\Delta t$ also has a defined bandwidth and a half-sample delay. If successive input noise samples are independent with RMS $\sigma$, derivative noise is $\sqrt2\sigma/\Delta t$. Reducing the interval improves time resolution but increases noise. The analog circuit makes the same trade through its corner frequencies and noise gain. In either case, define the input-noise spectrum and useful signal band, then filter before estimating a slope that has physical meaning.

Capacitor microphonics, cable pickup and source resistance can dominate a quiet amplifier. In a control system, differentiating the measured variable rather than a discontinuous command can avoid a large setpoint kick; retain a finite high-frequency rolloff and include its phase in the loop design.

<details>
<summary>Worked self-check: where must equal corners lie for 1% derivative magnitude error at 1 kHz?</summary>
<div>

Require $1/[1+(1000/f_c)^2]\ge0.99$, giving $f_c\ge9.95$ kHz. At that minimum corner, added phase lag is about 11.5°, so a 1° phase requirement instead needs approximately 114.6 kHz corners. The amplifier must still maintain adequate loop margin at that higher bandwidth.

</div>
</details>

<section id="check">

<div className="section-heading"><span>07</span><h2>Check your understanding</h2></div>

<details>
<summary>1. Why does a bare op-amp differentiator ring?</summary>
<div>

Its noise gain, $1 + sR_FC_{IN}$, rises at 20 dB per decade and meets the op amp's open-loop gain, falling at 20 dB per decade, with almost no phase margin. The closed loop is a second-order resonator with $Q = \sqrt{\omega_tR_FC_{IN}}$, 25 in the example, and any edge or noise excites it.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>2. With $R_F$ = 47 kΩ, $C_{IN}$ = 2.2 nF and a 10 MHz op amp, where is the resonance and what is its Q?</summary>
<div>

$R_FC_{IN}$ = 103 µs. $f_0 = \sqrt{2\pi × 10^7/1.03×10^{-4}}/2\pi$ ≈ **124 kHz**, and $Q = \sqrt{6.28×10^7 × 1.03×10^{-4}}$ ≈ **80**. A faster op amp makes the resonance higher and sharper, not better.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>3. What do $R_{IN}$ and $C_F$ each do?</summary>
<div>

$R_{IN}$ limits the input branch's admittance at high frequency, so the noise gain levels off at $1 + R_F/R_{IN}$ instead of rising forever; that restores phase margin. $C_F$ rolls off the signal gain above $1/(2\pi R_FC_F)$, limiting noise and high-frequency gain.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>4. A 1 V edge rises in 1 µs into a differentiator with $R_FC_{IN}$ = 100 µs. Why is the output not −100 V?</summary>
<div>

The edge's frequency content lies far above the differentiator's band (16 kHz), where its gain has been capped and then rolled off. The output is a limited pulse, and in any case the op amp's swing would clip at a few volts.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>5. Why must a derivative term in a controller be filtered?</summary>
<div>

An unfiltered derivative amplifies measurement noise in proportion to frequency, producing large, useless actuator commands. A roll-off above the frequencies where the derivative's phase lead is needed keeps the benefit and limits the noise.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>6. Over what frequency range does the compensated example differentiate within about 3%?</summary>
<div>

Up to roughly 3 kHz. At 1 kHz the gain is within 0.4% of the ideal; at 10 kHz, where the 16 kHz poles are close, it is 28% low. Choose the corners about ten times above the highest frequency you need to differentiate.

</div>
</details>
