<h1>Diode-connected MOSFET loads<br/><em>A transistor can act as an incremental resistor.</em></h1>

<p className="dek">Derive the resistance of a gate–drain-connected device, use it as the load of a common-source stage, and see why its gain is fixed by device geometry, bounded by headroom, and remarkably linear.</p>

<div className="chapter-meta"><span>50–70 min</span><span>Bias atlas (Ch. 52) · Small-signal resistance (Ch. 3)</span></div>

</div>

<div className="opening-grid">

<div><span className="tiny-label">PREDICT BEFORE CALCULATING</span><p className="opening-question">Replace a 3 kΩ drain resistor by a transistor that drops the same 3 V at the same current. Will the gain go up, down or stay the same?</p></div>

<div>

In an integrated circuit, resistors are large, imprecise and poorly matched to transistors, while transistors are small and match each other well. So the first thing an IC designer reaches for is a transistor that behaves like a resistor. Connect a MOSFET's gate to its drain and it becomes a two-terminal device: the **diode connection**.

It is not a resistor at DC: its current is a square law of its voltage. But for small signals it presents a resistance, and a common-source stage loaded by it has a gain that depends only on a ratio of transistor sizes. This chapter derives that resistance, uses it as a load, and finds both what it is good for and why it gives less gain than the resistor it replaces.

</div>

</div>

<div className="learning-map">

<span className="tiny-label">THE PATH THROUGH THIS CHAPTER</span><p>Two channel currents share one output → What resistance does a diode connection present? → Apply KCL before introducing a gain → Exchange headroom for gain → What breaks the straight line → Check your understanding.</p>

</div>

<section id="model">

<div className="section-heading"><span>01</span><h2>Two channel currents share one output</h2></div>

<DiodeLoadCircuit/>

$M_1$ is an ordinary common-source input device. $M_2$ has its gate and drain both on the rail, and its source is the output. There is no other path to the output, so KCL is simply $I_{D1} = I_{D2}$.

A diode-connected MOSFET is **always in saturation when it conducts**. Its gate–drain voltage is zero, so $V_{DS} = V_{GS}$. If it is on, $V_{GS} > V_T$, and therefore $V_{DS} > V_{GS} - V_T = u$, which is the saturation condition. We never need to test $M_2$'s region; only $M_1$ can leave saturation.

For the moment each transistor's body is tied to its own source. In a single-well process the bodies of all NMOS devices share the grounded substrate, and $M_2$, whose source is not at ground, then suffers body effect. Section 05 shows what that changes.

</section>

<section id="resistance">

<div className="section-heading"><span>02</span><h2>What resistance does a diode connection present?</h2></div>

Apply a small voltage $v$ across the diode-connected device and ask what current flows. Because the gate is the drain, $v_{gs} = v_{ds} = v$, and the small-signal model gives $i = g_mv_{gs} + g_{ds}v_{ds} = (g_m + g_{ds})v$:

<Equation tex={String.raw`r_{\text{diode}}=\frac{1}{g_m+g_{ds}}\approx\frac1{g_m}=\frac{u}{2I_D}`} label="INCREMENTAL RESISTANCE OF A DIODE CONNECTION"/>

<DiodeSmallSignalFigure/>

The controlled current source sits across its own control voltage, so it is indistinguishable from a conductance $g_m$. Since $g_m$ is typically a hundred times $g_{ds}$, the diode connection is a low resistance, set almost entirely by its transconductance.

<div className="worked-example">

<span className="tiny-label">WORKED EXAMPLE / SAME TRANSISTOR, TWO CONNECTIONS</span><h3>A 1 mA device with $k$ = 2 mA/V² and $\lambda$ = 0.02 V⁻¹</h3>

<p>The overdrive is $u = \sqrt{2I_D/k}$ = 1 V and $g_m = ku$ = 2 mS. Its output resistance is $r_o \approx 1/(\lambda I_D)$ = 50 kΩ, so $g_{ds}$ = 20 µS.</p>

<p><strong>Diode connected:</strong> $r = 1/(2\text{ mS} + 0.02\text{ mS})$ = <strong>495 Ω</strong>. <strong>With its gate held at a fixed voltage</strong>, as a current source, its drain presents $r_o$ = <strong>50 kΩ</strong>. Same device, same current, a factor of 100 apart. The difference is entirely whether the gate is allowed to follow the drain.</p>

</div>

That factor of 100 is the whole story of this chapter and the next few. A diode connection is a convenient low resistance; a current source with a fixed gate is a high one. Chapter 54 uses both together in a current mirror.

### The opening question

The stage of the figure drops 3 V across $M_2$ at 1 mA: $V_T$ = 1 V plus $u_2$ = 2 V of overdrive. A 3 kΩ resistor drops the same 3 V at 1 mA. With $g_{m1}$ = 2 mS, the resistor load gives a gain of $-g_{m1}R_D$ = **−6**. The diode load gives $-g_{m1}/g_{m2}$ = −2 mS / 1 mS = **−2**.

The ratio between a diode's incremental and large-signal resistance explains it:

<Equation tex={String.raw`\frac{r_{\text{diode}}}{V_{DS}/I_D}=\frac{u/2I_D}{(V_T+u)/I_D}=\frac{u}{2(V_T+u)}`}/>

Two things lose gain. The threshold voltage consumes headroom while contributing no incremental resistance at all. And even the overdrive part counts only half, because the square law is twice as steep as a straight line through the origin. Here $u/(2(V_T + u))$ = 2/6, the factor of 3 between the two gains.

</section>

<section id="derive">

<div className="section-heading"><span>03</span><h2>Apply KCL before introducing a gain</h2></div>

Small-signal formulas are a shortcut. The DC equation of this stage is simple enough to solve exactly, and the exact answer is surprising. With both devices in saturation and $\lambda$ = 0:

<Equation tex={String.raw`\tfrac12 k_1(V_{in}-V_{T})^2=\tfrac12 k_2(V_{DD}-V_{out}-V_{T})^2`}/>

Both sides are squares of positive quantities while both devices conduct, so take the square root:

<Equation tex={String.raw`V_{out}=V_{DD}-V_{T}-\sqrt{\frac{k_1}{k_2}}\,(V_{in}-V_{T})`} label="EXACT TRANSFER, BOTH IN SATURATION"/>

This is a **straight line**. The two square laws cancel, so over its whole range the stage is perfectly linear in this model, with slope $-\sqrt{k_1/k_2}$. Since the currents are equal and $g_m = 2I_D/u$, the same slope can be written three ways:

<Equation tex={String.raw`A_v=-\sqrt{\frac{k_1}{k_2}}=-\frac{g_{m1}}{g_{m2}}=-\frac{u_2}{u_1}`} label="GAIN = RATIO OF OVERDRIVES"/>

<DiodeTransferFigure/>

The line runs from cutoff ($V_{in} < V_T$, where the output rests at $V_{DD} - V_T$ with only leakage flowing) to the point where $V_{out}$ falls to $V_{in} - V_T$ and $M_1$ enters triode. The gain depends only on $k_1/k_2 = (W_1/L_1)/(W_2/L_2)$, a ratio of drawn dimensions. It does not depend on the current, the supply, temperature, or the process's absolute mobility. That is why the diode-connected load was a mainstay of early NMOS logic and is still used for wide-band, low-gain stages.

With finite output conductances and an AC-coupled load $R_L$, write KCL at the output node. $M_1$ pulls $g_{m1}v_{in} + g_{ds1}v_{out}$ out of the node to ground. The diode $M_2$, with its gate and drain at AC ground (the supply) and its source at the output, has $v_{gs2} = -v_{out}$ and $v_{ds2} = -v_{out}$, so it supplies $-(g_{m2} + g_{ds2})v_{out}$ into the node. The load draws $v_{out}/R_L$. Setting the sum to zero, $g_{m1}v_{in} + v_{out}(g_{m2} + g_{ds2} + g_{ds1} + 1/R_L) = 0$, which gives

<Equation tex={String.raw`A_v=-\frac{g_{m1}}{g_{m2}+g_{ds2}+g_{ds1}+1/R_L}`}/>

For $g_{m1}$ = 2 mS, $g_{m2}$ = 1 mS, both $g_{ds}$ = 20 µS and $R_L$ = 10 kΩ, $A_v$ = −2/(1 + 0.04 + 0.1) = −1.75. The diode's low resistance makes the stage insensitive to loading: the same 10 kΩ load would cut a 3 kΩ resistor stage from −6 to −4.6.

</section>

<section id="cases">

<div className="section-heading"><span>04</span><h2>Exchange headroom for gain</h2></div>

Hold the current at 1 mA and the input device at $k_1$ = 2 mA/V² (so $u_1$ = 1 V and $V_{in}$ = 2 V), and change only the load size:

<MosCases kind="diode"/>

A small (narrow, long) load gives high gain but needs a large overdrive, which pulls the output down toward $M_1$'s saturation edge. A large load is stiff, sits near the rail and gives less than unity gain. Because $|A_v| = u_2/u_1$ and $V_{out} = V_{DD} - V_T - u_2$, gain and output level are locked together:

<DiodeHeadroomFigure/>

<div className="worked-example">

<span className="tiny-label">WORKED EXAMPLE / A GAIN OF −2.5, AND WHY NOT −10</span><h3>Size the load for 1 mA from 5 V</h3>

<p>With $u_1$ = 1 V, a gain of −2.5 needs $u_2$ = 2.5 V. Then $k_2 = 2I_D/u_2^2$ = 2/6.25 = <strong>0.32 mA/V²</strong>, so the load's $W/L$ is 1/6.25 of the input device's: the size ratio is the gain squared.</p>

<p>Output: $V_{out}$ = 5 − 1 − 2.5 = 1.5 V. $M_1$ needs $V_{DS} \ge u_1$ = 1 V, so the output can fall only 0.5 V before $M_1$ leaves saturation. That is the gain–headroom trade in one number.</p>

<p>Now try −10. If $u_1$ stays 1 V, $u_2$ = 10 V: impossible in 5 V. Reduce the input overdrive to 0.3 V; then $u_2$ = 3 V, $V_{out}$ = 1.0 V with 0.7 V of margin, and the size ratio is 100 : 1. It can be built, but a 100 : 1 device ratio is costly in area, the 0.3 V input overdrive makes the bias sensitive to threshold, and the stage swings only a fraction of a volt. Diode loads are for gains of a few.</p>

</div>

<div className="margin-note">

**Gain for bandwidth, not for nothing.** With load capacitance $C$, the output pole is at $g_{m2}/2\pi C$ and the gain–bandwidth product is $g_{m1}/2\pi C$, independent of the load. At 2 pF: a diode load with gain −2 has its pole at 80 MHz; a 3 kΩ resistor load with gain −6 has its pole at 27 MHz. Both have a 160 MHz gain–bandwidth product. The diode load spends gain to buy bandwidth.

</div>

</section>

<section id="limits">

<div className="section-heading"><span>05</span><h2>What breaks the straight line?</h2></div>

The perfect line relied on three assumptions: identical thresholds for the two devices, no channel-length modulation, and no body effect. Each fails in a real circuit.

**Body effect.** In a common-substrate process $M_2$'s body is grounded while its source is the output. As the output rises, $V_{SB2}$ rises, $M_2$'s threshold rises, and the cancellation is spoiled:

<DiodeBodyFigure/>

<div className="worked-example">

<span className="tiny-label">WORKED EXAMPLE / BODY EFFECT ON THE LOAD</span><h3>$k_2/k_1$ = 1/4, $\gamma$ = 0.5 √V, $2\phi_F$ = 0.6 V, $V_{in}$ = 2 V</h3>

<p>The current is still 1 mA (set by $M_1$), so $u_2$ is still 2 V. But $V_{T2} = 1 + 0.5(\sqrt{0.6 + V_{out}} - \sqrt{0.6})$ depends on the output. Solve $V_{out} = 5 - V_{T2} - 2$ by iteration: starting from 2 V gives $V_{T2}$ = 1.42 V and $V_{out}$ = 1.58 V; repeating converges to <strong>$V_{out}$ = 1.64 V</strong>, $V_{T2}$ = 1.36 V. The output has dropped 0.36 V, all of it lost from $M_1$'s headroom.</p>

<p>For signals, the moving source adds a body transconductance $g_{mb2} = g_{m2}\gamma/(2\sqrt{2\phi_F + V_{SB}})$ = 0.167 $g_{m2}$, in parallel with $g_{m2}$. The gain becomes $-g_{m1}/(g_{m2} + g_{mb2})$ = −2/1.167 = <strong>−1.71</strong>, and since $g_{mb2}$ varies with output voltage, the line is now curved.</p>

</div>

**Channel-length modulation.** With $\lambda \ne 0$ each current gains a factor $(1 + \lambda V_{DS})$, and the two devices have different $V_{DS}$, so the cancellation is again imperfect. The effect is small in long devices and grows in short-channel ones.

**DC loading.** A load resistor connected at DC draws a third current, so the operating point moves. Only an AC-coupled load enters the gain formula as a simple parallel conductance.

**Threshold mismatch.** A difference between $V_{T1}$ and $V_{T2}$ shifts the intercept of the line, moving the DC output by $\sqrt{k_1/k_2}$ times the input-referred part. At a gain of −2, 20 mV of mismatch moves the output by about 40 mV: an offset, not a gain error.

Finally, "diode connected" means gate tied to drain. It is a different thing from the MOSFET's intrinsic body diode, the parasitic PN junction between body and drain, which conducts only when the drain is pulled the wrong way.

<div className="takeaway"><span className="tiny-label">THE IDEA TO KEEP</span><p>A diode-connected MOSFET is a resistor of value $1/g_m$ for signals. As a load it gives a gain equal to <em>the ratio of overdrives</em>: precise and linear, but bounded by the headroom it consumes.</p></div>

</section>

<section id="check">

<div className="section-heading"><span>06</span><h2>Check your understanding</h2></div>

<details>
<summary>1. Why is a diode-connected MOSFET always in saturation when it conducts? Does that make the input transistor safe too?</summary>
<div>

With gate tied to drain, $V_{DS} = V_{GS}$, and conduction requires $V_{GS} > V_T$, so $V_{DS} > V_{GS} - V_T$. That is the saturation condition. It says nothing about $M_1$: its drain is the output, which falls as the input rises, and $M_1$ enters triode when $V_{out} < V_{in} - V_T$.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>2. A device carries 0.5 mA with $u$ = 0.4 V. What resistance does it present when diode connected?</summary>
<div>

$g_m = 2I_D/u$ = 2.5 mS, so $r \approx 1/g_m$ = 400 Ω, or slightly less with $g_{ds}$ included. Note how small: the same device as a current source might present hundreds of kilohms.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>3. Why is the ideal transfer exactly straight, and what does that buy?</summary>
<div>

Both devices carry the same current and both obey a square law, so the square roots of the two currents are equal and linear in their overdrives. The output is therefore a linear function of the input over the whole range where both conduct. A diode-loaded stage has very low distortion for its gain, unlike a resistor-loaded stage whose second harmonic is $v_g/4u$.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>4. Why does doubling the gain need four times the size ratio?</summary>
<div>

The gain is $\sqrt{k_1/k_2}$. Doubling it needs $k_1/k_2$ four times larger, and since $|A_v| = u_2/u_1$ it also doubles the load overdrive, which costs headroom. Large gains are impractical with diode loads for both reasons.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>5. With a grounded body on the load, why does the gain fall?</summary>
<div>

The load's source is the output. When it rises the body effect raises $M_2$'s threshold, which reduces $M_2$'s gate–source drive in the same direction as the source rising. For signals this appears as an extra conductance $g_{mb2}$ in parallel with $g_{m2}$, lowering the gain to $-g_{m1}/(g_{m2} + g_{mb2})$, and making it vary with output level.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>6. When would you choose a diode load over a current-source load?</summary>
<div>

When you want a low, well-defined gain with high bandwidth and low distortion, a predictable DC level (for example, a level shifter or a bias generator), or a stage whose gain is set by geometry and independent of process and temperature. When you need high voltage gain, use a current-source load (Chapter 60): its fixed gate makes its incremental resistance $r_o$ rather than $1/g_m$.

</div>
</details>
