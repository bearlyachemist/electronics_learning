<div className="opening-grid">

<div><span className="tiny-label">PREDICT BEFORE CALCULATING</span><p className="opening-question">An ideal integrator has 100 µV of input offset and no signal. How long before its output is useless?</p></div>

<div>

Chapter 8 built an integrator from a resistor and a capacitor, and found that it integrated only while its output stayed small compared with its input. Put the capacitor in an op amp's feedback path and the summing node stays at 0 V whatever the output does: the input current is exactly $V_{in}/R$, and the capacitor accumulates it faithfully over volts of output.

That faithfulness is also the problem. The capacitor accumulates every constant current, including the one produced by the op amp's offset and bias current, without limit. A practical integrator is always a compromise between integrating the signal and forgetting the errors, specified over a window of time and frequency.

</div>

</div>

<section id="model">

<div className="section-heading"><span>01</span><h2>Add a DC feedback path</h2></div>

<OpAmpFigure mode="integrator"/>

With the plus input grounded, feedback holds the minus input at 0 V, so the input current is $V_I/R_{IN}$. It cannot enter the op amp, so it flows onto the feedback capacitor, and the output moves to keep the minus input at zero:

<Equation tex={String.raw`C_F\frac{dV_O}{dt}=-\frac{V_I}{R_{IN}}\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad V_O(t)=V_O(0)-\frac{1}{R_{IN}C_F}\int_0^tV_I\,dt`}/>

The output is the accumulated history of the input, plus its initial value. The resistor $R_F$ across the capacitor gives the circuit a finite DC gain so that constant errors cannot accumulate forever. A reset switch across the capacitor is the alternative: it sets the initial condition exactly, but its leakage and charge injection then join the error budget.

</section>

<section id="derive">

<div className="section-heading"><span>02</span><h2>Derive both frequency and time behavior</h2></div>

With $R_F$ in place, the feedback impedance is $R_F \parallel C_F$:

<Equation tex={String.raw`H(s)=-\frac{R_F/R_{IN}}{1+sR_FC_F},\qquad \frac{dV_O}{dt}+\frac{V_O}{R_FC_F}=-\frac{V_I}{R_{IN}C_F}`} label="LEAKY INTEGRATOR"/>

Well above $1/(2\pi R_FC_F)$, $H \approx -1/(sR_{IN}C_F)$: integration. Below it, the circuit is an inverting amplifier of gain $-R_F/R_{IN}$. In time, a stored output decays with time constant $R_FC_F$ when the input is zero; integration is accurate only for intervals much shorter than that.

At the top end, the integrator needs loop gain like any op-amp circuit. Its noise gain is $1 + Z_F/R_{IN}$, which falls toward 1 at high frequency, so it needs a unity-gain-stable op amp, and above the op amp's $f_t$ it stops integrating:

<IntegratorDriftFigure/>

<div className="worked-example">

<span className="tiny-label">WORKED EXAMPLE / RATE, DRIFT AND THE FEEDBACK RESISTOR</span><h3>$R_{IN}$ = 10 kΩ, $C_F$ = 10 nF</h3>

<p><strong>Signal rate.</strong> A constant 0.1 V input moves the output at 0.1/(10 kΩ × 10 nF) = 1000 V/s = 1 V/ms.</p>

<p><strong>Offset with no $R_F$.</strong> The opening question: 100 µV of offset is a constant input, moving the output at 1 V/s. From zero, a ±4.5 V output reaches its rail in <strong>4.5 s</strong>. Bias current does the same: 1 nA onto 10 nF is 0.1 V/s. An "ideal" integrator is useless for anything longer than a few seconds.</p>

<p><strong>With $R_F$.</strong> 100 kΩ, 1 MΩ and 10 MΩ give time constants of 1, 10 and 100 ms, lower corners of 159, 15.9 and 1.59 Hz, and DC gains of −10, −100 and −1000. The offset settles at 1, 10 and 100 mV. Larger $R_F$ integrates to lower frequencies but holds a larger offset.</p>

<p><strong>A sine in the window.</strong> With $R_F$ = 1 MΩ, a 0.1 V, 1 kHz sine gives $0.1/(2\pi × 1\text{ kHz} × 10\text{ kΩ} × 10\text{ nF})$ = 0.159 V, shifted +90°. A 0.1 V step instead heads for −10 V, the DC gain times 0.1 V, and saturates at −4.5 V on the way.</p>

</div>

<span className="tiny-label">WORKED EXAMPLE / DERIVE THE FREQUENCY</span><h3>$R_1$ = 100 kΩ, $C$ = 10 nF, $R_2$ = 10 kΩ, $R_3$ = 20 kΩ, ±5 V outputs</h3>

<p><strong>Thresholds.</strong> The comparator switches when its plus input crosses 0 V. That input is a divider between the triangle and the square: zero when $V_{tri}R_3 = -V_{sq}R_2$, so at $V_{tri} = \mp V_S R_2/R_3$ = ∓2.5 V.</p>

<p><strong>Ramp.</strong> The square's ±5 V drives the integrator at $\mp V_S/R_1C$ = ∓5 V/ms.</p>

<p><strong>Period.</strong> Each half-cycle covers $2V_SR_2/R_3$ = 5 V at 5 V/ms: 1 ms. So</p>

</div>

<Equation tex={String.raw`f=\frac{1}{2\Delta t}=\frac{1}{4R_1C}\cdot\frac{R_3}{R_2}=500\ \text{Hz}`}/>

The supply voltage has cancelled: a larger swing raises both the ramp rate and the thresholds. The amplitude is set by $R_2/R_3$, the frequency by $R_1C$ and that ratio. The comparator must be noninverting (its feedback to the plus input); an inverting Schmitt trigger in this loop would latch at one rail.

<section id="charge">

<div className="section-heading"><span>05</span><h2>Measure charge</h2></div>

Remove $R_{IN}$ and connect a charge source, such as a piezoelectric sensor or a particle detector, directly to the summing node. Any charge it delivers ends up on $C_F$:

<Equation tex={String.raw`\Delta V_O=-\frac{\Delta Q}{C_F}`}/>

This is a **charge amplifier**. Because the summing node does not move, the sensor's own capacitance and its cable's capacitance do not affect the gain, only the noise. A piezoelectric accelerometer delivering 100 pC into a 1 nF feedback capacitor gives −100 mV, whether its cable is 1 m or 10 m long. $R_F$, often 1 GΩ or more, bleeds the charge away with a time constant $R_FC_F$ = 1 s: the amplifier measures vibration above about 0.16 Hz, but not a static force.

**In control loops** an integrator provides the I of a PI controller (Chapter 45): it drives the steady-state error to zero. When the actuator saturates, the integrator keeps accumulating error, **winds up**, and the loop overshoots badly on recovery. Anti-windup, by clamping the integrator's output or resetting it, changes the equations while engaged and must be designed deliberately.



<h3 id="book-notes">Dual-slope integration makes a time ratio instead of a capacitor calibration</h3>

Integrate an unknown constant voltage $V_X$ through R for a known interval $T_1$. The output changes by $-V_XT_1/(RC)$. Then apply an opposite-polarity reference of magnitude $V_R$ through the **same** R and integrate until the output returns to its initial voltage. The charge-balance condition is

<Equation tex={String.raw`V_XT_1=V_RT_2,\qquad V_X=V_R\frac{T_2}{T_1}`}/>

For $V_X=0.75$ V, $V_R=1.5$ V and $T_1=20$ ms, the return time is **10 ms**. With R = 100 kΩ and C = 100 nF, the first integration excursion is 1.5 V. That excursion must fit the amplifier's output range, but ideal R and C cancel from the final time-ratio measurement.

Cancellation requires the same effective integration path in both phases. Different switch resistances, reference settling, offset, bias current and dielectric absorption introduce unequal charge. The zero-crossing comparator's delay adds a timing term that matters most for short return times. Reset must also establish a reproducible initial condition without leaving charge-injection residue.

The first phase averages the input over a defined window. For a sinusoidal interference at frequency f, that rectangular average has a magnitude response $|\sin(\pi fT_1)/(\pi fT_1)|$. An exactly 20 ms window rejects an ideal 50 Hz component, but rejects 60 Hz less completely. Thus the architecture connects precision conversion, integration and interference rejection; the clock and reference still determine practical accuracy.

<section id="limits">

<div className="section-heading"><span>06</span><h2>Design the capacitor as a real component</h2></div>

**Dielectric absorption.** Some dielectrics store charge in slowly relaxing dipoles. After a reset, a few tenths of a percent of the previous voltage creeps back. Polypropylene, polystyrene and C0G ceramic are good; general-purpose ceramics and electrolytics are poor.

**Leakage.** Leakage through the capacitor, the reset switch, the circuit board and the op amp's input sets a floor on long integrations, exactly like an extra $R_F$. At 1 GΩ of board leakage and 10 nF, the effective time constant is 10 s.

**Voltage coefficient and size.** High-K ceramics lose capacitance with voltage and temperature, making the integration rate signal dependent. For accuracy use film or C0G, which limits practical values to tens of nanofarads.

Specify an integrator as a window: the shortest and longest times (or highest and lowest frequencies) over which it must integrate, the accuracy, and the largest accumulated output. Then check initial conditions, input polarity, drift, saturation and recovery.

<div className="takeaway"><span className="tiny-label">THE IDEA TO KEEP</span><p>An op-amp integrator accumulates every constant current, signal or error, at $V/R_{IN}C_F$. A resistor across the capacitor <em>bounds the error at the cost of the lowest frequencies</em>; beyond that, integration is a window, not an operation.</p></div>

</section>

<section id="precision-depth">

<div className="section-heading"><span>+</span><h2>Compare integration errors and design a calibrated charge measurement</h2></div>

<h3>Current-input and voltage-input integrators have different offset errors</h3>

For an ideal current source into the summing node, input bias current directly adds a ramp: $|dV_o/dt|=|I_B|/C_F$. A constant offset changes the virtual-ground voltage and reset level, but by itself does not create a ramp if the current source has infinite output resistance and no other DC path. A voltage input through $R_{in}$ is different: offset develops a current $V_{OS}/R_{in}$ even when the source is grounded.

<Equation tex={String.raw`\left|\frac{dV_o}{dt}\right|\le\frac{|I_B|+|V_{OS}|/R_{in}+|I_{switch}|+|I_{board}|}{C_F}`}/>

For $R_{in}=2$ MΩ and $C_F=100$ nF, a low-bias part with 2 pA bias but 1 mV offset has a 502 pA worst-case current equivalent, or **5.02 mV/s** drift. A part with 50 pA bias and 10 µV offset totals 55 pA, or 0.55 mV/s. The smaller bias current does not win this voltage-input example. If the signal is a true current source, the comparison can reverse. Include its finite output resistance when that idealization is insufficient.

<h3>Leakage, reset and dielectric memory act on different time scales</h3>

For a constant effective current $I$ with a finite feedback leakage resistance $R_L$, the deviation from reference satisfies

<Equation tex={String.raw`v_o(t)=-IR_L+\left[v_o(0)+IR_L\right]e^{-t/(R_LC_F)}`}/>

For short times it resembles the desired ramp. The fractional accumulated-charge error is approximately $t/(2R_LC_F)$. With $R_L=100$ GΩ and $C_F=100$ nF, the time constant is 10000 s; a 100 s integration has about 0.5% leakage curvature. A 1 pC reset-switch charge on that capacitor causes a 10 µV initial step. Dielectric absorption adds slower history-dependent recovery that one leakage resistor cannot model. Chapter 91 shows why diverting switch leakage away from the summing node can matter more than choosing a still-smaller amplifier bias current.

<h3>The triangle oscillator needs both a ramp and a reliable decision</h3>

For symmetric square-wave levels, both the ramp rate and thresholds scale with output amplitude, producing the frequency cancellation derived above. Unequal source/sink limits or a shifted reference can spoil symmetry. Derive each half-period separately as threshold excursion divided by its actual slope. Comparator propagation delay lets the ramp continue beyond the nominal threshold, increasing amplitude and period. At a 1 V/ms slope, 5 µs delay adds 5 mV of overshoot at each transition. A slow saturating op amp used as the decision element can add much larger, overdrive-dependent delay; a comparator with suitable output levels and hysteresis is easier to characterize.

<h3>A dose monitor measures integrated optical current</h3>

An optically filtered photodiode, current integrator and threshold detector form the engineering basis of the book's UV exposure monitor. The measured quantity is charge, $Q=\int I_{photo}\,dt$, which can be calibrated to optical energy only with the sensor's spectral responsivity, filter transmission and geometry defined. Wavelength weighting matters: equal broadband optical power need not produce equal sensor current. An electronic threshold is an instrument setting, not by itself a biological exposure calibration.

For an original example, 2 nA into 2 µF gives a 1 mV/s ramp and 0.6 V after ten minutes. At one-tenth light level the ramp is 0.1 mV/s; an extra 0.1 nA leakage is now a 50% error. Increasing the capacitor reduces the ramp but does not improve signal-to-leakage ratio. A TIA followed by a calibrated current divider can separate the sensitive input from the large storage capacitor, but adds offset, ratio and range errors.

Another approach integrates on a small capacitor and counts repeated fixed charge increments. With $C=100$ pF and a 1 V threshold excursion, each cycle represents 100 pC; 2 nA gives 20 counts/s and 12000 counts in ten minutes. A 5 µs reset loses about 0.01% of continuous input charge at that rate, but 1 pC reset charge is a 1% error per increment. Counter resolution can improve while analog charge accuracy remains limited. Calibrate the charge packet and include dark current, threshold drift and reset dead time.

<section id="check">

<div className="section-heading"><span>07</span><h2>Check your understanding</h2></div>

<details>
<summary>1. How long can an ideal integrator tolerate 100 µV of offset with $R_{IN}$ = 10 kΩ and $C_F$ = 10 nF?</summary>
<div>

The offset is integrated at 100 µV/(10 kΩ × 10 nF) = 1 V/s, so a ±4.5 V output saturates in about **4.5 s**. In practice bias current adds a similar rate. Without a feedback resistor or a reset, no integration longer than a few seconds is meaningful.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>2. What does $R_F$ across the capacitor do, and what does it cost?</summary>
<div>

It gives the circuit a finite DC gain, $-R_F/R_{IN}$, so offsets settle at a bounded value instead of ramping. The cost is that the circuit integrates only above $1/(2\pi R_FC_F)$, and stored results decay with time constant $R_FC_F$.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>3. In the triangle oscillator, why does the supply voltage cancel out of the frequency?</summary>
<div>

The ramp rate is proportional to the comparator's output swing $V_S$, and so are the switching thresholds, $\pm V_SR_2/R_3$. Doubling $V_S$ doubles both, so each half-cycle takes the same time.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>4. Why must the oscillator's comparator be noninverting?</summary>
<div>

The integrator inverts. For the loop to reverse the ramp at each threshold, the comparator must switch in the direction that sends the integrator back. With an inverting comparator the loop has the wrong overall sign: the ramp drives the comparator to the state that continues the same ramp, and the circuit latches at a rail.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>5. Why is a charge amplifier's gain independent of the sensor's cable capacitance?</summary>
<div>

The summing node is a virtual ground, so the cable's capacitance, connected from that node to ground, never has any voltage across it and takes no charge. All the sensor's charge goes onto $C_F$. The cable capacitance raises the noise gain, and hence the noise, but not the signal gain.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>6. What is integrator windup?</summary>
<div>

When a control loop's actuator saturates, the error cannot be corrected, but the integrator keeps accumulating it. When the error finally reverses, the integrator must first unwind all that accumulated charge, causing a large, slow overshoot. Anti-windup limits the integrator during saturation.

</div>
</details>
