<h1>Difference amplifiers and resistor CMRR<br/><em>Subtract two large voltages, keep the small difference.</em></h1>

<p className="dek">Derive the four-resistor difference amplifier without assuming the ratios match, separate its differential and common-mode gains, find how resistor mismatch sets its CMRR, and check it statistically and in a real current-sensing circuit.</p>

<div className="chapter-meta"><span>50–70 min</span><span>Inverting and noninverting amplifiers (Chs. 83–84) · Pair CMRR (Ch. 71)</span></div>

</div>

<div className="opening-grid">

<div><span className="tiny-label">PREDICT BEFORE CALCULATING</span><p className="opening-question">A difference amplifier is built from four 0.1% resistors. How large an error can it make on a 10 mV signal riding on 5 V?</p></div>

<div>

Many measurements are differences: the voltage across a current shunt in a 12 V supply line, the output of a bridge whose both ends sit at half the supply, the signal on a cable whose ground is not the receiver's ground. Each needs a circuit that responds to the difference between two inputs and ignores what they share.

One op amp and four resistors can do it: an inverting path for one input and a noninverting path for the other. If the resistor ratios match, the common part cancels exactly. They never match exactly, and because the common part may be a thousand times larger than the difference, a tiny mismatch can be a large error. This chapter quantifies it.

</div>

</div>

<div className="learning-map">

<span className="tiny-label">THE PATH THROUGH THIS CHAPTER</span><p>Write both input paths → Expose differential and common-mode terms → Compare ratio mismatch at high common mode → Worst case and statistics → A high-side current sensor → Check source and input range together → Check your understanding.</p>

</div>

<section id="model">

<div className="section-heading"><span>01</span><h2>Write both input paths</h2></div>

<OpAmpFigure mode="difference"/>

$V_1$ drives the minus input through $R_1$, with feedback $R_2$: an inverting amplifier. $V_2$ reaches the plus input through the divider $R_3$, $R_4$: a noninverting path. Define the two ratios $a = R_2/R_1$ and $b = R_4/R_3$. The plus input sits at $bV_2/(1 + b)$; feedback makes the minus input follow it; superposition gives the output.

Equal nominal resistors give unity subtraction only if their ratios are actually equal. A source resistance in series with either input changes that input's ratio, so a mismatch in source impedances is a mismatch in ratios.

</section>

<section id="derive">

<div className="section-heading"><span>02</span><h2>Expose differential and common-mode terms</h2></div>

<Equation tex={String.raw`V_O=-aV_1+cV_2,\qquad c=\frac{(1+a)\,b}{1+b}`}/>

Rewrite the inputs as a common-mode voltage and a difference, $V_1 = V_C - v_d/2$ and $V_2 = V_C + v_d/2$:

<Equation tex={String.raw`V_O=A_d\,v_d+A_{cm}V_C,\qquad A_d=\frac{a+c}{2},\quad A_{cm}=c-a,\quad\mathrm{CMRR}=\left|\frac{A_d}{A_{cm}}\right|`} label="DIFFERENCE AMPLIFIER GAINS"/>

If $a = b = G$, then $c = G$, $A_{cm} = 0$ and $V_O = G(V_2 - V_1)$. Now let the ratios differ slightly, $b = a(1 + \epsilon)$. To first order,

<Equation tex={String.raw`A_{cm}\approx\frac{a\,\epsilon}{1+a},\qquad\mathrm{CMRR}\approx\frac{1+G}{|\epsilon|}`} label="RESISTOR-LIMITED CMRR"/>

$\epsilon$ is the mismatch between the two **ratios**, not the tolerance of one resistor. Four resistors each within ±0.1% can make the ratios differ by up to 0.4%.

</section>

<section id="cases">

<div className="section-heading"><span>03</span><h2>Compare ratio mismatch at high common mode</h2></div>

<div className="worked-example">

<span className="tiny-label">WORKED EXAMPLE / 0.1% OF RATIO MISMATCH</span><h3>Unity gain, $a$ = 1, $b$ = 1.001</h3>

<p>$c = 2 × 1.001/2.001$ = 1.0005, so $A_d$ = 1.00025 and $A_{cm}$ = 0.0005. CMRR = 2000, about <strong>66 dB</strong>.</p>

<p>Now the opening question: $V_C$ = 5 V with $v_d$ = 10 mV. The common-mode leakage is 0.0005 × 5 V = <strong>2.5 mV</strong> at the output, beside the wanted 10 mV. A 0.1% resistor error has become a <strong>25%</strong> measurement error. With four independent 0.1% parts the ratio mismatch can reach 0.4%, and the error 100%.</p>

<p>Better resistors improve this in proportion: ratio mismatches of 0.01% and 0.001% give 86 and 106 dB. Resistor networks, made side by side on one substrate, specify ratio tracking of 0.01% or better even when their absolute tolerance is 0.1% or worse.</p>

</div>

**Higher gain helps.** At gain 10 with matched ratios, a 0.1% mismatch gives $(1 + 10)/0.001$ = 11 000, 81 dB, because the common-mode error stays roughly the same size while the wanted output is ten times larger. But the plus input now sits at $(10/11)V_C$: a 5 V common mode puts 4.55 V on the op amp's input, which a 5 V-supply op amp may not accept even though the output is only 100 mV.

The op amp's own CMRR adds to the resistors'. If the op amp has 100 dB and the resistors give 66 dB, the resistors dominate; the combined value (adding the two common-mode gains, as fractions, with their signs) is at worst slightly below the poorer one.

</section>

<section id="monte">

<div className="section-heading"><span>04</span><h2>Worst case and statistics</h2></div>

A worst-case analysis assumes every resistor is at the edge of its tolerance in the most damaging direction. A **Monte Carlo** analysis draws many random sets of resistor values and looks at the distribution of results:

<CmrrMonteCarloFigure/>

Both are useful, for different questions. "Will every unit meet 54 dB?" needs the worst case: yes. "How many units will reach 60 dB?" needs the distribution: about 92%. Production test or trimming can then reject or adjust the tail. If every unit must exceed 80 dB, no selection of individual 0.1% resistors will do it reliably; a matched network or a monolithic difference amplifier will.

</section>

<section id="sense">

<div className="section-heading"><span>05</span><h2>A high-side current sensor</h2></div>

<CurrentSenseCircuit/>

<div className="worked-example">

<span className="tiny-label">WORKED EXAMPLE / MEASURE 0–2 A IN A 12 V LINE</span><h3>50 mΩ shunt, gain 20, 1% resistors versus a 0.05% network</h3>

<p><strong>Signal.</strong> 2 A through 50 mΩ is 100 mV, amplified 20 times to 2 V: 1 V/A. The shunt dissipates 0.2 W at full current.</p>

<p><strong>Common mode.</strong> Both shunt terminals sit near 12 V. With 1% resistors the ratio mismatch can be 4% in the worst case, and the CMRR $(1 + 20)/0.04$ = 525. The common-mode gain is $A_{cm} \approx a\epsilon/(1 + a)$ = 20 × 0.04/21 = 0.038, so the 12 V common mode produces <strong>0.46 V</strong> of output error, equivalent to 0.46 A. Useless.</p>

<p>With a 0.05% matched network, $\epsilon \le$ 0.1% worst case, $A_{cm}$ = 0.00095 and the error is 11 mV, 11 mA. And when the 12 V supply varies by ±1 V, the error changes by only ±1 mA.</p>

<p><strong>Range.</strong> The op amp's plus input sits at 12 × 100/105 = 11.4 V. An op amp on a 5 V supply cannot accept that. Either power the op amp from more than about 13 V, or use a difference amplifier with an attenuating input network, or use a dedicated high-side current-sense amplifier whose input stage runs above its own supply.</p>

</div>

</section>

<section id="limits">

<div className="section-heading"><span>06</span><h2>Check source and input range together</h2></div>

**Unequal input impedances.** The minus input looks like $R_1$ to its source; the plus input looks like $R_3 + R_4$. A common-mode voltage therefore draws different currents from the two inputs. If the sources have unequal impedances, the voltages reaching the resistors differ, which is a ratio mismatch. With 10 kΩ input resistors, a 100 Ω source on one input and none on the other is a 1% ratio error, limiting CMRR to 46 dB at unity gain. Buffer the inputs (Chapter 86) when source impedances are significant or unequal.

**AC common-mode rejection.** Stray capacitances across the resistors, if unequal, make the ratios frequency dependent. A difference amplifier with 90 dB of DC CMRR may have 50 dB at 100 kHz. Symmetric layout and matched capacitors across $R_2$ and $R_4$ help; so does filtering both inputs identically.

**Ratings and faults.** The common-mode voltage appears across the input resistors. If an input can see a fault voltage, the resistors must withstand it and the op amp's input must be protected. Check the internal plus-input voltage, the output swing and the resistors' power at the worst common mode.

<div className="takeaway"><span className="tiny-label">THE IDEA TO KEEP</span><p>A four-resistor difference amplifier rejects common mode only as well as its two ratios match: $\mathrm{CMRR} \approx (1 + G)/\epsilon$. Use <em>matched resistor networks</em>, equal source impedances, and check the internal input voltage at the worst common mode.</p></div>

</section>

<section id="precision-depth">

<div className="section-heading"><span>+</span><h2>Preserve Kelvin accuracy and common-mode rejection in real wiring</h2></div>

<h3>Difference, differential input and differential output describe different things</h3>

A four-resistor **difference amplifier** computes a scaled difference but presents finite and generally unequal input impedances. An **instrumentation amplifier** adds high-impedance input stages so the source does not set the resistor ratios. A **fully differential amplifier** supplies two output voltages and a controlled output common mode. One device may combine several of these properties; the name alone does not establish input loading or common-mode range.

With matched ratios $R_2/R_1=R_4/R_3=k$ and the lower noninverting resistor returned to $V_R$, nodal analysis gives

<Equation tex={String.raw`v_o=k(v_2-v_1)+V_R`}/>

This supports ground-offset cancellation, low-side current sensing, balanced-line reception and translating a bipolar difference to a single-supply output. $V_R$ must be driven at sufficiently low impedance: its source resistance adds to $R_4$ and alters both gain and CMRR. An integrated subtractor can include a precise network, but still needs valid input voltages, supply headroom and a suitable reference driver.

<h3>Sense the shunt, not its current-carrying connections</h3>

A 20 mΩ shunt at 2 A develops 40 mV and dissipates 80 mW. Gain 50 produces 2 V. Including an extra 5 mΩ solder/trace resistance in a two-wire voltage measurement raises the measured voltage to 50 mV, a **25% error**. Route separate Kelvin sense traces from the shunt element's terminals. The sense traces carry negligible signal current, and must not share the large load-current return path.

Low-side sensing shifts the load's local ground and can miss a current path that bypasses the shunt. High-side sensing preserves the ground connection but places a small difference on a much larger common-mode voltage. A ground-referenced op amp is not automatically suitable just because a resistor divider lowers its pin voltages; check divider currents, power, transient voltage, common-mode rejection and protection. Dedicated high-side current-sense architectures can solve that port problem.

At 10% of the 2 A full scale, the shunt voltage is only 4 mV. A 40 µV offset is already 1% of that reading, even though it is only 0.1% of full scale. A 1% shunt and 0.1% amplifier gain accuracy cannot establish 1% of reading after zero errors and temperature drift are added.

<h3>Source and RC imbalance limit AC rejection</h3>

Equal DC resistor ratios do not ensure equal AC ratios. A 100 kΩ source feeding 10 pF extra input capacitance has a pole near 159 kHz; the same capacitance behind 1 kΩ has a pole near 15.9 MHz. A 10 kHz common-mode disturbance is therefore delayed differently in the two arms and becomes a differential signal. Match the complete impedance ratios, including source resistance and protection capacitance, or buffer the sources first.

For a simplified pair of otherwise unity transfers, a time-constant mismatch $\Delta\tau$ creates $|H_1-H_2|\approx\omega|\Delta\tau|$ well below the poles. A 100 ns mismatch at 10 kHz leaves 0.00628 of common mode, only about **44 dB** rejection. The amplifier's intrinsic 120 dB DC CMRR cannot repair an imbalance outside its input terminals.

Balanced cable receivers also need equal input impedances to preserve balance. Termination, common-mode interference, source grounding and cable capacitance must be considered together. Equalization can compensate known differential cable loss, but raises high-frequency noise and does not solve common-mode overrange.

<details>
<summary>Worked self-check: what does a ground error do?</summary>
<div>

A shunt gives 10 mV difference while both sense points sit 200 mV above the receiver's ground. The ideal gain-50 subtractor produces 0.5 V relative to its reference. A common-mode gain of 0.002 adds 0.4 mV output error. If one input is mistakenly taken from receiver ground instead of the shunt's lower Kelvin terminal, the circuit measures approximately 210 mV and requests 10.5 V, likely clipping completely.

</div>

<section id="check">

<div className="section-heading"><span>07</span><h2>Check your understanding</h2></div>

<details>
<summary>1. How large an error can four 0.1% resistors make on 10 mV at 5 V common mode?</summary>
<div>

A 0.1% ratio mismatch gives a common-mode gain of about 0.0005 at unity gain, so 5 V leaks 2.5 mV: 25% of the 10 mV signal. With all four resistors at the worst edge, the ratio mismatch can reach 0.4% and the error 10 mV, 100%.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>2. Why does the CMRR depend on ratio matching rather than on absolute resistor values?</summary>
<div>

The common-mode gain is $c - a$, where $a = R_2/R_1$ and $c$ depends on $b = R_4/R_3$. If all four resistors are 1% high, both ratios are unchanged and the CMRR is unaffected; only the difference between the ratios matters.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>3. A unity-gain difference amplifier needs 80 dB of CMRR from its resistors. What ratio mismatch is allowed?</summary>
<div>

80 dB is 10 000, and CMRR ≈ $(1 + G)/\epsilon$ = 2/$\epsilon$, so $\epsilon \le$ **0.02%**. Individual resistors cannot guarantee that; a matched network specified for ratio tracking can.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>4. Why does increasing the gain improve the resistor-limited CMRR?</summary>
<div>

The common-mode gain $a\epsilon/(1 + a)$ approaches $\epsilon$ for large $a$, roughly constant, while the differential gain grows as $a$. Their ratio, the CMRR, grows as $1 + a$.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>5. What is the difference between a worst-case and a Monte Carlo analysis of CMRR?</summary>
<div>

The worst case assumes every resistor at its tolerance limit in the most damaging combination, giving a guaranteed minimum (54 dB for four 0.1% parts at unity gain). Monte Carlo samples random values and gives the distribution of what real units achieve (median near 68 dB). One sets a guarantee, the other predicts yield.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>6. A 100 Ω source drives one input of a 10 kΩ unity-gain difference amplifier. What CMRR results?</summary>
<div>

That input's resistor is effectively 10.1 kΩ, a 1% ratio error. CMRR ≈ 2/0.01 = 200, about **46 dB**, regardless of how good the four resistors are. Buffering the inputs, as an instrumentation amplifier does, removes the problem.

</div>
</details>
