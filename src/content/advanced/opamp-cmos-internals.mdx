<div><span className="tiny-label">PREDICT BEFORE CALCULATING</span><p className="opening-question">A CMOS op amp's gates draw almost no current. Does that make it accurate near both supply rails?</p></div>

<div>

Most op amps made today are CMOS. They run from 1.8 V or 5 V supplies, draw microamps, have inputs that take picoamps, and are advertised as "rail-to-rail". Each of those claims comes from a specific circuit choice, and each holds under specific conditions.

This chapter builds the CMOS op amp from the MOSFET chapters (57–76) and then examines four of its specifications closely. It also compares the result with the bipolar op amp of Chapter 75, because choosing between them (Chapter 77) depends on exactly these differences.

<div className="section-heading"><span>01</span><h2>Build the op amp from the MOS studies</h2></div>

<InternalAmplifierFigure technology="mos"/>

This is Chapter 70's amplifier: an NMOS pair with a PMOS mirror load (Chapter 67), an NMOS common-source gain stage with a PMOS current-source load (Chapter 60), and a Miller capacitor that splits the poles. The ideal tail and fixed PMOS bias stand for real bias networks (Chapters 54–58). The bodies are drawn tied to their sources to keep the algebra simple; in a substrate-based process the NMOS bodies are grounded, which shifts thresholds and changes the input range.

The gates draw only leakage at DC, which is the CMOS op amp's great advantage for high-impedance sources. They still present capacitance, sit behind protection diodes that leak, and have voltage ratings.

<section id="derive">

<div className="section-heading"><span>02</span><h2>Translate device parameters into amplifier behavior</h2></div>

<Equation tex={String.raw`g_m=\frac{2I_D}{u},\qquad A_0\approx g_{m1}R_1\,g_{m2}R_2,\qquad f_u\approx\frac{g_{m1}}{2\pi C_C},\qquad SR=\frac{I_T}{C_C}=u_0\cdot2\pi f_u`}/>

The last relation is Chapter 75's slew rate per unit bandwidth with $I_T/g_{m1} = u_0$ for a MOS pair. At 0.2 V of overdrive that is four times the bipolar value: CMOS op amps slew faster for the same bandwidth, and pay for it in voltage noise and offset, because $g_m$ per microamp is lower.

<div className="worked-example">

<span className="tiny-label">WORKED EXAMPLE / THREE INPUT-PAIR DESIGNS AT 50 µA PER SIDE</span><h3>Overdrive 0.1, 0.2 and 0.4 V, $C_C$ = 20 pF</h3>

<p>$g_m = 2I_D/u$ = 1.0, 0.5 and 0.25 mS per device. (Each overdrive needs a different device width at the same current, not a different gate voltage on the same device.) The first-stage transconductance $g_{m1}$ is the same as one device's, so $f_u = g_{m1}/2\pi C_C$ = <strong>8.0, 4.0 and 2.0 MHz</strong>.</p>

<p>The slew rate is $I_T/C_C$ = 100 µA / 20 pF = <strong>5 V/µs</strong> in all three. So the lowest overdrive gives the most bandwidth and the least noise for the same slew rate, but needs the widest devices, with more capacitance and worse matching relative to signal (Chapter 54).</p>

</div>



<h3 id="book-notes">Constant total tail current does not guarantee constant MOS transconductance</h3>

With matched effective device factors in strong inversion, each complementary input pair's small-signal transconductance is proportional to the square root of its tail current. If a fixed total current $I$ is divided as fractions x and $1-x$ between the two pairs, the pairs' transconductances are $\sqrt{2k\,xI/2}$ and $\sqrt{2k(1-x)I/2}$ (per device, each device carrying half its pair's tail). Their sum, divided by the value $\sqrt{kI}$ when one pair carries everything, is

<Equation tex={String.raw`\frac{G_{m,tot}}{G_{m,edge}}=\sqrt{x}+\sqrt{1-x}`}/>

<RailGmExtensionFigure/>

At an edge, only one pair operates and the ratio is 1. At equal current sharing it is $\sqrt2=\mathbf{1.414}$. Thus even perfectly constant total tail current leaves a 41.4% transconductance hill in this square-law example. With a fixed compensation capacitor that can move crossover frequency and change phase margin as common-mode input changes.

In bipolar or weak-inversion input stages, transconductance is approximately proportional to current, so constant current sum is a more direct solution. Strong-inversion CMOS needs a different allocation or extra effective device width. For example, keeping the same summed transconductance when only one equal-width pair remains active requires four times one pair's previous tail current, because its transconductance must double. That is a power tradeoff, not a free rail-range extension.

Real complementary pairs have different mobilities, dimensions, offsets and handover shapes. Constant-$g_m$ control addresses bandwidth variation; it does not automatically remove an offset step or switching-current disturbance during handover. Test distortion and settling across common mode as well as at mid-supply.

<section id="cases">

<div className="section-heading"><span>03</span><h2>Input range and complementary pairs</h2></div>

An NMOS input pair needs its gates a $V_{GS}$ plus a tail compliance above the negative rail. On a 3.3 V supply with $V_T$ = 0.7 V, 0.2 V of overdrive and 0.15 V for the tail, the lowest common-mode input is about 1.05 V. If the mirror's reference drain is at 2.4 V, the input devices leave saturation above about 3.1 V. A sensor whose output sits at 0.2 V cannot drive this input at all.

A PMOS pair has the mirror-image range: it works down to below ground and stops about a volt short of the top rail. Put both in parallel, with their output currents combined, and the input works from rail to rail:

<RailToRailInputFigure/>

The total transconductance is not constant: it doubles in the middle, where both pairs are on. Since $f_u \propto g_{m1}$, the unity-gain frequency doubles too, and the phase margin falls. Offset changes as the input crosses from one pair to the other, because the two pairs have different offsets: a common-mode-dependent offset that looks like poor CMRR. Good rail-to-rail input stages use constant-$g_m$ circuits that reduce each pair's tail current where both conduct, or avoid the problem with a single pair and an internal charge pump that lifts its supply above the rail.

That answers the opening question: zero DC gate current does not by itself give accurate operation near the rails. Near the rails only one pair works, the input stage's characteristics change, and the CMRR specification often excludes the transition region.

</section>

<section id="output">

<div className="section-heading"><span>04</span><h2>Rail-to-rail outputs and their load</h2></div>

A source-follower output (Chapter 69) can never get closer to a rail than one $V_{GS}$, because its gate would have to go beyond the rail. CMOS op amps instead use **common-source** output devices, with drains at the output:

<RroOutputCircuit/>

With no load, a saturated common-source device can pull its drain to within millivolts of its source, the rail. With a load current $I_L$, it is a resistance $R_{on}$ in triode, and the output stops short by $I_LR_{on}$:

<RroSwingFigure/>

<div className="worked-example">

<span className="tiny-label">WORKED EXAMPLE / HOW RAIL-TO-RAIL IS IT?</span><h3>3.3 V supply, output devices with 50 Ω on-resistance</h3>

<p><strong>Light load</strong> (100 kΩ to mid-supply, 16 µA): the output reaches within 1 mV of either rail, plus a few millivolts of internal headroom.</p>

<p><strong>2 kΩ load to mid-supply:</strong> at a 3.2 V output the load draws 0.8 mA, so the PMOS drops 40 mV. Swing: about 0.05 V to 3.25 V.</p>

<p><strong>200 Ω load:</strong> about 6.6 mA at the extremes, a 0.33 V drop. Swing: about 0.33 V to 2.97 V. The output is still "rail-to-rail" in the advertisement, but not in this circuit.</p>

<p>The common-source output also has a high open-loop output resistance, kilohms rather than tens of ohms. Feedback divides it by the loop gain, which falls with frequency, so at high frequency the output impedance rises and interacts with capacitive loads (Chapter 88).</p>

</div>

</section>

<section id="leakage">

<div className="section-heading"><span>05</span><h2>Bias current, leakage and temperature</h2></div>

A MOS gate's own leakage is tiny, but every input is protected by diodes to the rails, and their reverse leakage is the real bias current. Diode leakage roughly doubles every 10 °C:

<LeakageTempFigure/>

<div className="worked-example">

<span className="tiny-label">WORKED EXAMPLE / A HIGH-IMPEDANCE SENSOR AT TEMPERATURE</span><h3>100 kΩ source, noise gain 100</h3>

<p>At 25 °C, 1 pA through 100 kΩ is 100 nV: negligible. At 85 °C the bias current is $2^6$ = 64 times larger, 64 pA, and the error 6.4 µV, 0.64 mV at the output.</p>

<p>Now add a slightly contaminated circuit board with 1 nA of surface leakage into the input node: 100 µV at the input, <strong>10 mV at the output</strong>, far more than the op amp itself. At picoamp levels, the assembly sets the accuracy: guard rings, clean boards, and keeping the input node off the board entirely in extreme cases.</p>

</div>

<section id="limits">

<div className="section-heading"><span>06</span><h2>Treat rail range and low noise as separate goals</h2></div>

**Flicker noise.** CMOS input devices have far higher $1/f$ noise than bipolar ones; corner frequencies of 1–10 kHz are common (Chapter 71). Large input devices lower it, at the cost of capacitance. For DC precision, auto-zero and chopper CMOS amplifiers modulate the input and remove both offset and flicker noise (Chapter 77), introducing switching artifacts of their own.

**Mismatch.** CMOS offsets are typically millivolts untrimmed, larger than bipolar's, because threshold matching is poorer than $V_{BE}$ matching. Trimming at the factory or auto-zeroing brings them to microvolts.

**Low voltage.** Every overdrive and threshold eats into a 1.8 V supply. Designs there use weak inversion, wide-swing biasing (Chapter 58), and single-stage or folded architectures rather than stacked cascodes.

**The complete comparison.** A CMOS op amp is not simply "better" than a bipolar one: it trades voltage noise and offset for bias current, slew rate and supply range. Chapter 77 turns these trades into a selection method.

<div className="takeaway"><span className="tiny-label">THE IDEA TO KEEP</span><p>CMOS op amps offer picoamp inputs (doubling every 10 °C), rail-to-rail inputs <em>with a transconductance and offset that change across the range</em>, and outputs that reach the rails only at light load.</p></div>

</section>

<section id="precision-depth">

<div className="section-heading"><span>07</span><h2>Evaluate RRIO accuracy across common mode and load</h2></div>

<h3>Rail-to-rail input range can contain an accuracy transition</h3>

Two complementary input pairs can have different offsets. If their effective offsets are −20 µV and +30 µV, a smooth handover across a 0.5 V common-mode interval changes input-equivalent offset by 50 µV. The local effective CMRR is about $0.5/50\times10^{-6}=10000$, or **80 dB**, even when either pair alone has much better rejection. At noise gain 100 this is a 5 mV output change. A common-mode sweep can therefore expose a nonlinearity missed by testing at a single DC bias.

Constant-transconductance control stabilizes bandwidth but does not automatically match those offsets or cancel the two pairs' current noise. A single input pair powered by an internal charge pump can avoid pair handover; the extra switching can introduce ripple, startup behavior and new filtering requirements. Check the architecture's consequences at the source impedance and bandwidth of the actual circuit.

<h3>Output swing, output impedance and output accuracy are different tests</h3>

Near a rail, the conducting MOSFET approaches triode operation. A 40 Ω effective on-resistance supplying 2 mA needs roughly 80 mV headroom. Feedback cannot remove this headroom once the gate drive has reached its limit. With lighter loading the output may get much closer; measure source and sink directions separately.

Away from the rail, a common-source output can have appreciable **open-loop** resistance. Its gain depends on the load, so $A\beta$ can fall under heavy loading. The same load then affects gain error, distortion and stability, not just swing. Internal local feedback can shape this resistance with frequency; a single unloaded DC number is insufficient. Capacitive loads add an especially important extra state, developed in Chapter 88.

For an ADC with a 0–3.3 V range, a 10 mV residual output floor loses roughly 199 codes of a 16-bit conversion, because one LSB is 50.35 µV. “Rail-to-rail output” alone cannot establish use of the last code. A pull-down or extra negative rail can improve the floor in some architectures, but changes loading, dissipation and power sequencing; verify the amplifier and converter constraints together.

<h3>Class-AB current control keeps the handover smooth</h3>

An efficient output stage should keep a small overlap current near zero load yet deliver much larger current on demand. One useful idealized control law is $I_NI_P=I_Q^2$ together with $I_N-I_P=I_L$. Substitute $I_P = I_N - I_L$ into the product: $I_N^2 - I_LI_N - I_Q^2 = 0$. The quadratic formula, keeping the positive root because currents are positive, gives

<Equation tex={String.raw`I_N=\frac{I_L+\sqrt{I_L^2+4I_Q^2}}2,\qquad I_P=\frac{-I_L+\sqrt{I_L^2+4I_Q^2}}2`}/>

With $I_Q=100$ µA and $I_L=1$ mA, the branches carry about 1.010 mA and 9.90 µA. The weak branch remains conducting, reducing abrupt crossover. This is an explanatory current-control model, not a transistor-level claim about every class-AB IC. Real bias circuits must remain controlled across temperature, supply, load and process spread; too much overlap wastes power, while too little increases distortion.

<h3>Test RRIO across a grid of operating points</h3>

Use at least low, middle and high common-mode voltages, both load polarities, minimum intended supply and temperature endpoints. At each point check offset, large-step recovery, distortion and swing. Input range and output range can fail independently: an inverting circuit holds the inputs near a fixed reference while its output swings, whereas a follower sweeps both. That is why the same chip can look good in one connection and poor in another.

<details>
<summary>Worked self-check: where should a ground-referenced sensor be biased?</summary>
<div>

Suppose an amplifier's accuracy transition occupies 1.2–1.7 V on a 3.3 V supply. Biasing a ±100 mV follower signal at 1.45 V traverses it every cycle. Biasing at 0.8 V avoids that transition if the specified common-mode range and output swing support 0.7–0.9 V. Changing the bias is useful only after checking those independent limits and the destination's required reference.

</div>
</details>

<section id="check">

<div className="section-heading"><span>08</span><h2>Check your understanding</h2></div>

<details>
<summary>1. Does negligible gate current guarantee accurate operation near both rails?</summary>
<div>

No. Input accuracy near the rails depends on the input stage's common-mode range. A single NMOS pair stops working about a volt above the negative rail; a complementary pair works rail to rail but changes transconductance and offset at the transition, and the CMRR is often specified only away from it.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>2. On 3.3 V with $V_T$ = 0.7 V, 0.2 V overdrive and 0.15 V tail compliance, what is an NMOS pair's lowest common-mode input?</summary>
<div>

$V_T + u + u_T$ = 0.7 + 0.2 + 0.15 = **1.05 V** above the negative rail. A PMOS pair, or a complementary pair, is needed to go lower.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>3. Why does a complementary input stage's unity-gain frequency change across the input range?</summary>
<div>

$f_u = g_{m1}/2\pi C_C$, and in the middle of the range both pairs contribute transconductance, doubling $g_{m1}$. Near either rail only one pair works. Without a constant-$g_m$ circuit, $f_u$ (and the phase margin, noise and offset) varies with common mode.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>4. Why can a common-source output stage reach the rails when a source-follower stage cannot?</summary>
<div>

A source follower's output is one $V_{GS}$ away from its gate, and its gate cannot go beyond the rail, so the output stops a threshold plus an overdrive short. A common-source device's output is its drain, which can approach its source (the rail) to within $I_LR_{on}$, without any gate voltage beyond the rail.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>5. A CMOS op amp's bias current is 1 pA at 25 °C. Roughly what is it at 105 °C?</summary>
<div>

Doubling every 10 °C over 80 °C is $2^8$ = 256 times: about **256 pA**. Board leakage often grows faster still.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>6. Why do CMOS op amps typically slew faster than bipolar ones of the same bandwidth?</summary>
<div>

$SR = (I_T/g_{m1})\cdot2\pi f_u$. For a MOS pair $I_T/g_{m1} = u_0$, typically 0.1–0.5 V; for a bipolar pair it is $2U_T$ ≈ 52 mV. The MOS pair needs more tail current for the same transconductance, and that current charges $C_C$ faster.

</div>
</details>
