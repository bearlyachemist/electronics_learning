<div className="opening-grid">

<div><span className="tiny-label">PREDICT BEFORE CALCULATING</span><p className="opening-question">Adding a resistor in series with an op amp's input can reduce its output error. When does it also make things worse?</p></div>

<div>

An ideal op amp with zero input gives zero output. A real one gives a few millivolts, and that error stays put as a signal arrives, adding to every reading. In an audio stage nobody cares; in a thermocouple amplifier, where 1 °C is 41 µV, a 1 mV offset is a 25 °C error.

Chapters 65–67 showed where these errors come from inside the chip: mismatched thresholds, bias currents, finite gain. This chapter treats the op amp as a black box with specified errors, puts each at the node where it acts, and adds them up honestly. The result is an error budget, the document that decides which op amp a precision design needs.

</div>

</div>

<section id="model">

<div className="section-heading"><span>01</span><h2>Assign error sources to specific nodes</h2></div>

<ErrorModelCircuit/>

Model the real op amp as an ideal one with three error sources at its inputs. The offset voltage $V_{OS}$ is the differential input needed to bring the output to zero, drawn as a small battery in series with one input. The bias currents $I_{B+}$ and $I_{B-}$ flow into the two inputs; take them as **signed**, positive into the amplifier, because their signs decide whether they cancel or add. Their difference is the **offset current** $I_{OS}$.

Keep the specifications distinct. Offset voltage is not the $v_o/A$ that finite gain requires. Bias current is a DC current, not current-noise density. Drift is a change with temperature, and a datasheet's "typical" is not a guaranteed maximum. Read each specification at the supply, common-mode voltage, temperature and load you will actually use.

</section>

<section id="derive">

<div className="section-heading"><span>02</span><h2>Solve the signed noninverting equation</h2></div>

KCL at the minus input includes $I_{B-}$; the plus input sits at $V_{IN} - I_{B+}R_{SRC}$; the amplifier sees $V_{OS}$ added to its input. Solve it step by step.

<div className="derivation">

<span className="tiny-label">DERIVATION / THE NONINVERTING AMPLIFIER WITH EVERY DC ERROR</span>

1. **KCL at the minus input.** Current leaves through $R_G$ to ground, through $R_F$ to the output, and into the pin as $I_{B-}$:
<Equation tex={String.raw`\frac{v_-}{R_G}+\frac{v_--v_o}{R_F}+I_{B-}=0`}/>

2. **Solve for $v_-$.** Multiply by $R_F\parallel R_G$, and note $(R_F\parallel R_G)/R_F = R_G/(R_F+R_G) = \beta$:
<Equation tex={String.raw`v_-=\beta v_o-I_{B-}\,(R_F\parallel R_G)`}/>
   The bias current pulls the minus input below the divider voltage.

3. **The plus input.** $I_{B+}$ flows into the pin through the source resistance, so $v_+ = V_{IN} - I_{B+}R_{SRC}$.

4. **The amplifier law.** Model the offset as a source in series with the plus input: $v_o = A_0(v_+ + V_{OS} - v_-)$. Substitute steps 2 and 3:
<Equation tex={String.raw`v_o=A_0\Bigl[V_{IN}+V_{OS}-I_{B+}R_{SRC}+I_{B-}(R_F\parallel R_G)-\beta v_o\Bigr]`}/>

5. **Collect $v_o$.** Move $A_0\beta v_o$ to the left and divide by $1 + A_0\beta$. Then divide numerator and denominator by $A_0\beta$ and write $NG = 1/\beta$:
<Equation tex={String.raw`v_o=\frac{A_0}{1+A_0\beta}\Bigl[\cdots\Bigr]=\frac{NG}{1+NG/A_0}\Bigl[\cdots\Bigr]`}/>

</div>

The complete result, with the bracket written out:

<Equation tex={String.raw`v_o=\frac{NG}{1+NG/A_0}\Bigl[V_{IN}+V_{OS}-I_{B+}R_{SRC}+I_{B-}\,(R_F\parallel R_G)\Bigr],\qquad NG=1+\frac{R_F}{R_G}`} label="NONINVERTING AMPLIFIER WITH DC ERRORS"/>

Read the bracket as the input-referred error. Every term in it is multiplied by the noise gain, just like the signal. $I_{B-}$ flows out through $R_F$, so the output must rise to supply it; $I_{B+}$ flowing through $R_{SRC}$ pulls the plus input down. If the two bias currents are equal and the two input resistances are equal, $R_{SRC} = R_F \parallel R_G$, the bias terms cancel, leaving only $I_{OS}$ times that resistance.

Three more errors enter the same bracket. **Finite gain** produces a gain error of $-NG/A_0$ times the reading. **Common-mode rejection** turns a common-mode change $\Delta V_{CM}$ into an input error $\Delta V_{CM}/\text{CMRR}$. **Power-supply rejection** does the same for supply changes.

</section>

<section id="cases">

<div className="section-heading"><span>03</span><h2>Compare source resistances</h2></div>

<ErrorVsSourceFigure/>

<div className="worked-example">

<span className="tiny-label">WORKED EXAMPLE / SIGNED TERMS THAT CANCEL AND ADD</span><h3>$R_F$ = 100 kΩ, $R_G$ = 10 kΩ, $V_{OS}$ = +100 µV, $I_{B+}$ = 50 nA, $I_{B-}$ = 55 nA</h3>

<p>$R_F \parallel R_G$ = 9.09 kΩ, so the $I_{B-}$ term is +500 µV at the input. With source resistances of 0, 9.09 kΩ and 100 kΩ, the $I_{B+}$ term is 0, −455 and −5000 µV. The totals are <strong>+600, +145 and −4400 µV</strong>, and at the output, times 11: +6.6 mV, +1.6 mV and −48.4 mV.</p>

<p>Matching the resistances leaves 100 µV of offset and $I_{OS} \cdot 9.09$ kΩ = 45 µV. It cannot remove the offset voltage, and it only helps when the two bias currents are nearly equal, which is true for bipolar input stages but not for many bias-compensated or CMOS parts.</p>

<p><strong>More terms.</strong> At $A_0 = 10^5$ and 0.1 V input, finite gain lowers the output by 121 µV. A 1 µV/°C drift over 50 °C adds 50 µV input (0.55 mV output). An 80 dB CMRR turns a 2 V common-mode change into 200 µV.</p>

</div>

That answers the opening question. A matching resistor in series with the plus input cancels the average bias current, but it adds its own thermal noise ($\sqrt{4kTR}$, 12 nV/√Hz for 9 kΩ) and, if the bias currents are unequal or of opposite sign, it can make the error worse. Use it with bipolar op amps that have specified offset current; leave it out for FET- and CMOS-input parts whose bias currents are picoamps.

<section id="budget">

<div className="section-heading"><span>04</span><h2>Build a worst-case budget</h2></div>

A budget lists every error, input-referred, at the worst case over the operating conditions, and compares the total with the requirement. Uncorrelated random errors are sometimes combined as a root sum of squares; a **guaranteed** bound adds magnitudes.

<div className="worked-example">

<span className="tiny-label">WORKED EXAMPLE / A THERMOCOUPLE AMPLIFIER</span><h3>Gain 100, ±0.5 °C, 0–40 °C ambient</h3>

<p><strong>Requirement.</strong> A type-K thermocouple produces about 41 µV/°C, so ±0.5 °C allows about ±20 µV of input-referred error. The signal itself is at most a few tens of millivolts, near ground, so CMRR matters only for interference.</p>

<p><strong>General-purpose op amp</strong> (1 mV offset, 10 µV/°C drift, 50 nA bias): offset alone is 1000 µV, fifty times the budget, and drift over 40 °C is 400 µV. Calibration could remove the offset but not the drift. It fails.</p>

<p><strong>Precision bipolar op amp</strong> (25 µV, 0.3 µV/°C, 1 nA, 120 dB CMRR): calibrate out the initial offset; then drift 12 µV, bias 0.5 µV, CMRR 1 µV, 0.05% gain resistors 5 µV at a 10 mV reading, noise 0.25 µV p-p. Total about <strong>19 µV</strong>: just inside, with calibration.</p>

<p><strong>Auto-zero (chopper) op amp</strong> (5 µV, 0.02 µV/°C): uncalibrated total about <strong>12 µV</strong>. The gain resistors are now the largest error.</p>

</div>

<ThermocoupleBudgetFigure/>

The budget did two jobs. It chose the op amp, and it showed what to improve next: in the chopper design, better resistors, not a better amplifier. It also revealed what was not in the budget yet: the thermocouple's own cold-junction compensation, which is usually the largest error of all.

</section>

<section id="null">

<div className="section-heading"><span>05</span><h2>Null what is repeatable</h2></div>

<OffsetNullCircuit/>

An initial offset is repeatable, so it can be trimmed out. Many op amps have null pins; when they do not, add a small adjustable voltage at the noninverting input. In the circuit above, the wiper's ±15 V is divided by 150 kΩ and 10 Ω to ±1 mV, enough to cancel any offset within ±1 mV. Because the noise gain amplifies the plus input, the correction acts exactly like an input voltage.

Nulling has costs. It holds at one temperature: an op amp nulled at 25 °C still drifts. For some bipolar op amps, nulling with the internal pins actually changes the drift; for an external network, the divider's own resistors and the rails' stability enter the budget. Calibrating in software, by measuring the offset and subtracting it, achieves the same thing without a trimmer, and can be repeated as the temperature changes.



<h3 id="book-notes">Null the assembled measurement, and keep calibration conditions</h3>

For an undistorted symmetric waveform, the midpoint between positive and negative output peaks estimates its DC displacement. Peaks at +121 mV and −79 mV have 200 mV peak-to-peak span but a **+21 mV** midpoint. An AC-coupled scope can hide that displacement; use a DC measurement when deciding whether to trim. With asymmetric distortion, the peak midpoint is not the time average.

Calibrate with the source configuration the instrument will actually use. Suppose noise gain is 100, input offset is +100 µV, and a bias-current drop adds +1 mV through a 10 kΩ source. The output error is 110 mV. Shorting the input to ground during trimming removes the source resistance and reveals only 10 mV. After the real source is reconnected, **100 mV remains** from the term the calibration never saw.

A zero-input calibration corrects an intercept at one temperature, supply and common-mode level. A second known input is needed to separate gain error from offset. Repeating those measurements at temperature reveals drift. Matching source resistances can cancel mean bias-current effects when the two currents track, but offset-current mismatch remains.

Simulation is useful only for the mechanisms included in its model. Asadi's offset-trim example points out that a symbol may display adjustment pins whose action is absent from the supplied model. Likewise, a macro-model with fixed offset cannot demonstrate drift or calibration repeatability. Check the model description before concluding that a failed simulated trim means the real circuit cannot be trimmed.

<section id="limits">

<div className="section-heading"><span>06</span><h2>Include the complete signal source</h2></div>

**Source resistance that varies.** A sensor's resistance often changes with its signal or temperature. Bias current through a varying resistance becomes a gain error or a nonlinearity, not just an offset.

**Leakage you did not specify.** A CMOS op amp may have 1 pA of bias current, but its input protection diodes, the circuit board and a fingerprint can leak nanoamps, doubling every 10 °C. Guard rings around high-impedance inputs, driven at the input's own potential, stop that leakage from reaching the input.

**Resistors are components too.** The gain is a ratio of resistors, so their tolerance is a gain error; their temperature coefficients and self-heating make it drift. A 0.1% resistor with 25 ppm/°C drifts 0.1% over 40 °C.

**Range.** After adding the worst-case errors, check again that the output stays in its swing range and the inputs in their common-mode range. Calibration removes errors at the calibration point only; it does not remove drift, noise, hysteresis or nonlinearity.

<div className="takeaway"><span className="tiny-label">THE IDEA TO KEEP</span><p>Put each error at its node, keep its sign, refer it to the input, and <em>multiply everything in the bracket by the noise gain</em>. A worst-case budget chooses the op amp and shows what to improve next.</p></div>

</section>

<section id="precision-depth">

<div className="section-heading"><span>07</span><h2>Design a precision instrument around a complete error budget</h2></div>

<h3>Precision, resolution and dynamic range have different budgets</h3>

A 10 mV full-scale meter accurate to 1% allows 100 µV absolute error. That is 1% of full scale but **10% of a 1 mV reading**. To promise 1% of reading at 1 mV, zero error must fall below 10 µV. ADC resolution only sets the step size; it does not remove analog offset, drift, gain error or thermal voltages. State separately the minimum signal, full scale, allowed zero error and allowed scale-factor error.

For a gain-1000 millivoltmeter, 200 µV input offset becomes 200 mV output offset. A 10 MΩ input return converts 10 pA into another 100 µV input error. A precision, low-bias part is needed even if the output meter movement is forgiving. Chapter 87 develops a low-voltage alternative that controls meter **current** directly and avoids depending on coil resistance.

<h3>An analog memory can subtract a large baseline</h3>

<NullMemoryFigure/>

Let a main amplifier produce $y=G(x-h)$, where $h$ is a stored baseline voltage. During NULL, a slow integrator adjusts $h$ according to $dh/dt=y/\tau$. For constant input, $dx/dt = 0$, so $d(x-h)/dt = -dh/dt = -G(x-h)/\tau$. That is a first-order decay of the error $x - h$ with time constant $\tau/G$; it falls to 1% when $e^{-Gt/\tau} = 0.01$:

<Equation tex={String.raw`\frac{d(x-h)}{dt}=-\frac{G}{\tau}(x-h),\qquad t_{1\%}=\frac{\tau}{G}\ln100`}/>

With $G=20$ and $\tau=2$ s, the error time constant is 100 ms and 1% nulling takes 461 ms. During HOLD the switch disconnects the correcting signal, and the output shows changes from the stored baseline. If $G$ changes to 200 while $\tau$ stays fixed, nulling becomes ten times faster and less tolerant of extra loop lag. Scale $\tau$ with gain to preserve the nulling bandwidth. The null loop must include the correct inversion; the integrator sign here is positive so a positive output increases the subtracted baseline.

This is a **change measurement**. Initial main-amplifier offset is absorbed into the stored null, while subsequent drift remains. The nulling amplifier's own offset can leave a nonzero residual. A digitally stored baseline and a DAC can serve the same function, but introduce reference, DAC linearity and quantization errors.

<h3>Follow every error to the final input</h3>

For gain stages $G_1,G_2,G_3$, an error added at stage 2's input is referred to the original input by dividing by $G_1$; an error added at stage 3's output is divided by $G_1G_2G_3$. Early gain reduces later error contributions, provided the first stage can tolerate the baseline and common-mode voltage.

| Contribution | Original design assumption | Input-referred bound |
|---|---|---|
| Front-end offset change | 0.15 µV/°C across 20 °C | 3 µV |
| Bias current through source imbalance | 25 pA and 20 kΩ | 0.5 µV |
| Common-mode change | 0.5 V, 110 dB CMRR | 1.58 µV |
| Supply change | 0.2 V, 120 dB PSRR | 0.2 µV |
| Resistor-ratio drift | 2 ppm/°C, 20 °C, 20 mV signal | 0.8 µV |
| Hold-node droop | 2 pA, 1 µF, 60 s; baseline attenuation 1/100 | 1.2 µV |

The deterministic bounds sum to **7.28 µV**. A separate 0.7 µV RMS noise estimate is not a guaranteed peak limit. Specify the required confidence or detection threshold before combining it with drift. Some terms may correlate; root-sum-square is appropriate only with justified statistical models, not merely because it yields a smaller answer.

<h3>Resistors, capacitors and the nulling switch</h3>

Gain accuracy depends on **ratio** tolerance and ratio tempco. Two individual resistors with 10 ppm/°C absolute coefficients can still differ by 20 ppm/°C in the worst case; a matched network may specify much better tracking. Initial calibration does not correct subsequent ratio drift, voltage coefficient, moisture or unequal self-heating. At 10 V across 10 kΩ, one resistor dissipates 10 mW; a neighboring lightly loaded resistor can be at a different temperature.

A hold capacitor has three distinct errors: leakage causes continuous droop, dielectric absorption recalls earlier voltages after reset, and switch charge causes a step. For 1 µF, 2 pA gives 2 µV/s; 1 pC injected charge gives 1 µV instantly. A 100 GΩ leakage resistance at 2 V adds 20 pA, ten times the assumed amplifier current. Increasing capacitance reduces current-induced droop but increases acquisition time and may increase total leakage. A leakage-cancellation current proportional to held voltage can correct a simple leakage resistance at one temperature; it cannot erase dielectric absorption and must not overcompensate into a growing hold voltage.

<h3>Input errors that a one-line specification hides</h3>

Input impedance includes both a DC conductance and capacitance. Bias-current cancellation can make average current small without making its noise small; sign and common-mode dependence matter. Offset bounds may apply only at a particular supply and common-mode voltage. Use the actual CMRR and PSRR at the disturbance frequency: 80 dB rejects a 100 mV ripple to 10 µV input equivalent, whereas 120 dB at DC would predict only 0.1 µV. The positive and negative supply rejection can differ.

Thermal EMFs at dissimilar-metal junctions can exceed the amplifier's offset. Minimize temperature gradients, use matched connections, keep power devices away from input junctions, and wait after handling a sensitive board. Protection devices, switches, connectors and board contamination belong in the current budget alongside the amplifier.

If a required parameter is unspecified, record the gap and characterize candidate parts under the intended voltage, temperature and humidity. A favorable typical graph supports a prototype hypothesis; it is not a production guarantee. Distinguish guaranteed design, screened parts and measured one-off performance in the resource's worked examples.

<summary>Worked self-check: what remains after a perfect zero adjustment?</summary>
<div>

A 50 µV initial offset can be nulled at 25 °C, but a 0.2 µV/°C drift gives 4 µV after a 20 °C change. A hold node with 5 pA into 2 µF drifts by 150 µV in 60 s; if its effect at the sensor input is attenuated 100:1, that contributes 1.5 µV. The nulled instrument therefore still has at least a 5.5 µV worst-case drift budget from these two terms.

<section id="check">

<div className="section-heading"><span>08</span><h2>Check your understanding</h2></div>

<details>
<summary>1. A gain-of-25 amplifier has 80 µV of offset. What is its output offset?</summary>
<div>

$25 × 80$ µV = **2 mV**, before any other errors. Offset is multiplied by the noise gain, the same as a signal at the plus input.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>2. With $R_F$ = 90 kΩ and $R_G$ = 10 kΩ, what source resistance cancels equal bias currents? With 4 nA of offset current, what remains at the output?</summary>
<div>

$R_F \parallel R_G$ = **9 kΩ**. The residual is $NG × I_{OS} × 9\text{ kΩ}$ = 10 × 4 nA × 9 kΩ = **360 µV** at the output.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>3. Offset drift is 0.5 µV/°C over 80 °C at a noise gain of 50. What is the output drift?</summary>
<div>

Input change: 0.5 × 80 = 40 µV. Output: 50 × 40 µV = **2 mV**. Initial offset and other drifts come on top of this.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>4. When does a bias-cancelling resistor make the error worse?</summary>
<div>

When the two bias currents are not nearly equal: for bias-compensated bipolar parts they can have opposite signs, and for CMOS parts they are tiny and unrelated. The resistor then adds an $I_{B+}R$ term without cancelling anything, plus its own thermal noise.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>5. Why does nulling the offset at room temperature not solve the thermocouple problem with a general-purpose op amp?</summary>
<div>

Its drift, 10 µV/°C over a 40 °C range, is 400 µV, twenty times the whole budget. Nulling removes the initial offset at one temperature; the drift is a change from that point and remains.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>6. Should you add worst-case errors or combine them as a root sum of squares?</summary>
<div>

For a guaranteed specification, add magnitudes: the worst case of each can occur together. For a statistical estimate of what most units will do, combine independent random errors as a root sum of squares. State which one a budget uses.

</div>
</details>
