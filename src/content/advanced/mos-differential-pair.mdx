<h1>The MOS differential pair<br/><em>Two transistors share one current.</em></h1>

<p className="dek">Split the inputs into differential and common-mode parts, derive the exact square-law steering curve before linearizing it, analyze the pair with half-circuits, and see why its linearity, its offset and its transconductance all trade against one number: the overdrive.</p>

</div>

<div className="opening-grid">

<div><span className="tiny-label">PREDICT BEFORE CALCULATING</span><p className="opening-question">In a differential pair with an ideal tail, one drain current rises by 100 µA. How much does the other fall?</p></div>

<div>

The differential pair is the input stage of nearly every op amp, comparator and analog multiplier. Two matched transistors have their sources joined and fed by a single tail current. Whatever the inputs do, the two drain currents must add up to that tail current; the inputs only decide how it is shared.

That one constraint gives the pair its two great properties. It responds to the **difference** between its inputs and ignores what they have in common. And because the shared current is conserved exactly, its transfer curve is symmetric, so even-order distortion cancels. Chapter 36 built the bipolar version. The MOS pair has the same structure, a different steering law, and a linear range that the designer can choose.

</div>

</div>

<span className="tiny-label">THE PATH THROUGH THIS CHAPTER</span><p>Separate differential and common-mode coordinates → Solve the steering law before linearizing → Half-circuits → Watch currents move together → Match the pair and its surroundings → Check your understanding.</p>

<section id="model">

<div className="section-heading"><span>01</span><h2>Separate differential and common-mode coordinates</h2></div>

<MosDifferentialFigure mode="resistor"/>

Any two input voltages can be rewritten as a difference and an average:

<Equation tex={String.raw`v_d=V_1-V_2,\qquad V_C=\frac{V_1+V_2}{2},\qquad V_1=V_C+\frac{v_d}{2},\quad V_2=V_C-\frac{v_d}{2}`}/>

With matched devices and an ideal tail, $I_1 + I_2 = I_T$ always. That answers the opening question: if one current rises by 100 µA, the other falls by exactly 100 µA. At $v_d$ = 0 each carries $I_T/2$. Changing $V_C$ moves the shared source node up and down with it, leaving the currents unchanged, until some transistor or the tail runs out of headroom.

At balance each device has overdrive $u_0 = \sqrt{I_T/k}$ and transconductance $g_{m0} = ku_0 = \sqrt{kI_T} = I_T/u_0$, using this book's convention $I_D = \tfrac12ku^2$ for each device. The common source sits at $V_S = V_C - V_T - u_0$.

</section>

<section id="derive">

<div className="section-heading"><span>02</span><h2>Solve the steering law before linearizing</h2></div>

Write the two branch overdrives as $u \pm v_d/2$, where $u$ is their average (it shifts as the source node moves). Adding the two square laws:

<Equation tex={String.raw`I_T=\tfrac12k\left[(u+\tfrac{v_d}{2})^2+(u-\tfrac{v_d}{2})^2\right]=k\left(u^2+\frac{v_d^2}{4}\right)\;\Rightarrow\;u=\sqrt{\frac{I_T}{k}-\frac{v_d^2}{4}}`}/>

Subtracting them gives the difference current, $I_1 - I_2 = kuv_d$, so

<Equation tex={String.raw`I_1-I_2=k\,v_d\sqrt{\frac{I_T}{k}-\frac{v_d^2}{4}}=g_{m0}\,v_d\sqrt{1-\frac{v_d^2}{4u_0^2}},\qquad |v_d|\le\sqrt2\,u_0`} label="EXACT SQUARE-LAW STEERING"/>

Beyond $|v_d| = \sqrt2\,u_0$ one transistor carries the whole tail and the other is off. Near balance the square root is 1, and each drain current changes by $\pm g_{m0}v_d/2$, half the input on each side. With equal drain resistors there are three possible outputs, and three gains:

<Equation tex={String.raw`\frac{v_{o2}}{v_d}=+\frac{g_{m0}R_D}{2},\qquad\frac{v_{o1}}{v_d}=-\frac{g_{m0}R_D}{2},\qquad\frac{v_{o2}-v_{o1}}{v_d}=g_{m0}R_D`}/>

State which output you mean before comparing gains: the differential output has twice the gain of either drain.

<MosBjtSteeringFigure/>

### Linear range is a design choice

A bipolar pair steers completely within about ±4$V_T$ ≈ ±100 mV, fixed by physics. A MOS pair steers completely at $\sqrt2\,u_0$, and the designer chooses $u_0$. The cost is transconductance: at fixed tail current, $g_{m0} = I_T/u_0$, so doubling the linear range halves $g_m$. Even a MOS pair at only 0.2 V of overdrive has nearly three times the bipolar pair's linear range, and about a quarter of its $g_m$ ($2V_T/u_0$ = 0.26).

**No even harmonics.** The steering curve is odd: $I_1 - I_T/2$ changes sign with $v_d$. So even with a single-ended output, a matched pair with an ideal tail produces no second harmonic. The first distortion term is cubic. To size it, write the steering law with $I_T = ku_0^2$ (each side carries $\tfrac12ku_0^2$ at balance): $I_1 - I_2 = ku_0v_d\sqrt{1 - v_d^2/4u_0^2}$. For small $v_d$, $\sqrt{1-x} \approx 1 - x/2$, so

<Equation tex={String.raw`I_1-I_2\approx ku_0v_d-\frac{k\,v_d^3}{8u_0}`}/>

Put $v_d = a\cos\theta$ and use $\cos^3\theta = \tfrac34\cos\theta + \tfrac14\cos3\theta$. The third-harmonic amplitude is $\tfrac14\cdot ka^3/(8u_0) = ka^3/(32u_0)$, while the fundamental is $ku_0a$ to first order. Their ratio, for a sine of peak $a$, is

<Equation tex={String.raw`\frac{H_3}{H_1}\approx\frac{a^2}{32\,u_0^2}`}/>

At $a$ = 0.2 V and $u_0$ = 1 V that is 0.13%. A single common-source transistor driven with the same 0.1 V on its gate would have 2.5% second harmonic.

</section>

<section id="halves">

<div className="section-heading"><span>03</span><h2>Half-circuits</h2></div>

Symmetry makes small-signal analysis of the pair easy. Decompose any input into its differential and common-mode parts, analyze each separately, and add the results.

<HalfCircuitFigure/>

**Differential signals.** With $+v_d/2$ on one gate and $-v_d/2$ on the other, one current rises exactly as much as the other falls, so the tail node does not move. For differential signals the tail is AC ground, whatever is connected to it. Each half is a common-source stage with a grounded source, and $v_{o1} = -g_{m0}R_D \cdot v_d/2$.

**Common-mode signals.** With $v_c$ on both gates, both currents change together, and their sum flows through the tail's incremental resistance $R_T$. Splitting the tail into two parallel resistors of $2R_T$, one per half, keeps each half identical. Each half is then a degenerated stage with gain

<Equation tex={String.raw`A_{cm}=\frac{v_{o1}}{v_c}=-\frac{g_{m0}R_D}{1+2g_{m0}R_T}\approx-\frac{R_D}{2R_T}`}/>

An ideal tail ($R_T \to \infty$) rejects common mode completely. A real tail, a transistor current source with output resistance $r_o$ or a cascode with $g_mr_o^2$, makes $A_{cm}$ small but not zero. Chapter 66 develops this into the common-mode rejection ratio.

</section>

<div className="section-heading"><span>04</span><h2>Watch currents move together</h2></div>

Use $I_T$ = 2 mA, $k$ = 2 mA/V², $V_T$ = 1 V, $R_D$ = 1 kΩ, a 5 V supply and $V_C$ = 2.5 V:

<MosCases kind="differential"/>

<div className="worked-example">

<span className="tiny-label">WORKED EXAMPLE / BALANCE AND A LARGE INPUT</span><h3>$I_T$ = 2 mA, $k$ = 2 mA/V², $R_D$ = 1 kΩ</h3>

<p><strong>Balance.</strong> $u_0 = \sqrt{2/2}$ = 1 V, so $V_S$ = 2.5 − 1 − 1 = 0.5 V. Each drain carries 1 mA and sits at 4 V; each $g_m$ is 2 mS. The drain-to-drain gain is $g_{m0}R_D$ = +2; each single drain has ±1.</p>

<p><strong>Large input, $v_d$ = +0.8 V.</strong> The exact steering law gives $I_1 - I_2 = 2 × 0.8\sqrt{1 - 0.16}$ = 1.466 mA, so the branches carry 1.733 and 0.267 mA. Their sum is still 2 mA. The drain difference is 1.466 V, 8% below the linear prediction of 1.6 V, because the input is now a large fraction of the 1.41 V steering limit.</p>

<p><strong>Headroom.</strong> At $v_d$ = 0.8 V, $M_1$'s drain falls to 5 − 1.733 = 3.27 V. Its gate is at 2.9 V, so it stays saturated while $V_D \ge V_G - V_T$ = 1.9 V: a margin of 1.37 V. Raise $V_C$ to 4 V and the same input puts $M_1$'s gate at 4.4 V: the drain would need to stay above 3.4 V, and 3.27 V is not enough. The ideal steering law has not changed, but $M_1$ is now in triode.</p>

</div>

<section id="limits">

<div className="section-heading"><span>05</span><h2>Match the pair and its surroundings</h2></div>

A real pair is not perfectly matched, and the imbalance appears as **input offset**: the differential input needed to make the two outputs equal.

<div className="derivation">

<span className="tiny-label">DERIVATION / OFFSET OF A RESISTOR-LOADED PAIR</span>

1. **Balance condition.** The drains are equal when $I_1R_{D1} = I_2R_{D2}$. Write $R_{D1,2} = R_D(1 \pm \epsilon_R/2)$ with $\epsilon_R = \Delta R_D/R_D$, and $I_{1,2} = I_T/2 \pm \delta$. To first order the balance requires $\delta = -\epsilon_R\,I_T/4$: the side with the larger resistor must carry slightly less current.

2. **What produces $\delta$.** A differential input $v$ steers $\pm g_{m0}v/2$ (section 02). A threshold difference $\Delta V_T$ acts exactly like an input voltage in series with one gate. A fractional $k$ difference $\epsilon_k$ at equal overdrive splits the tail as $I_{1,2} = (I_T/2)(1 \pm \epsilon_k/2)$, a current shift of $\epsilon_kI_T/4$.

3. **Solve for the input that balances.** The steered current must cancel the $k$ shift and supply the resistor requirement:
<Equation tex={String.raw`\frac{g_{m0}}{2}\,(v-\Delta V_T)=-\frac{I_T}{4}\left(\epsilon_R+\epsilon_k\right)`}/>

4. **Refer to the input.** Divide by $g_{m0}/2$ and use $g_{m0} = I_T/u_0$, so $I_T/(2g_{m0}) = u_0/2$. The magnitude of the required input is the offset, with each term able to take either sign in practice:

</div>

Three sources dominate:

<Equation tex={String.raw`V_{OS}\approx\Delta V_T+\frac{u_0}{2}\left(\frac{\Delta R_D}{R_D}+\frac{\Delta k}{k}\right)`} label="INPUT OFFSET OF A RESISTOR-LOADED PAIR"/>

The threshold term enters directly. The resistor and $k$ terms are percentage errors in current or gain, referred to the input through $I_T/(2g_{m0}) = u_0/2$. Here again the overdrive is a lever: large overdrive gives a wide linear range and small threshold sensitivity relative to the signal, but makes the percentage terms larger in millivolts.

<DiffOffsetFigure/>

The tail matters as well. Its finite output resistance creates common-mode gain at each drain; perfect symmetry cancels it in the drain difference, but mismatch converts it into a differential error. And the tail's current noise moves both drains together, which a differential output rejects and a single-ended output does not.

<div className="takeaway"><span className="tiny-label">THE IDEA TO KEEP</span><p>A pair conserves its tail current, so it amplifies differences, cancels even harmonics, and rejects common mode through the tail's resistance. Its overdrive $u_0$ sets <em>the linear range ($\sqrt2\,u_0$), the transconductance ($I_T/u_0$) and the offset sensitivity</em> all at once.</p></div>

</section>

<section id="check">

<div className="section-heading"><span>06</span><h2>Check your understanding</h2></div>

<details>
<summary>1. One drain current rises by 100 µA. How much does the other fall?</summary>
<div>

Exactly 100 µA, as long as the tail is ideal and both transistors are on. The tail current is conserved, so the two currents can only redistribute. With a finite-resistance tail, a common-mode input can change both together, so the statement holds only for differential inputs.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>2. Why is the tail node an AC ground for differential signals?</summary>
<div>

With symmetric $\pm v_d/2$ inputs, the two currents change by equal and opposite amounts, so the total current into the tail node does not change. With no change of current, its voltage does not change either, whatever impedance the tail presents. Each half is then a grounded-source stage.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>3. What is the steering limit of a pair with $I_T$ = 2 mA and $k$ = 2 mA/V²? With $k$ = 50 mA/V²?</summary>
<div>

$u_0 = \sqrt{I_T/k}$ = 1 V, so full steering at $\sqrt2$ V ≈ 1.41 V. With $k$ = 50 mA/V², $u_0$ = 0.2 V and full steering at 0.28 V. The wider device has 5 times the $g_m$ and a fifth of the linear range.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>4. Why does a matched pair produce no second harmonic, even from a single drain?</summary>
<div>

$I_1 - I_T/2 = (I_1 - I_2)/2$, and $I_1 - I_2$ is an odd function of $v_d$. An odd function of a sine contains only odd harmonics. The square-law nonlinearity of each device is still there, but the two devices' second-order terms cancel through the shared tail.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>5. At $u_0$ = 1 V, a 1% drain-resistor mismatch causes how much input offset? At $u_0$ = 0.2 V?</summary>
<div>

$(u_0/2)(\Delta R/R)$ = 0.5 V × 0.01 = **5 mV** at 1 V of overdrive, and **1 mV** at 0.2 V. A smaller overdrive makes the pair more sensitive (more $g_m$ per unit of tail current), which shrinks every input-referred current error.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>6. Raising the common-mode input to 4 V broke a pair that worked at 2.5 V. Which device failed, and why did the steering curve not warn you?</summary>
<div>

The input device whose current rose: its gate went up with $V_C$ while its drain fell with its larger current, so $V_{DS}$ fell below its overdrive and it entered triode. The ideal steering law assumes both devices saturated and an ideal tail; it says nothing about compliance, which must be checked separately (Chapter 66).

</div>
</details>
