<div className="opening-grid">

<div><span className="tiny-label">PREDICT BEFORE CALCULATING</span><p className="opening-question">A PMOS mirror sources 2 mA into a load. As the load voltage rises toward the positive rail, does the current stay constant?</p></div>

<div>

The mirror of Chapter 54 sinks current to ground. Real circuits need currents in both directions and of many sizes: a 20 µA tail for an input pair, 200 µA for a second stage, 2 mA for an output stage. Making a separate reference for each would waste power and matching.

Instead, one reference feeds a **bias tree**. NMOS mirrors produce sinks; a PMOS mirror turned upside down produces sources; and changing the width of the output transistor scales the copy. This chapter builds that tree, then follows the errors that it accumulates along the way.

</div>

</div>

<span className="tiny-label">THE PATH THROUGH THIS CHAPTER</span><p>Change polarity and reference direction → Scale current, then solve the supply constraints → Compare available PMOS drain headroom → Make ratios accurate with unit devices → Build and budget a bias tree → Check your understanding.</p>

</div>

<section id="model">

<div className="section-heading"><span>01</span><h2>Change polarity and reference direction</h2></div>

<MosMirrorFigure mode="ratio"/>

A PMOS mirror is the NMOS mirror reflected about a horizontal line: sources and bodies at $V_{DD}$, the reference current **pulled out** of the diode-connected device by a sink below it, and the output current **pushed into** a load connected to ground.

Signs are the usual source of confusion. Rather than juggle negative $V_{GS}$, $V_{DS}$ and $V_T$, use positive magnitudes throughout: $V_{SG}$, $V_{SD}$, $|V_{TP}|$ and $|I_D|$. Then the PMOS law has exactly the NMOS form:

<Equation tex={String.raw`|I_D|=\tfrac12k_p\,(V_{SG}-|V_{TP}|)^2(1+\lambda V_{SD})\quad\text{for }V_{SD}\ge V_{SG}-|V_{TP}|`}/>

Holes have roughly a third of the electron mobility, so for equal $k$ a PMOS device must be two to three times wider than its NMOS counterpart. In this chapter's examples both types use $k$ = 2 mA/V² per unit device and $|V_T|$ = 1 V.

</section>

<section id="derive">

<div className="section-heading"><span>02</span><h2>Scale current, then solve the supply constraints</h2></div>

Give the output device $m$ times the reference device's width at the same length, so $k_2 = mk_1$. At the shared gate voltage, the saturation law gives $m$ times the current:

<Equation tex={String.raw`I_{OUT}=m\,I_{REF}\quad(\lambda=0),\qquad V_G=V_{DD}-|V_{TP}|-\sqrt{2I_{REF}/k_p}`}/>

Both $m > 1$ (amplify) and $m < 1$ (attenuate) work. A 0.5 mA reference can bias a 0.25 mA branch with $m$ = 0.5 and a 1 mA branch with $m$ = 2.

The headroom condition is also reflected. The output device needs $V_{SD} \ge u_p$, and its source is at the rail, so the output node must stay **below** the rail by at least one overdrive:

<Equation tex={String.raw`V_{OUT}\le V_{DD}-u_p`} label="PMOS COMPLIANCE"/>

<div className="worked-example">

<span className="tiny-label">WORKED EXAMPLE / A RESISTOR-FED PMOS REFERENCE</span><h3>0.5 mA from 5 V</h3>

<p>Put $R_{REF}$ from the diode-connected PMOS drain to ground. The device needs $u = \sqrt{2 × 0.5/2}$ = 0.707 V, so $V_{SG}$ = 1.707 V and its drain (and gate) sit at 5 − 1.707 = 3.293 V. The resistor therefore carries 0.5 mA with 3.293 V across it: $R_{REF}$ = <strong>6.59 kΩ</strong>.</p>

<p>Do not assume the whole supply appears across the resistor; that would give 10 kΩ, which actually passes only about 0.34 mA. And remember Chapter 54: this reference still moves with the supply, here by 1/(6.59 kΩ + 0.71 kΩ) ≈ 0.14 mA/V, or 27%/V.</p>

</div>

</section>

<section id="cases">

<div className="section-heading"><span>03</span><h2>Compare available PMOS drain headroom</h2></div>

<PmosComplianceFigure/>

Now the opening question is answered: the current is roughly constant, rising slightly because of $\lambda$, **until** the output rises to within one overdrive of the rail. With 1 V of overdrive, a 5 V sourcing mirror is a current source only up to 4 V. Close to the rail, where a load might look "easy", it fails completely.

The table shows the same mirror (reference 1 mA, ratio 2, $\lambda$ = 0.02 V⁻¹) at three source–drain voltages. With a 5 V rail, $V_{SD}$ = 0.2, 2 and 4 V correspond to output node voltages of 4.8, 3 and 1 V:

<MosCases kind="ratio"/>

At $V_{SD}$ = 0.2 V the output device is in triode and delivers 0.71 mA instead of 2 mA. At 2 V it is almost exact, because 2 V is close to the reference device's own $V_{SD}$. At 4 V it is 4% high. These are the same three effects as in the NMOS mirror; only the direction of the headroom has changed.

<div className="margin-note">

**Ratios cost capacitance.** A 1 : 10 mirror hangs ten gate capacitances on the reference node, which has only the reference device's $g_m$ to drive them. The node's time constant is about $(1 + m)C_{gs}/g_{m1}$. Usually that is nanoseconds and harmless. But a fast transient on the large output device's drain couples through its gate–drain capacitance into the shared gate, and a high-ratio bias line can ring or "kick" every branch it feeds. Bypass the gate line when you can.

</div>

</section>

<section id="units">

<div className="section-heading"><span>04</span><h2>Make ratios accurate with unit devices</h2></div>

The ratio $k_2/k_1$ is what the circuit uses, but the designer draws widths and lengths. They are not the same thing. The effective width of a channel is smaller than its drawn width by an edge loss $\Delta W$, and the effective length differs from the drawn length by $\Delta L$. With $k \propto W_{eff}/L_{eff}$ and equal lengths, the ratio of the two $k$ values is the ratio of effective widths. Drawing one device four times as wide as another does not make its $k$ four times larger:

<Equation tex={String.raw`\frac{k_2}{k_1}=\frac{4W-\Delta W}{W-\Delta W}\ne 4`}/>

<UnitRatioFigure/>

The cure is to build every device from identical **unit cells**. A 4× device is four unit transistors in parallel; it has four times every edge, so the edge losses scale with the current and cancel in the ratio. Non-integer ratios are made from integers: 1 : 2.5 becomes 2 units : 5 units. Keep the length the same in every branch, because $\Delta L$, $\lambda$ and $V_T$ all depend on length.

<div className="worked-example">

<span className="tiny-label">WORKED EXAMPLE / HOW BIG IS THE SYSTEMATIC ERROR?</span><h3>A 1 : 4 mirror with 1 µm units and $\Delta W$ = 0.1 µm</h3>

<p>One device drawn 4 µm wide against one drawn 1 µm wide: $k_2/k_1$ = 3.9/0.9 = 4.33, an <strong>8.3%</strong> ratio error, larger than any random mismatch you would expect. Four separate 1 µm units: 3.6/0.9 = <strong>4.00</strong> exactly.</p>

<p>Random mismatch still remains, and it is set mostly by the smaller side. If one unit has 1σ current mismatch $\sigma_u$, a 4-unit device averages four of them and has $\sigma_u/2$. The ratio's mismatch is $\sqrt{\sigma_u^2 + (\sigma_u/2)^2}$ = 1.12 $\sigma_u$. To improve it, make the reference side larger too.</p>

</div>

None of this applies directly to discrete parts. Two MOSFETs of different part numbers, or even of the same part, do not have $k$ in the ratio of their current ratings. With discrete transistors, set ratios with source resistors (Chapter 56) and let the resistors, not the devices, define the currents.

</section>

<section id="tree">

<div className="section-heading"><span>05</span><h2>Build and budget a bias tree</h2></div>

<BiasTreeCircuit/>

A complete bias network combines both polarities. The NMOS reference generates a gate line that any number of NMOS sinks can share. To make a source, one NMOS sink pulls current out of a diode-connected PMOS, which generates a PMOS gate line for any number of PMOS sources. The circuit above produces 400 µA of sink and 200 µA of source from one 100 µA reference.

<div className="worked-example">

<span className="tiny-label">WORKED EXAMPLE / BUDGET THE TREE</span><h3>Current, headroom and accumulated error</h3>

<p><strong>Reference.</strong> At 100 µA each unit needs $u = \sqrt{2 × 0.1/2}$ = 0.316 V, so $V_{GS}$ = 1.316 V and $R_{REF}$ = (5 − 1.316)/0.1 mA = 36.8 kΩ.</p>

<p><strong>Supply current.</strong> 100 µA (reference) + 400 µA ($M_5$) + 100 µA ($M_2$/$M_3$) + 200 µA ($M_4$) = 800 µA, of which 600 µA reaches loads. The two "overhead" branches cost 25%; using a 1 : 10 ratio from a 20 µA reference would cut the overhead to a few percent, at the cost of matching.</p>

<p><strong>Headroom.</strong> $M_5$'s load must leave its drain above 0.32 V. $M_4$'s load must keep its drain below 5 − 0.32 = 4.68 V. Both are generous because the overdrive is small.</p>

<p><strong>Systematic error.</strong> With $\lambda$ = 0.02 V⁻¹, $M_2$'s drain sits at $V_{DD} - V_{SG3}$ = 3.68 V while $M_1$'s sits at 1.32 V: the copy is $(1.0737/1.0263)$ = 4.6% high. If $M_4$'s output is at 2 V, its $V_{SD}$ = 3 V against $M_3$'s 1.32 V, adding another 3.3%. The 200 µA source actually delivers about <strong>216 µA</strong>, 8% high, with two perfectly matched mirrors in the chain. Each mirror in a chain multiplies in its own drain-voltage error.</p>

<p><strong>Random error.</strong> At $u$ = 0.32 V even 3 mV of threshold mismatch gives 2% per mirror; two mirrors in series give about 2.8%. Choose the overdrive with the chain length in mind.</p>

</div>

The remedies are the subject of the next chapters: source resistors to make currents depend on resistor ratios and to reduce threshold sensitivity (Chapter 56), and cascodes that hold each output drain at the same voltage as its reference (Chapters 57–58).

### Ratioed source resistors

When degeneration resistors are added to a ratioed mirror, their drops must also be ratioed. To keep the same $V_{GS}$ in both branches at their intended currents, make the source resistors inversely proportional to the currents: $R_{S2}/R_{S1} = I_{REF}/I_{OUT}$. A 1 : 4 mirror with 1 kΩ under its 0.25 mA reference needs 250 Ω under its 1 mA output, so both drops are 0.25 V.

### Start-up

This tree has an externally imposed reference resistor, so its only operating point is the intended one. Self-biased trees, in which the reference current is itself derived from a mirror output, also have a solution with every current at zero. They need a start-up circuit that injects a little current at power-up and then gets out of the way.

<div className="takeaway"><span className="tiny-label">THE IDEA TO KEEP</span><p>Scale currents with <em>counted unit devices</em>, turn sinks into sources with a diode-connected device of the other polarity, and remember that every mirror in a chain adds its own drain-voltage and mismatch errors.</p></div>

</section>

<section id="check">

<div className="section-heading"><span>06</span><h2>Check your understanding</h2></div>

<details>
<summary>1. A 5 V PMOS mirror runs at 0.4 V overdrive. What is the highest output node voltage at which it still sources its nominal current?</summary>
<div>

It needs $V_{SD} \ge u$ = 0.4 V, and its source is at 5 V, so the output must stay at or below **4.6 V**. Above that the output device enters triode and the current collapses toward zero as the output reaches the rail.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>2. Does drawing an output transistor twice as wide always give twice the current?</summary>
<div>

No. The effective width is reduced by an edge loss, so a single wide device gives a ratio greater than 2 (for the usual sign of $\Delta W$). Two parallel copies of the unit device give exactly 2 apart from random mismatch. Even then the ratio holds only with both devices in saturation, at equal drain voltages and equal temperature.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>3. A 100 µA reference feeds mirrors with ratios 1, 2 and 5. What is the total supply current?</summary>
<div>

The outputs carry 100 + 200 + 500 = 800 µA; adding the 100 µA reference branch gives **900 µA**. Large ratios from a small reference reduce the overhead fraction, but increase gate loading on the reference node and make the reference device's mismatch more important.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>4. Why does a two-mirror chain in the example deliver 8% too much current even with perfectly matched devices?</summary>
<div>

Each mirror's output device sits at a much larger drain–source voltage than its diode-connected reference device. With $\lambda$ = 0.02 V⁻¹, each extra volt adds about 2%. The NMOS mirror's output is at 3.68 V against 1.32 V, the PMOS mirror's at 3 V against 1.32 V: the two errors, 4.6% and 3.3%, multiply.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>5. In a degenerated 1 : 4 mirror, the reference branch has $R_S$ = 1 kΩ at 0.25 mA. What output source resistor keeps the ratio, and why?</summary>
<div>

**250 Ω.** Equal $V_{GS}$ in both branches requires equal source voltages at the intended currents: 0.25 mA × 1 kΩ = 1 mA × 250 Ω = 0.25 V. Source resistors must scale inversely with current, just as widths scale directly.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>6. Why turn an NMOS sink into a PMOS source with a diode-connected PMOS, rather than just using a resistor to the rail?</summary>
<div>

A resistor's current depends on its voltage, so it is a poor current source. The diode-connected PMOS converts the sink current into a PMOS gate voltage that any number of PMOS devices can share. Each copy is then a high-impedance source with its own compliance, scaled by its own unit count.

</div>
</details>
