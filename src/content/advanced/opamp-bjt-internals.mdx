<div className="opening-grid">

<div><span className="tiny-label">PREDICT BEFORE CALCULATING</span><p className="opening-question">Bipolar input stages give the most transconductance per microamp. Why, then, do classic bipolar op amps slew so slowly?</p></div>

<div>

The classic general-purpose op amps of the 1970s were all-bipolar, and many precision parts still are. Their architecture is Chapter 75's two-stage amplifier with bipolar transistors in place of MOSFETs: a differential pair with a mirror load, a common-emitter gain stage with a current-source load, a Miller capacitor, and a push–pull output.

Swapping the transistor type changes the specifications in predictable ways. Bipolar transistors have more $g_m$ per microamp, so lower voltage noise and higher gain; they draw base current, so bias current and current noise; and their steep exponential law makes the input stage saturate after a few tens of millivolts, which, as the opening question hints, sets the slew rate.

</div>

</div>

<section id="model">

<div className="section-heading"><span>01</span><h2>Follow an original simplified construction</h2></div>

<InternalAmplifierFigure technology="bjt"/>

This is a teaching architecture, not a replica of a commercial part. An NPN differential pair steers the tail current. A PNP mirror combines the two branch changes at $V_X$ (Chapter 72). An NPN common-emitter stage with a PNP current-source load provides the second stage's gain. The Miller capacitor $C_C$ bridges that stage. Ideal tail and bias generators stand for circuits omitted here.

As in Chapter 75, follow the signal to find the polarity: the left input, on the mirror's diode side, raises $V_X$, and the common-emitter stage inverts it, so the left input is inverting. A complementary emitter-follower output (section 05) buffers the gain stage from the load. Real designs vary this with PNP inputs, folded cascodes, level shifters and Darlington gain stages, but the signal path is recognizably the same.

</section>

<section id="derive">

<div className="section-heading"><span>02</span><h2>Connect exponential physics to specifications</h2></div>

<Equation tex={String.raw`g_m=\frac{I_C}{U_T},\qquad r_\pi=\frac{\beta}{g_m},\qquad I_B=\frac{I_C}{\beta},\qquad U_T=\frac{k_BT}{q}\approx25.85\text{ mV at 300 K}`}/>

Each line maps to a datasheet entry. $g_m = I_C/U_T$ is the highest transconductance per unit current of any transistor: about 39 mS per milliamp, against roughly 10 mS per milliamp for a MOSFET at 0.2 V overdrive (Chapter 76). That gives a bipolar input stage high gain and low **voltage noise** at modest current. $I_B = I_C/\beta$ is the **input bias current**: tens to hundreds of nanoamps for a simple pair. And because $r_\pi$ is finite, the **input resistance** is finite too, megohms rather than teraohms.

The pair's steering law is exponential, not square law (Chapter 36):

<Equation tex={String.raw`I_1-I_2=I_T\tanh\frac{v_d}{2U_T}`}/>

<MosBjtSteeringFigure/>

Its differential transconductance at balance is $I_T/2U_T$, but it saturates quickly: expanding $\tanh x \approx x - x^3/3$, the gain is compressed by 1% at $v_d$ ≈ 9 mV, and the pair is fully steered by about ±100 mV.

</section>

<section id="cases">

<div className="section-heading"><span>03</span><h2>Compare three input bias currents</h2></div>

<div className="worked-example">

<span className="tiny-label">WORKED EXAMPLE / BIAS CURRENT, GAIN AND THE SOURCE</span><h3>Branch currents 10, 50 and 100 µA, $\beta$ = 200</h3>

<p><strong>Transconductance:</strong> 0.39, 1.93 and 3.87 mS. <strong>Base currents:</strong> 50, 250 and 500 nA.</p>

<p><strong>Through a 100 kΩ source</strong> those base currents drop 5, 25 and 50 mV before any cancellation (Chapter 78). The same stage that gives excellent voltage noise with a 100 Ω microphone is a poor choice for a 100 kΩ sensor.</p>

<p><strong>Gain.</strong> With a 100 µA tail, the stage's transconductance is about 1.93 mS. Into a 50 kΩ first-stage output resistance that is a gain of 97. A second stage of gain 100 brings the total to about 9700 before loading.</p>

</div>

Bipolar precision parts reduce bias current in two ways. **Super-beta** transistors, with very thin bases, reach $\beta$ of several thousand. **Bias-current cancellation** injects a matched current into each input to supply most of the base current. Both cut the DC current, but cancellation leaves the base current's shot noise, so the input **current noise** remains, and the residual bias currents can have either sign, which defeats the resistor-matching trick of Chapter 78.

</section>

<section id="slew">

<div className="section-heading"><span>04</span><h2>Why bipolar op amps slew slowly</h2></div>

From Chapter 75, the unity-gain frequency is $f_u = g_{m1}/2\pi C_C$, and the slew rate is $SR = I_T/C_C$. Divide one by the other and $C_C$ cancels:

<Equation tex={String.raw`SR=\frac{I_T}{g_{m1}}\cdot2\pi f_u`} label="SLEW RATE PER UNIT BANDWIDTH"/>

For an undegenerated bipolar pair, $g_{m1} = I_T/2U_T$, so $I_T/g_{m1} = 2U_T$ ≈ 52 mV, and

<Equation tex={String.raw`SR\approx2U_T\cdot2\pi f_u\approx0.33\ \text{V/µs per MHz}`}/>

That answers the opening question. For a given bandwidth, the bipolar pair's high transconductance per microamp means it needs only a little tail current, and that small current then charges $C_C$ slowly. The ratio of slew rate to bandwidth is fixed by $U_T$, a physical constant. A 1 MHz bipolar op amp slews at about a third of a volt per microsecond, whatever its designer does with currents and capacitors.

<SlewGbwFigure/>

The way out is to reduce $g_{m1}$ per unit tail current. **Emitter degeneration** adds $I_TR_E$ to $2U_T$: with 10 kΩ in each emitter at a 100 µA tail, $I_TR_E$ = 1 V and the slew rate rises twentyfold for the same bandwidth. A **MOS or JFET input** naturally has $I_T/g_m = u_0$, several times $2U_T$. The cost in both cases is higher offset and voltage noise, because the input stage converts less voltage into current. Fast bipolar op amps use degeneration, current-feedback architectures, or slew-enhancement circuits that boost the tail current only during large steps.

</section>

<section id="output">

<div className="section-heading"><span>05</span><h2>Add output drive and protection</h2></div>

A complementary emitter follower, NPN sourcing and PNP sinking, buffers the high-impedance gain-stage output. Chapter 37 covered its crossover distortion and class-AB biasing; inside an op amp, the loop gain further suppresses what remains. Its base currents load the gain stage, which is why output stages often use Darlington or compound devices.

An op amp must also survive a short circuit to ground or a rail:

<ShortCircuitLimitCircuit/>

<div className="worked-example">

<span className="tiny-label">WORKED EXAMPLE / CURRENT LIMIT AND DISSIPATION</span><h3>27 Ω sense resistor, ±15 V supplies</h3>

<p><strong>Limit.</strong> $Q_2$ turns on when the sense drop reaches about 0.65 V: 0.65/27 = <strong>24 mA</strong>.</p>

<p><strong>Dissipation in a short.</strong> With the output shorted to ground and the op amp trying to drive it high, $Q_1$ carries 24 mA with almost 15 V across it: about <strong>360 mW</strong>, continuously. A small package with 150 °C/W of thermal resistance rises by more than 50 °C. Datasheets rate short-circuit duration for exactly this reason, and some parts add thermal shutdown.</p>

<p><strong>Normal cost.</strong> At 10 mA of load current the sense resistor drops 0.27 V, reducing the output swing and adding 27 Ω to the open-loop output resistance, which feedback then divides by the loop gain.</p>

</div>

**Recovery.** When the output saturates, transistors in the gain and output stages saturate too and store charge. Recovery can take microseconds, longer than the small-signal response suggests (Chapter 79). Rail-to-rail designs use common-emitter output devices to swing closer to the rails, at the cost of a higher open-loop output impedance.

**Input range.** An NPN input pair's common-mode range stops about a volt above the negative rail (tail compliance plus $V_{BE}$) and a volt or two below the positive rail. Some bipolar input stages exhibit phase reversal when driven beyond their range: the output jumps to the wrong rail. Check the datasheet's input range, not just the supply.

<div className="takeaway"><span className="tiny-label">THE IDEA TO KEEP</span><p>Bipolar inputs give the most $g_m$ per microamp, hence low voltage noise and high gain, but base current, current noise and <em>a slew rate tied to bandwidth by $2U_T$</em>: about 0.33 V/µs per MHz unless the input stage is degenerated.</p></div>

</section>

<section id="check">

<div className="section-heading"><span>06</span><h2>Check your understanding</h2></div>

<details>
<summary>1. Why do classic bipolar op amps slew slowly?</summary>
<div>

$SR/2\pi f_u = I_T/g_{m1}$, and for an undegenerated bipolar pair that is $2U_T$ ≈ 52 mV, fixed by physics. A 1 MHz bipolar op amp therefore slews at about 0.33 V/µs. The high transconductance per microamp that gives low noise also means little tail current per unit of bandwidth.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>2. A bipolar pair runs 50 µA per side with $\beta$ = 200. What bias current flows in each input, and what error does it cause through 100 kΩ?</summary>
<div>

$I_B$ = 50 µA/200 = **250 nA**, which drops **25 mV** across 100 kΩ, a large offset unless cancelled or matched (Chapter 78).

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>3. At what differential input does an undegenerated bipolar pair's gain fall by 1%?</summary>
<div>

$\tanh x ≈ x(1 - x^2/3)$, so 1% compression at $x^2/3$ = 0.01, $x$ = 0.173, $v_d = 2U_Tx$ ≈ **9 mV**. A MOS pair at 1 V overdrive stays within 1% to about 0.28 V.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>4. How does emitter degeneration change slew rate, bandwidth and noise?</summary>
<div>

It reduces $g_{m1}$ per unit of tail current, so for the same unity-gain frequency, $C_C$ shrinks and the slew rate $I_T/C_C$ rises: $SR = (2U_T + I_TR_E)\cdot2\pi f_u$. The cost is more input-referred voltage noise and offset, and the resistors' own thermal noise.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>5. Why does bias-current cancellation not remove current noise?</summary>
<div>

Cancellation injects a matched DC current to supply most of the base current. The base current still flows, and its shot noise, set by the full base current, is still present. The DC bias falls; the noise current does not, and the uncancelled residue can have either sign.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>6. A 27 Ω sense resistor limits the output current. Roughly what current, and what is the risk in a sustained short?</summary>
<div>

About 0.65 V/27 Ω ≈ **24 mA**. In a short to ground from ±15 V supplies, the output transistor dissipates about 15 V × 24 mA = 360 mW continuously, which can overheat a small package unless the part has thermal protection.

</div>
</details>
