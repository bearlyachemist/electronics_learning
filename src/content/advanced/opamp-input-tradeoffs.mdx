<div><span className="tiny-label">PREDICT BEFORE CALCULATING</span><p className="opening-question">Why can the op amp with the lowest voltage noise be the noisiest choice for a high-resistance sensor?</p></div>

<div>

Op-amp selection guides sort parts by their headline numbers: lowest noise, lowest offset, highest speed. Each headline is measured under conditions chosen to flatter it. A 1 nV/√Hz bipolar amplifier is measured with a zero-ohm source; a femtoamp CMOS amplifier's noise is quoted at 10 kHz, far above its flicker corner.

The useful question is: with my source, my bandwidth and my temperature range, which amplifier gives the smallest total error? The last six chapters supplied the pieces. This one puts them together into a method.

<section id="model">

<div className="section-heading"><span>01</span><h2>Include source impedance in every comparison</h2></div>

An op amp's input is characterized by four quantities: voltage noise $e_n$, current noise $i_n$, offset voltage and bias current. The source contributes its own thermal noise and turns both currents into voltages. Different input technologies sit at different points in this space:

| Input | Voltage noise | Current noise | Bias current | Typical strength |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Bipolar | lowest, 1–10 nV/√Hz | high, 0.3–3 pA/√Hz | 1–500 nA | low-impedance sources |
| JFET | 3–20 nV/√Hz | very low, 1–10 fA/√Hz | pA, doubling per 10 °C | 10 kΩ – 100 MΩ sources |
| CMOS | 5–50 nV/√Hz, high $1/f$ | lowest, ~1 fA/√Hz | fA–pA | very high impedance, low power |
| Chopper / auto-zero | 10–50 nV/√Hz, no $1/f$ | modest, with spikes | pA–nA, with spikes | DC precision |

These are typical ranges, not limits; the next sections show how to compare specific parts.

</section>

<section id="derive">

<div className="section-heading"><span>02</span><h2>Build a frequency-aware input budget</h2></div>

For a source impedance $Z_S(f)$, the total input-referred noise density is

<Equation tex={String.raw`e_{tot}^2(f)=e_n^2(f)+i_n^2(f)\,|Z_S(f)|^2+4k_BT\,\mathrm{Re}\,Z_S(f)`} label="TOTAL INPUT NOISE"/>

assuming uncorrelated amplifier noise sources and a passive source. For a resistive source, the voltage-noise and current-noise terms are equal at the **optimum source resistance**

<Equation tex={String.raw`R_{opt}=\frac{e_n}{i_n}`}/>

Near $R_{opt}$ the amplifier adds the least noise relative to the source. A bipolar part with 1 nV/√Hz and 2 pA/√Hz has $R_{opt}$ = 500 Ω; a JFET with 5 nV/√Hz and 5 fA/√Hz has $R_{opt}$ = 1 MΩ. Matching the amplifier to the source impedance matters more than minimizing either $e_n$ or $i_n$ alone.

For DC accuracy, use Chapter 78's signed bias-current terms, offset, drift and CMRR. Total output noise also includes the feedback resistors' noise and the frequency-dependent noise gain; integrate the noise power through the actual circuit's response.

</section>

<div className="worked-example">

<span className="tiny-label">WORKED EXAMPLE / TWO AMPLIFIERS, THREE SOURCES</span><h3>A: 2 nV/√Hz, 1 pA/√Hz. B: 8 nV/√Hz, 0.01 pA/√Hz. 300 K.</h3>

<p><strong>100 Ω source</strong> (1.29 nV/√Hz thermal): A gives 2.4, B 8.1 nV/√Hz. A is clearly better.</p>

<p><strong>10 kΩ source</strong> (12.9 nV/√Hz): A gives 16.4 (its current noise adds 10 nV/√Hz through 10 kΩ), B 15.2. Nearly a tie, both dominated by the resistor.</p>

<p><strong>1 MΩ source</strong> (129 nV/√Hz): A gives <strong>1008</strong>, because 1 pA/√Hz through 1 MΩ is 1 µV/√Hz. B gives 129, essentially the resistor alone.</p>

<p>That answers the opening question: A, the "low-noise" amplifier, is eight times noisier than B with the 1 MΩ source. The DC picture is worse still: if A's bias current is 100 nA and B's 2 pA, the 1 MΩ source produces a 100 mV offset with A and 2 µV with B.</p>

<section id="chopper">

<div className="section-heading"><span>04</span><h2>Chopping and auto-zeroing</h2></div>

For DC and very-low-frequency measurements, two errors dominate everything else: offset drift and $1/f$ noise. Both are slow. Two techniques remove slow errors by refusing to let the signal stay slow inside the amplifier.

<ChopperBlockFigure/>

A **chopper** modulates the input up to the chopping frequency, amplifies it there, and demodulates it back; the amplifier's own offset and flicker noise are modulated up and filtered away. An **auto-zero** amplifier periodically disconnects its input, measures its own offset on a capacitor, and subtracts it. Both reach offsets of a few microvolts and drifts of a few nanovolts per degree:

<LowFreqNoiseFigure/>

Neither is free. Chopping leaves ripple and spikes at $f_{ch}$ (often tens to hundreds of kilohertz) that must be filtered, and switching charge injection appears as input current spikes that can disturb a high-impedance source. Auto-zeroing folds high-frequency noise down into the baseband, raising the white noise floor. "Zero drift" is a product category, not literally zero error.

<div className="worked-example">

<span className="tiny-label">WORKED EXAMPLE / A SLOW BRIDGE MEASUREMENT</span><h3>350 Ω strain-gauge bridge, 0.1–10 Hz, 50 °C range</h3>

<p>The bridge's source resistance is about 350 Ω, near the bipolar part's $R_{opt}$, so its 3 nV/√Hz floor looks ideal. But in the 0.1–10 Hz band its flicker region contributes noise, and its drift, say 0.5 µV/°C, adds <strong>25 µV</strong> over 50 °C.</p>

<p>A chopper with 20 nV/√Hz and no $1/f$ region integrates to about <strong>0.06 µV rms</strong> over 0.1–10 Hz, and its drift of 0.02 µV/°C adds only 1 µV. The bridge's own thermal noise (2.4 nV/√Hz) is far below both amplifiers' floors here. For this slow measurement, the chopper wins by drift, not by noise density.</p>

</div>

</section>

<section id="limits">

<div className="section-heading"><span>05</span><h2>Choose against the actual measurement</h2></div>

A practical procedure:

1. **Characterize the source:** its impedance, including how it varies with frequency, signal and temperature, and its signal range.
2. **State the requirements:** resolution and accuracy, bandwidth (or settling time), temperature range, supply, load and power.
3. **Compute the budget** for each candidate under those conditions: noise integrated over the band, DC errors at the worst-case temperature, common-mode and output ranges.
4. **Check the side effects:** input capacitance for a photodiode, chopping spikes for a high-impedance source, phase reversal or protection leakage near the rails.

Some typical outcomes:

- **pH electrode** (about 1 GΩ, DC): bias current dominates everything. An electrometer-grade CMOS or JFET part with femtoamp bias, guarded inputs and a clean board.
- **Dynamic microphone** (about 200 Ω, audio band): voltage noise dominates. A low-noise bipolar part.
- **Photodiode** (high resistance, tens of picofarads, kHz–MHz): current noise and input capacitance both matter, and the capacitance shapes the noise gain (Chapter 87). A JFET or low-capacitance CMOS part.
- **Thermocouple or bridge** (low resistance, DC): drift and $1/f$ noise dominate. A chopper or auto-zero part.

Source impedance is often complex: a piezoelectric sensor is mostly capacitance, a photodiode is capacitance in parallel with a large resistance, a filter network varies with frequency. Use $Z_S(f)$ in the budget when a single resistance does not describe it.

<div className="takeaway"><span className="tiny-label">THE IDEA TO KEEP</span><p>Compare amplifiers by <em>total</em> error with your source: $e_n$ matters for low impedances, $i_n$ and bias current for high ones, drift and $1/f$ noise for slow measurements. $R_{opt} = e_n/i_n$ tells you which kind of source an amplifier was made for.</p></div>

</section>

<section id="precision-depth">

<div className="section-heading"><span>+</span><h2>Select the amplifier by source, operating point and architecture</h2></div>

<h3>A pinch-off tester measures an operating point, not a magic constant</h3>

<PinchOffTesterFigure/>

The servo holds the JFET source near zero by driving its gate. A 5 MΩ resistor to −5 V requests approximately 1 µA drain current; the drain is at +5 V. If the source rises, the minus input rises, the op amp lowers the gate voltage, and conduction decreases: restoring feedback. Gate voltage is then approximately $V_{GS}$ at 1 µA. The output-connected small capacitor provides a direct high-frequency feedback path around the slower JFET/source node; choose it by loop response, not by treating the JFET as instantaneous.

For the teaching Shockley model $I_D=I_{DSS}(1-V_{GS}/V_P)^2$, take $I_{DSS}=2$ mA and $V_P=-3$ V. At 1 µA,

<Equation tex={String.raw`V_{GS}=V_P\left(1-\sqrt{I_D/I_{DSS}}\right)=-2.933\ \mathrm V`}/>

That is close to, but not exactly, −3 V. Datasheets define $V_{GS(off)}$ at a specified residual current and drain voltage; compare like conditions. A 10 nA bias current at the sensed source is already 1% of the test current. Offset changes the source voltage, test current and inferred gate-source voltage. Guard and clean the high-impedance node, keep the gate junction reverse biased, and provide enough output swing for the most negative expected gate voltage. A device outside that swing gives a saturated tester, not a valid pinch-off reading.

<h3>Seven useful precision classes</h3>

A selection table is most useful when it compares constraints at the same supply, temperature, common mode and load. These are design classes rather than a shopping list of historic parts:

| Class | Principal strength | Tradeoff to calculate |
|---|---|---|
| Conventional precision bipolar | Low voltage noise and stable offset | Bias current and current noise with source impedance |
| Super-beta or bias-compensated bipolar | Smaller DC current error | Residual current noise, protection and current sign |
| Precision JFET | Low input current and useful AC performance | Temperature leakage, input capacitance and common-mode range |
| Conventional CMOS | Low supply voltage and small room-temperature bias | Flicker noise, mismatch and RRIO handover |
| Chopper / auto-zero | Small low-frequency offset and drift | Switching current, ripple, aliasing and recovery |
| Micropower precision | Battery life and low heating | Bandwidth, slew, output current and settling |
| Wideband precision | Dynamic accuracy and low distortion | Supply current, layout, gain restrictions and load stability |

Check minimum and maximum supply, signal range, offset and its temperature dependence, **integrated** voltage noise, signed bias current and current noise, frequency-dependent CMRR/PSRR, GBW, slew rate, settling and distortion. Test output current and common-mode transitions too. Single, dual and quad packages change board area, routing, thermal coupling and channel interactions. Unused channels require a valid stable connection; leaving their inputs floating can cause oscillation or excess current.

<h3>Auto-zero details that decide whether it helps</h3>

A low DC offset is valuable for bridges and thermocouples, but input switching draws short pulses that a large source impedance converts to voltage. For an illustrative 20 fC net charge per cycle at 50 kHz, the average current is 1 nA; across 1 MΩ that is 1 mV. Balanced switching may reduce the net charge greatly, while still leaving pulse-related noise. Input RC filtering can reduce pulses but changes the input's impedance, acquisition and bias-error path.

Auto-zero sampling folds wideband noise into baseband. Chopping shifts offset and flicker away from DC but leaves ripple and harmonics. Hybrid architectures use both mechanisms. A 5 nV/√Hz flat noise density over 10 Hz ENBW gives 15.8 nV RMS; it says nothing about a 100 kHz ripple spur. Check spectra, overload recovery, common-mode-dependent input current, multiplexed-source settling and startup. Synchronization and carefully chosen filtering can help; asynchronous sampling can turn switching tones into low-frequency beats.

<h3>Stored correction and alternating channels explain auto-zero side effects</h3>

A simple auto-zero input stage has a correction phase, in which its inputs are connected to a known equal voltage and its offset is stored, followed by a signal phase, in which that stored correction is applied. The stored quantity need not be directly at the signal inputs: an auxiliary transconductance stage can inject a compensating current based on a voltage stored elsewhere. An active integrator can arrange switching between two nearly equal virtual-ground voltages, reducing charge injection compared with switching between widely different node voltages. These changes improve a specific internal error mechanism, not every external source-impedance effect.

One stage used alternately for correction and signal has gaps in its forward transfer. A **ping-pong** design alternates two input stages so one can process the signal while the other corrects itself. Their handover can leave mismatch transients; multi-path designs can preserve a fast signal path while a slower nulling path corrects DC. Check the signal response around the clock and correction bandwidth, not merely the DC offset.

A stored noise sample becomes a low-frequency correction error. As a simple sampling model, white noise density $e_n$ integrated over one-sided bandwidth $B$ gives sample variance $e_n^2B$. If successive correction samples are independent at rate $f_s$, their held low-frequency noise density is approximately $e_n\sqrt{2B/f_s}$. With B = 1 MHz and $f_s=100$ kHz, that illustrative factor is $\sqrt{20}\approx4.47$. Real filtering, correlations, duty cycle and multi-path cancellation modify it, but the example explains why allowing unlimited bandwidth in the correction loop can raise baseband noise.

A traditional low-bandwidth chopper instrument instead modulates a DC signal into an AC-coupled amplifier and demodulates it. Its signal bandwidth is limited relative to the modulation frequency; it is not automatically interchangeable with a wideband internally corrected op amp. At submicrovolt levels, thermal junction voltages and gradients at the board's connections can dominate either architecture. Reducing input offset does not remove those external voltage sources.

<h3>Other amplifier types have different equations</h3>

A voltage-feedback op amp senses differential voltage and is governed by $A$ and noise gain. A **current-feedback** amplifier typically has a low-impedance inverting port; its feedback resistor is part of the internal dynamic design, so use its transimpedance model and specified resistor range rather than blindly applying $f_t/NG$. An **OTA** delivers $i_o=g_mv_d$ and needs an output load to create voltage; controlled $g_m$ is useful for tunable filters. A **buffer** supplies current at near-unity gain but may need an external precision loop. **Power amplifiers**, **isolation amplifiers**, **programmable-gain amplifiers**, **instrumentation amplifiers** and **fully differential amplifiers** solve different current, grounding, range or interface problems. Choose the ports and feedback structure before comparing headline bandwidths.

A precision/low-noise input amplifier followed by a fast output amplifier can combine strengths. The slow stage must correct DC error while the fast path carries the required bandwidth; two unconstrained nested high-gain loops can oscillate. Chapter 93 develops a composite-buffer model and its separate fast feedback path.

<section id="check">

<div className="section-heading"><span>06</span><h2>Check your understanding</h2></div>

<details>
<summary>1. Why can the lowest-voltage-noise amplifier be the noisiest choice for a high-resistance sensor?</summary>
<div>

Low-voltage-noise bipolar inputs usually have high current noise. Through a high source resistance, $i_nR_S$ dominates: 1 pA/√Hz through 1 MΩ is 1 µV/√Hz, far above both the amplifier's 2 nV/√Hz and the resistor's own 129 nV/√Hz.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>2. What is the optimum source resistance of an amplifier with $e_n$ = 4 nV/√Hz and $i_n$ = 0.4 pA/√Hz?</summary>
<div>

$R_{opt} = e_n/i_n$ = 4 nV/0.4 pA = **10 kΩ**. Near that source resistance its voltage and current noise contribute equally and its noise relative to the source is smallest.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>3. How does a chopper remove offset drift?</summary>
<div>

It modulates the signal to a high frequency before the amplifier, and demodulates it after. The amplifier's offset and $1/f$ noise are added between the two modulators, so the second modulator moves them up to the chopping frequency, where a low-pass filter removes them, while it moves the signal back to DC.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>4. Why might a chopper amplifier be a poor choice for a 1 GΩ electrode?</summary>
<div>

Its input switches inject charge, appearing as current spikes and an average bias current that can be nanoamps, much larger than an electrometer CMOS part's femtoamps. Through 1 GΩ, even 1 pA is 1 mV. Its low drift is wasted if bias current dominates.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>5. A 350 Ω bridge measured from 0.1 to 10 Hz over 50 °C: why can a chopper with 20 nV/√Hz beat a bipolar part with 3 nV/√Hz?</summary>
<div>

Over 0.1–10 Hz, the bipolar part's $1/f$ noise and especially its offset drift (25 µV over 50 °C at 0.5 µV/°C) dominate. The chopper has no $1/f$ region and drifts only about 1 µV, so its total error is far smaller despite its higher white noise floor.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>6. Why must the budget use the source impedance at the frequency of interest?</summary>
<div>

Many sources are reactive: a photodiode's capacitance makes its impedance fall with frequency, a piezoelectric sensor is mostly capacitive. Current noise multiplies $|Z_S(f)|$, and only the real part of $Z_S$ produces thermal noise. A single DC resistance can badly misestimate both.

</div>
</details>
