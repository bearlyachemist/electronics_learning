<h1>Transimpedance amplifiers<br/><em>Turn a current into a voltage without letting the input move.</em></h1>

<p className="dek">Convert a photodiode's current to a voltage at a virtual ground. Find why the detector's capacitance makes the noise gain rise with frequency, choose the feedback capacitor for fast settling, and budget the noise and dynamic range of a real receiver.</p>

<div className="chapter-meta"><span>55–75 min + exploration</span><span>Bandwidth and stability (Ch. 79) · Common gate (Ch. 68) · Input technologies (Ch. 82)</span></div>

</div>

<div className="opening-grid">

<div><span className="tiny-label">PREDICT BEFORE CALCULATING</span><p className="opening-question">Adding a capacitor across the feedback resistor lowers a transimpedance amplifier's bandwidth. Can it make the measurement settle faster?</p></div>

<div>

Many sensors produce a current: photodiodes, photomultipliers, ionization chambers, some gas sensors. The simplest current-to-voltage converter is a resistor, but a resistor lets its voltage change with the current, which disturbs a sensor that needs a fixed bias, and forms a slow RC with the sensor's capacitance (Chapter 68 met that problem).

An op amp with a feedback resistor holds the sensor at a virtual ground and turns the current into an output voltage of $-IR_F$. The idea fits in one line; the design does not, because the sensor's capacitance sits at the op amp's input, and as far as stability is concerned, that changes everything.

</div>

</div>

<section id="model">

<div className="section-heading"><span>01</span><h2>Define current direction and capacitance</h2></div>

<OpAmpFigure mode="tia"/>

The photodiode injects $I_{PD}$ into the minus input, the plus input is grounded, and the feedback network is $R_F$ in parallel with $C_F$. Everything capacitive at the minus input, the photodiode's junction capacitance, the op amp's input capacitance and the wiring, is lumped as $C_{IN}$. Feedback holds the minus input at 0 V, so the photodiode operates at zero bias (photovoltaic mode) and its current flows through $R_F$: a positive injected current makes the output go negative.

On a single supply the plus input is held at a reference above ground; for speed the photodiode is often reverse biased, which lowers its capacitance but adds dark current. Both are details of the same circuit.

</section>

<section id="derive">

<div className="section-heading"><span>02</span><h2>Solve signal gain and frequency-dependent noise gain</h2></div>

KCL at the minus node, with finite gain $A$ so that $v_- = -v_o/A$:

<Equation tex={String.raw`Z_F=\frac{R_F}{1+sR_FC_F},\qquad NG(s)=1+sC_{IN}Z_F,\qquad\frac{V_O}{I_{PD}}=-\frac{Z_F}{1+NG(s)/A(s)}`} label="TRANSIMPEDANCE WITH FINITE GAIN"/>

With infinite gain the transimpedance is just $-Z_F$: $R_F$ at low frequency, rolled off by $C_F$. The interesting quantity is the noise gain. At DC it is 1: with the input current source removed, nothing but $R_F$ connects the output to the minus input, and nothing connects the minus input to ground. But $C_{IN}$ does connect it to ground at higher frequencies, and the noise gain rises:

<Equation tex={String.raw`NG(f)\approx\begin{cases}1 & f\ll\dfrac{1}{2\pi R_F(C_{IN}+C_F)}\\[6pt] 1+\dfrac{C_{IN}}{C_F} & f\gg\dfrac{1}{2\pi R_FC_F}\end{cases}`}/>

</section>

<section id="stability">

<div className="section-heading"><span>03</span><h2>See the stability problem</h2></div>

The loop crosses unity where the noise gain meets the open-loop gain. If the noise gain is still rising there, at 20 dB per decade, while $A(f)$ is falling at 20 dB per decade, their closing rate is 40 dB per decade: the loop phase is near −180° and the circuit rings or oscillates. Without $C_F$ the noise gain rises without limit and a TIA with any significant input capacitance is unstable.

<TiaNoiseGainFigure/>

$C_F$ levels the noise gain off at $1 + C_{IN}/C_F$. If it has levelled off before meeting $A(f)$, the crossing is at a 20 dB/decade closing rate and the loop is stable. Setting the levelling point at the crossing gives roughly 45° of phase margin:

<Equation tex={String.raw`C_F\approx\sqrt{\frac{C_{IN}}{2\pi R_Ff_t}},\qquad f_{-3\,dB}\approx\sqrt{\frac{f_t}{2\pi R_FC_{IN}}}`} label="TIA COMPENSATION STARTING POINT"/>

The second result is the useful one for planning: the achievable bandwidth grows only as the square root of the op amp's $f_t$, and falls as the square root of $R_F$ and of the input capacitance.

</section>

<section id="cases">

<div className="section-heading"><span>04</span><h2>Compare three feedback capacitors</h2></div>

<TiaStepFigure/>

<div className="worked-example">

<span className="tiny-label">WORKED EXAMPLE / 1 MΩ, 50 pF, A 1 MHz OP AMP</span><h3>Choose $C_F$</h3>

<p><strong>Starting point.</strong> $C_F = \sqrt{50\text{ pF}/(2\pi × 1\text{ MΩ} × 1\text{ MHz})}$ = 2.8 pF, and the bandwidth $\sqrt{1\text{ MHz}/(2\pi × 1\text{ MΩ} × 50\text{ pF})}$ ≈ 56 kHz.</p>

<p><strong>Three choices.</strong> $C_F$ = 1 pF: noise-gain plateau 51, crossing while still rising; 52% overshoot, 65 µs to settle within 1%. $C_F$ = 2.8 pF: plateau 19; 15% overshoot, <strong>25 µs</strong>. $C_F$ = 10 pF: plateau 6, feedback pole 16 kHz; no overshoot, but 43 µs.</p>

<p>That answers the opening question. Going from 1 pF to 2.8 pF lowers the ideal bandwidth, $1/(2\pi R_FC_F)$, from 159 kHz to 56 kHz, yet the measurement settles in less than half the time, because the ringing is gone. Judge a TIA by its settling to the required accuracy, not by its −3 dB frequency.</p>

<p><strong>Practical $C_F$.</strong> 2.8 pF is comparable to the stray capacitance across a resistor's body (0.1–0.3 pF) and the board. Real designs often end up with a nominal 2.2 or 2.7 pF part plus the parasitics, then verify with a pulse.</p>

</div>

<OpAmpLab kind="tia" client:load/>

<p className="caption">Live lab. $R_F$ = 1 MΩ, $C_{IN}$ = 50 pF, $A_0 = 10^5$. Sweep the feedback capacitance from 1 pF to 100 pF and watch the peaking near 50–100 kHz disappear while the bandwidth falls. Then raise the op amp's GBW at a fixed $C_F$ of 3 pF and note that the bandwidth improves only slowly.</p>

<section id="noise">

<div className="section-heading"><span>05</span><h2>Budget noise and dynamic range</h2></div>

<TiaNoiseFigure/>

Three noise sources compete, and the input capacitance reorders them with frequency.

**The feedback resistor** adds a current noise $\sqrt{4kT/R_F}$ in parallel with the signal: 0.129 pA/√Hz for 1 MΩ. A larger $R_F$ gives more transimpedance and **less** current noise, the reason TIAs use the largest $R_F$ the bandwidth allows.

**The op amp's voltage noise** is multiplied by the noise gain, which rises toward $1 + C_{IN}/C_F$. Referred to the input as a current, it is about $e_n \cdot 2\pi fC_{IN}$: 10 nV/√Hz with 50 pF at 50 kHz is 0.16 pA/√Hz, as large as the resistor's. This "$e_nC$ noise" is why low-capacitance detectors and low-voltage-noise op amps matter in fast receivers.

**The op amp's current noise** adds directly to the signal. A FET-input part's femtoamps are negligible; a bipolar part's picoamps would dominate.

<div className="worked-example">

<span className="tiny-label">WORKED EXAMPLE / RANGE AND SIGNAL-TO-NOISE</span><h3>1 MΩ, ±4.5 V output</h3>

<p><strong>Range.</strong> 10 nA, 1 µA and 10 µA give −10 mV, −1 V and −10 V. The output can reach only about ±4.5 V, so the largest measurable current is 4.5 µA. Dark current and the op amp's offset (say 1 nA and 1 mV) add 1 nA + 1 nA to every reading.</p>

<p><strong>Shot noise.</strong> A 1 µA photocurrent carries shot noise $\sqrt{2qI}$ = 0.57 pA/√Hz, larger than the resistor's 0.13. Above about 50 nA this receiver is limited by the light's own statistics, the best any receiver can do.</p>

<p><strong>Integrated.</strong> Over the 56 kHz bandwidth, the resistor alone contributes $0.129 × \sqrt{56\,000 × 1.57}$ ≈ 38 pA rms, and the $e_nC$ term a similar amount. A 10 nA signal is about 200 times the noise.</p>

</div>



<h3 id="book-notes">A dark-current error and a shot-noise floor need different corrections</h3>

A photodiode's steady dark current produces a DC output $-I_DR_F$, which can sometimes be measured and subtracted. Its shot noise remains after that subtraction. For independent carrier arrivals, the one-sided current-noise density is $i_n=\sqrt{2qI}$, using the total relevant mean photocurrent and dark current.

With an illustrative 1 µA mean current, the shot-noise density is **0.566 pA/√Hz**. In an ideal flat 10 kHz equivalent noise bandwidth, that becomes 56.6 pA RMS. A 1 MΩ transimpedance converts it to **56.6 µV RMS**, even if the mean output is perfectly calibrated. Reducing DC offset does not remove this random term.

Feedback resistance produces its own thermal current noise, $\sqrt{4k_BT/R_F}$, about 0.129 pA/√Hz for 1 MΩ at 300 K. Shot and resistor terms add in power. Amplifier current noise and frequency-shaped voltage noise must then join the budget with their actual transfer functions. If the transimpedance rolls off within the band, integrate that response instead of multiplying every density by the same square-root bandwidth.

Strong illumination can also overload the receiver, leaving a recovery problem when the detector returns to darkness. A larger feedback resistor increases sensitivity but reduces the mean-current range. Define maximum expected DC current, smallest signal modulation, noise bandwidth and overload sequence together; a noise calculation made only in darkness can miss the dominant operating case.

</section>

<section id="limits">

<div className="section-heading"><span>06</span><h2>Protect the summing node's capacitance budget</h2></div>

**Board capacitance is circuit.** A centimetre of trace or a sensor cable adds picofarads to $C_{IN}$ and changes the compensation. A long cable is not even lumped; it can resonate. Mount the detector as close to the op amp as possible, and guard the summing node.

**Bigger $R_F$ is not free.** It raises transimpedance and lowers current noise, but narrows the range, lowers the bandwidth ($\propto 1/\sqrt{R_F}$) and makes the circuit more sensitive to leakage across the board. A 1 GΩ resistor needs a guarded, clean layout.

**Overload.** A bright flash drives the output to a rail. Recovery from saturation can take far longer than the small-signal settling time; some receivers clamp the output or the summing node with diodes. Specify the dynamic range including recovery from the largest expected pulse.

<div className="takeaway"><span className="tiny-label">THE IDEA TO KEEP</span><p>A TIA's noise gain rises from 1 to $1 + C_{IN}/C_F$; <em>$C_F$ must level it off before it meets the open-loop gain</em>. Start from $C_F \approx \sqrt{C_{IN}/2\pi R_Ff_t}$, and judge the result by its settling and its noise, not its −3 dB point.</p></div>

</section>

<section id="precision-depth">

<div className="section-heading"><span>07</span><h2>Apply sensitive feedback to troubleshooting and precision DMMs</h2></div>

<h3>A stuck-node tracer turns trace resistance into a diagnostic signal</h3>

A copper trace 20 mm long, 0.5 mm wide and 35 µm thick has approximately $R=\rho L/(wt)=19.7$ mΩ using $\rho=1.72\times10^{-8}$ Ω·m. Injecting a controlled 10 mA diagnostic current produces about 197 µV along that length. Probe the voltage difference along successive trace segments: a drop indicates current flow toward the short, whereas a branch carrying no diagnostic current has little drop. The injection source needs current and voltage limits appropriate to the circuit under test; unpowered-board and powered-logic tests have different constraints.

<StuckNodeFigure/>

A floating, zero-centered sensitive amplifier can show the sign of the drop. An inverting gain −100 using 2 kΩ input and 200 kΩ feedback turns 197 µV into about −19.7 mV. Anti-parallel diodes across the feedback resistor progressively reduce transresistance at larger inputs, preserving sensitivity near zero without sending the indicator immediately off scale. This is the same idea as nonlinear feedback in a TIA, here driven by a voltage through a resistor.

With a symmetric diode model of saturation current $I_S$ and ideality factor $n$, KCL gives

<Equation tex={String.raw`\frac{v_i}{R_{in}}=-\frac{v_o}{R_F}-2I_S\sinh\left(\frac{v_o}{nU_T}\right)`}/>

The relation is odd and monotonic, with compression set by diode temperature and current. With $I_S=5$ nA, $n=1.1$ and $U_T=25.85$ mV, the diodes' small-signal conductance is $2I_S/(nU_T)$; in parallel with 200 kΩ it gives an effective resistance near 186.9 kΩ and initial voltage gain −93.4. A nominal passive gain −100 is therefore only an approximation even close to zero. Calibrate the indicator if its scale matters.

Offset must be smaller than the smallest sought trace drop. A 20 µV input offset is already 10% of the example's 197 µV. Battery operation provides a floating supply but not unlimited input common-mode isolation or fault withstand. Null before probing, use short paired leads, and distinguish a true trace drop from thermal EMF at the contacts.

<h3>Precision DMM front ends solve several problems at once</h3>

TAOE's two historic instrument case studies illustrate an architectural progression. A matched JFET follower pair gives very low input current and shields a precision downstream amplifier from a high-resistance source. Holding both JFET drain current and drain-source voltage nearly constant reduces input-dependent leakage, capacitance effects and gain nonlinearity. A differential common-source JFET stage adds gain ahead of the precision amplifier, reducing downstream input-referred noise and increasing total loop gain; larger devices improve voltage noise but add substantial input capacitance and require offset control.

These are **design lessons**, not reconstructed proprietary schematics. The quiet input pair, stable bias/compliance circuitry, precision gain network, guard, input protection, calibration and integrating ADC work as one system. The gain switch must preserve leakage and ratio accuracy, not merely select a resistor. A high-gain stage can tolerate a device's large initial offset only if the complete circuit has enough correction range and headroom.

For an original instrument model, a 10 V ADC span with two million uniform codes gives 5 µV per code at the ADC. Preceding gain 100 refers that to 50 nV at the input. That resolution does not imply 50 nV absolute accuracy. A 1 ppm gain error at 10 mV is 10 nV; a 1 µV residual zero error is twenty input codes; connector thermal gradients can dominate both. Averaging reduces independent white noise roughly as $1/\sqrt{N}$, but coherent mains pickup, drift and flicker require other measures. Integration over an integer number of line periods can reject the corresponding steady line-frequency component while slowing measurement.

<h3>Do not turn an input-current requirement into an untested assumption</h3>

A protection diode's leakage specified at high reverse voltage may poorly describe its conductance around a few millivolts. A JFET specified in normal saturation may have no guaranteed leakage at a deliberately tiny drain-source voltage. Characterize the intended operating point, cleanliness and temperature, record whether parts were screened, and include startup/overload recovery. An instrument with spectacular averaged noise can still have slow settling after a large preceding input or dielectric absorption in its range-switching network.

<summary>Worked self-check: what can the tracer infer from a zero drop?</summary>
<div>

It can infer that little diagnostic current flows through that segment at the measurement's sensitivity. A short can still lie beyond a parallel current path, an open connection or a segment with too little resistance. Verify injected current and compare successive branches; a zero reading alone does not prove a trace has no fault.

<section id="check">

<div className="section-heading"><span>08</span><h2>Check your understanding</h2></div>

<details>
<summary>1. Why can a feedback capacitor make a TIA settle faster?</summary>
<div>

Without enough $C_F$ the loop crosses unity while the noise gain is still rising, leaving little phase margin and causing ringing that takes a long time to decay. $C_F$ restores phase margin. Its lower ideal bandwidth costs less settling time than the ringing it removes: 25 µs instead of 65 µs in the example.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>2. Why is the noise gain 1 at DC but $1 + C_{IN}/C_F$ at high frequency?</summary>
<div>

At DC only $R_F$ connects the output to the minus input and nothing connects the minus input to ground, so the op amp acts as a follower for its own errors. At high frequency $C_{IN}$ and $C_F$ form a capacitive divider from the output to ground, with the minus input at its midpoint, giving a noninverting gain of $1 + C_{IN}/C_F$.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>3. With $R_F$ = 10 MΩ, $C_{IN}$ = 20 pF and a 10 MHz op amp, estimate $C_F$ and the bandwidth.</summary>
<div>

$C_F \approx \sqrt{20\text{ pF}/(2\pi × 10\text{ MΩ} × 10\text{ MHz})}$ ≈ **0.18 pF**, about the stray capacitance of the resistor itself. Bandwidth $\approx\sqrt{10\text{ MHz}/(2\pi × 10\text{ MΩ} × 20\text{ pF})}$ ≈ **89 kHz**.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>4. Why does a larger feedback resistor lower the TIA's input-referred current noise?</summary>
<div>

The resistor's thermal current noise is $\sqrt{4kT/R_F}$, which falls as $R_F$ rises, while the signal's conversion gain rises as $R_F$. Signal-to-noise improves as $\sqrt{R_F}$, at the cost of range and bandwidth.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>5. What is "$e_nC$ noise"?</summary>
<div>

The op amp's voltage noise $e_n$ appears across the input capacitance $C_{IN}$ and drives a noise current $e_n\cdot2\pi fC_{IN}$ through it, which the feedback resistor converts to output noise. It grows with frequency and with detector capacitance and often dominates a fast photodiode receiver.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>6. Above what photocurrent is a 1 MΩ receiver limited by shot noise rather than by its feedback resistor?</summary>
<div>

When $\sqrt{2qI}$ exceeds $\sqrt{4kT/R_F}$, that is $I > 2kT/(qR_F)$ = 0.052 V/1 MΩ ≈ **52 nA**. Above that, the light's own Poisson statistics dominate.

</div>
</details>
