<div className="opening-grid">

<div><span className="tiny-label">PREDICT BEFORE CALCULATING</span><p className="opening-question">An analog circuit computes exactly the right function on paper. What stops it from being an accurate instrument?</p></div>

<div>

Every circuit so far used feedback to make an op amp linear. Feedback can just as well make it nonlinear, in a controlled way: put a diode in the feedback path and the circuit computes a logarithm; apply feedback to the plus input and it makes decisions; let the loop gain be exactly one at one frequency and it oscillates.

The last section of the book then does what an engineer does with all of this: takes a sensor, a specification and a handful of op-amp circuits, and connects them into an instrument. The interesting part is not the connection. It is checking, stage by stage, that every range, error, noise and settling budget still closes once the pieces load and limit each other.

</div>

</div>

<section id="model">

<div className="section-heading"><span>01</span><h2>Let the feedback element define the function</h2></div>

<OpAmpFigure mode="log"/>

In an inverting amplifier, the input current $V_I/R_{IN}$ must flow through the feedback element, and the output becomes whatever voltage that element needs to carry it. A resistor gives a linear function. A diode, whose current is exponential in its voltage, gives the inverse: a **logarithm**. In the drawing, positive input current flows through the diode toward a negative output; with the minus input at virtual ground, $V_O = -V_D$.

The same principle builds antilog amplifiers (swap the diode and resistor), multipliers (add two logs, take the antilog) and true-rms converters. The functions are exact only as far as the element follows its law, which is the theme of this chapter.

</section>

<section id="log">

<div className="section-heading"><span>02</span><h2>Logarithms from a junction</h2></div>

With the junction law $I = I_Se^{V_D/nU_T}$:

<Equation tex={String.raw`V_O\approx-nU_T\ln\frac{V_I}{R_{IN}I_S},\qquad\Delta V_O=-nU_T\ln\frac{I_2}{I_1}\approx-59\ \text{mV per decade at 25 °C}`} label="LOG AMPLIFIER"/>

A log amplifier compresses six decades of current, 1 nA to 1 mA, into about 0.36 V. That is its value: photodiode receivers, dB meters and audio compressors all need it. But two terms in the equation are temperature sensitive:

<LogTransferFigure/>

<div className="worked-example">

<span className="tiny-label">WORKED EXAMPLE / WHERE THE LOG STOPS BEING ACCURATE</span><h3>A transistor log stage, 10 nA to 100 µA</h3>

<p><strong>Ideal.</strong> Two decades from 10 nA to 1 µA and two more to 100 µA give −118 mV per two decades at 25 °C.</p>

<p><strong>Leakage.</strong> At 10 nA, 1 nA of leakage (op amp bias current, board, protection) is a 10% current error, which the log turns into $59\text{ mV} × \log_{10}(1.1)$ = 2.5 mV: a 10% error in the quantity measured. At 100 µA the same leakage is invisible. The bottom of the range is set by leakage.</p>

<p><strong>Temperature.</strong> $I_S$ roughly doubles every 10 °C, shifting the whole curve, and $U_T = kT/q$ changes the slope by 0.34% per °C. A precision log amplifier uses a second, matched transistor carrying a reference current: the difference of the two junction voltages cancels $I_S$, leaving $U_T\ln(I/I_{REF})$, and a gain that rises with temperature (a thermistor or a PTAT resistor) cancels $U_T$.</p>

</div>

</section>

<section id="decide">

<div className="section-heading"><span>03</span><h2>Decisions from positive feedback</h2></div>

Feed the output back to the **plus** input and the op amp stops being a linear amplifier. Its output goes to one rail or the other and stays there until the input crosses a threshold that depends on which rail it is at. That is a Schmitt trigger: a comparator with hysteresis.

<SchmittCircuit/>

<SchmittFigure/>

<div className="worked-example">

<span className="tiny-label">WORKED EXAMPLE / SET THE THRESHOLDS</span><h3>5 V supply, $R_1$ = $R_2$ = 10 kΩ, $R_3$ = 82 kΩ</h3>

<p>With the output at 0 V, the plus input is a divider of 10 kΩ to 5 V against 10 kΩ ∥ 82 kΩ to ground: 2.36 V. With the output at 5 V it is 2.64 V. The input must rise above 2.64 V to switch the output low, and fall below 2.36 V to switch it back: 0.28 V of hysteresis, centered at 2.5 V.</p>

<p>Noise on the input smaller than 0.28 V peak-to-peak cannot cause chatter. A window comparator, two comparators with thresholds above and below a range, tells whether a signal is inside the range; the same hysteresis rule applies to each.</p>

</div>

An op amp can be used as a comparator, but slowly: it saturates and must recover, and some op amps misbehave with large differential inputs. Dedicated comparators switch in nanoseconds and have outputs designed for logic levels.

</section>

<section id="oscillate">

<div className="section-heading"><span>04</span><h2>Sine waves from a controlled loop</h2></div>

A Wien-bridge network (a series RC feeding a parallel RC) passes exactly 1/3 of its input, with zero phase shift, at $f_0 = 1/(2\pi RC)$. Follow it with a noninverting amplifier of gain $K$ and close the loop: at $f_0$ the loop gain is $K/3$. The loop has positive feedback, so its characteristic equation is $1 - K\beta(s) = 0$, with $\beta(s) = sRC/[(sRC)^2 + 3sRC + 1]$ from Chapter 48. Multiply through by the denominator: $(sRC)^2 + 3sRC + 1 - KsRC = 0$. Dividing by $(RC)^2$ and writing $\omega_0 = 1/RC$, the closed loop's characteristic equation is

<Equation tex={String.raw`s^2+(3-K)\,\omega_0\,s+\omega_0^2=0`}/>

so $K < 3$ gives a decaying response, $K > 3$ a growing one, and only $K = 3$ exactly gives a steady sine. No resistor ratio holds $K$ at exactly 3, so a real oscillator starts with $K$ slightly above 3 and uses a control loop to bring it down as the amplitude grows:

<WienStartupFigure/>

<div className="worked-example">

<span className="tiny-label">WORKED EXAMPLE / A 1 kHz OSCILLATOR</span><h3>$C$ = 10 nF, 2 V peak</h3>

<p>$R = 1/(2\pi × 1\text{ kHz} × 10\text{ nF})$ = 15.9 kΩ. The amplifier needs gain 3, say $R_F$ = 20 kΩ and $R_G$ = 10 kΩ, with a small extra gain (a few percent) that the control element removes.</p>

<p><strong>Amplitude control.</strong> Classic designs use a small incandescent lamp or thermistor in $R_G$, whose resistance rises with the output's rms value, or a JFET used as a voltage-controlled resistor driven by a rectified and filtered copy of the output. The control loop's time constant must be many cycles long, or it will modulate the sine within each cycle and distort it.</p>

<p><strong>Slew and noise.</strong> 2 V at 1 kHz needs only 0.013 V/µs. Distortion is set by the control loop's ripple and the amplifier's linearity, not its speed.</p>

</div>

Letting the op amp clip instead of controlling $K$ also limits the amplitude, but the waveform is then a distorted sine. The rule from Chapter 74 holds here in reverse: an oscillator is a loop deliberately placed at zero phase margin, and keeping it there needs active control.

</section>

<section id="chain">

<div className="section-heading"><span>05</span><h2>Design a complete measurement chain</h2></div>

**The specification.** Measure the strain on a 350 Ω full bridge, excited by 5 V, whose output at full scale is ±5 mV (1 mV/V), with 0.1% of full-scale accuracy, from DC to 100 Hz, into a 16-bit ADC with a 0–5 V range.

<SignalChainFigure/>

<div className="worked-example">

<span className="tiny-label">WORKED EXAMPLE / THE CHAIN, STAGE BY STAGE</span><h3>From ±5 mV on 2.5 V to a 16-bit code</h3>

<p><strong>Gain and reference.</strong> To use ±1 V of the ADC's range around its center, the gain is 1 V/5 mV = 200. An INA with first-stage gain 20 and subtractor gain 10 keeps its internal outputs at 2.5 ± 0.05 V, well inside their range (Chapter 81). Its reference pin is driven by a buffered divider from the 5 V excitation, so the output's center is half the excitation.</p>

<p><strong>Ratiometric measurement.</strong> The ADC's reference is the same 5 V excitation. If the excitation drifts by 1%, the bridge's output, the INA's reference and the ADC's full scale all drift by 1%, and the digital code does not change. A ratiometric chain needs no precision reference.</p>

<p><strong>Filter.</strong> A second-order Butterworth low-pass at 100 Hz (Chapter 85) sets the noise bandwidth to about 111 Hz and attenuates interference, including 50/60 Hz harmonics above the band, before the ADC samples.</p>

<p><strong>ADC driver.</strong> A rail-to-rail op amp and an RC charge reservoir settle the ADC's sampling kickback to half an LSB within its acquisition time (Chapter 78).</p>

</div>



<h3 id="book-notes">Multiplication can be made from time, switching and averaging</h3>

A voltage need not control transistor transconductance to become one factor in a product. Compare $V_X$ with a rising ramp from zero to $V_R$. For $0\le V_X\le V_R$, the comparator produces a duty fraction $D=V_X/V_R$. An analog switch then selects $V_Y$ for that fraction and zero for the remainder. A low-pass averages the switched waveform:

<Equation tex={String.raw`\overline V_O=D V_Y=\frac{V_XV_Y}{V_R}`}/>

With $V_X=1.2$ V, $V_Y=2.0$ V and a 3 V ramp range, D = 0.4 and the ideal output is **0.8 V**. This joins the switch, ramp generator, comparator and filter chapters into a time-division multiplier. It is initially a unipolar construction: signed multiplication requires additional switching or offset arrangements.

The averaging filter must pass changes in the desired product while rejecting switching ripple. A single-pole 100 Hz corner driven at 10 kHz attenuates the switching fundamental by about 40 dB, but it also makes the product's response slow. Ramp nonlinearity becomes multiplication nonlinearity; comparator delay creates a duty error proportional to delay divided by period; switch charge injection and reference drift add further terms.

Multiplying two sinusoids and averaging provides a **phase-sensitive detector**. With inputs $A\cos\omega t$ and $B\cos(\omega t+\phi)$, a multiplier normalized by $V_R$ gives a DC term $AB\cos\phi/(2V_R)$ and a term at twice the frequency. A quadrature reference gives the sine component as well. For A = 1 V, B = 0.1 V, $V_R=1$ V and $\phi=60°$, the in-phase output is **25 mV**. This detects a known-frequency signal amid other frequencies, but interference synchronous with the reference remains, and a square-wave switching reference also responds to its odd harmonics.

<section id="limits">

<div className="section-heading"><span>06</span><h2>Close every budget</h2></div>

Now the budget, referred to the ADC's input. One LSB is 5 V/65 536 = 76 µV; the 0.1% accuracy target is 1 mV of the ±1 V full scale.

| Error source | At the INA input | At the ADC | Comment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bridge + INA white noise, 10.3 nV/√Hz × √111 Hz | 0.11 µV rms | 22 µV rms | 0.3 LSB rms: fine |
| Offset, zero-drift INA, 5 µV | 5 µV | 1.0 mV | equals the whole target: calibrate at zero strain |
| Offset drift, 0.05 µV/°C × 40 °C | 2 µV | 0.4 mV | remains after calibration |
| Gain resistor 0.05%, 10 ppm/°C | — | 0.5 + 0.4 mV at full scale | calibrate gain, or buy a better resistor |
| Excitation drift | cancelled | — | ratiometric |
| Common mode 2.5 V × CMRR 110 dB | 8 µV, constant | — | becomes part of the offset; calibrated out |

After a zero and a full-scale calibration, the remaining errors (drift, gain-resistor temperature coefficient, noise) add to about 0.8 mV worst case: inside the 1 mV target, but only just. The budget also says where to spend money next: on the gain resistor's temperature coefficient, not on a quieter amplifier.

That answers the opening question. A circuit that computes the right function is a necessary start. An instrument also needs every error enumerated, referred to one place, compared with the requirement, and checked at the extremes: of temperature, of common mode, of signal, and in time, after an overload.

**Validate in stages:** DC operating points and internal ranges, resistor ratios, AC response with finite-gain op amps, transients and settling, noise integrated over the real bandwidth. The MOSFET and op-amp chapters provide the models, but every connection between stages creates a new loading, range or feedback question. State each one explicitly before calling the design complete.

<div className="takeaway"><span className="tiny-label">THE IDEA TO KEEP</span><p>A feedback element defines a function; <em>a budget defines an instrument</em>. Enumerate every error, refer it to one place, compare it with the specification, and check it at every extreme before connecting the last wire.</p></div>

</section>

<section id="precision-depth">

<div className="section-heading"><span>07</span><h2>Generate timed signals and drive differential converters</h2></div>

<h3>A programmable pulse width is a charge-balance calculation</h3>

<PulseTimingFigure/>

After a trigger, release the timing capacitor from reset and charge it with current $I_T$. When its voltage reaches $V_T$, a comparator resets the latch and ends the output pulse. Starting from $V_0$,

<Equation tex={String.raw`t_P=\frac{C_T(V_T-V_0)}{I_T}`}/>

For $C_T=10$ nF, $I_T=10$ µA and a programmable threshold 0.2–2.0 V above reset, pulse width is 0.2–2.0 ms. A 1 mV comparator offset gives 1 µs timing error, or 0.5% at the shortest pulse. A 20 pC reset-charge uncertainty gives 2 µs error. Comparator and latch delay add another term that does not scale with programmed pulse width. Define whether a trigger during the pulse is ignored or retriggers; the state machine must enforce that choice.

Ratiometric programming cancels reference drift when the same reference sets current and threshold: $I_T=V_R/R$ and $V_T-V_0=\alpha V_R$ give $t_P=\alpha RC_T$. If instead a control voltage sets charging current and the threshold is fixed, pulse width is inversely proportional to that control voltage. Name which quantity is programmed before claiming linear control. Reset must discharge the capacitor reproducibly and leave enough recovery time before the next trigger.

<h3>A single-supply VCO alternates equal current between two thresholds</h3>

Use an integrator, a comparator/latch and a polarity-selecting current path to alternate $+I$ and $-I$ through a timing capacitor. A single-supply implementation keeps the integrator's reference and capacitor voltage in valid ranges and uses threshold references rather than negative physical rails. The two transitions bound a triangle output; the latch gives the square output.

<Equation tex={String.raw`T=\frac{C\Delta V}{I_{up}}+\frac{C\Delta V}{I_{down}},\qquad I_{up}=I_{down}=\frac{V_C}{R}\ \Rightarrow\ f=\frac{V_C}{2RC\Delta V}`}/>

For $R=100$ kΩ, $C=10$ nF and $\Delta V=1$ V, the slope is 500 Hz/V. Control from 0.1 to 2 V gives 50–1000 Hz. If current generation and thresholds share a reference, frequency can depend on a voltage **ratio**, improving immunity to reference drift. But amplifier offset and bias current create a residual current: a 50 µV effective control offset is 0.05% at 100 mV, yet 5% at 1 mV. Near zero command the circuit can stall or drift rather than maintaining a perfect proportional frequency law.

Unequal up/down currents change duty cycle. With 10.1 and 9.9 µA, the two slopes differ by about 2%; total frequency changes much less because the reciprocal-current sum partly cancels the first-order mismatch. Threshold error, capacitance voltage coefficient, switch feedthrough and comparator delay still affect period and triangle amplitude. Budget the shortest half-cycle and all control-dependent internal swings.

<SwitchedVcoFigure/>

The switched-integrator implementation makes those equal currents explicitly. With $V_+=V_C/2$ and a 50 kΩ input resistor, the off-state input current is $V_C/(100\text{ k}\Omega)$. Turning on the grounded switch adds a 25 kΩ path, drawing $V_C/(50\text{ k}\Omega)$: twice the original current, so the **net** capacitor current reverses. For a steady control voltage, the output slopes are therefore $\pm V_C/(100\text{ k}\Omega\,C_T)$. With $V_R=3$ V, thresholds 1 and 2 V, and $C_T=10$ nF, the previous 500 Hz/V result follows. This version requires the op amp's inputs to work from near ground through 1 V as control ranges 0–2 V; its output ramps between 1 and 2 V. Those are separate range checks.

A 100 Ω on-resistance added to the 25 kΩ switch arm reduces its current by about 0.398%, changing the smaller difference current and hence ramp symmetry by roughly 0.8%. Control feedthrough and changing $V_C$ introduce additional transient terms; the frequency law assumes the control is approximately constant during a cycle. The Schmitt block must command Q on at the lower threshold and off at the upper threshold, with a defined initial latch state.

<h3>Through-hole and surface-mount versions need the same electrical tests</h3>

A through-hole prototype makes probing and replacement convenient; a compact surface-mount layout shortens high-frequency loops and reduces parasitic inductance. Neither package style guarantees good operation. Keep the timing capacitor and summing-node connection short, route comparator output edges away from the analog control input, decouple each IC locally, and keep return currents out of the reference path. Breadboard stray capacitance matters especially with small timing capacitors; an adapter changes pin pitch but retains its own capacitance and inductance. Keep probe pads on the ramp, thresholds, reference and square output so both implementations can be compared by frequency, duty cycle, amplitude, startup and supply sensitivity.

<h3>Zero-crossing detection has a slope-dependent timing error</h3>

A comparator observing a low-voltage sine can produce logic edges at each reference crossing. Its input common-mode range, overdrive recovery and output type must fit the circuit. An open-collector/open-drain output requires a pull-up; 10 kΩ with 20 pF gives a 200 ns RC time constant, which can dominate rising-edge timing even for a fast comparator. Push-pull logic-level outputs solve a different load problem.

An input error $V_E$ shifts the crossing by approximately $\Delta t=V_E/(2\pi fV_{pk})$. A 1 mV offset on a 1 V peak, 1 kHz sine shifts the edge by 159 ns. Hysteresis prevents chatter in noise but deliberately moves the rising and falling thresholds; 10 mV thresholds give about 1.59 µs shift near zero in that example. Comparator propagation delay and timing jitter add separately. This analysis assumes a safely scaled low-voltage input; any high-voltage source needs an appropriate isolated/scaled interface rather than a direct connection to the amplifier inputs.

<h3>Fully differential drive controls two independent output coordinates</h3>

<DifferentialDriverFigure/>

Define $v_{od}=v_{o+}-v_{o-}$ and $V_{OCM}=(v_{o+}+v_{o-})/2$. For the matched ideal voltage-feedback network shown, KCL at the two equal input-node voltages gives

<Equation tex={String.raw`v_{od}=\frac{R_F}{R_G}(v_2-v_1),\qquad v_{o+}=V_{OCM}+\frac{v_{od}}2,\qquad v_{o-}=V_{OCM}-\frac{v_{od}}2`}/>

With gain 2, differential input ±0.5 V and common-mode output 1.65 V, differential output is ±1 V and each output moves only between 1.15 and 2.15 V. Differential peak-to-peak swing is 2 V while each output's peak-to-peak swing is 1 V. The common-mode control loop sets their average; it has its own input range, output loading and dynamics. A two-op-amp follower/inverter can create a balanced pair but lacks the same integrated common-mode control and matching guarantees.

The FDA's **input** common-mode voltage is the voltage at its feedback input nodes, not automatically the source's average or the output common mode. For matched differential drive with $k=R_F/R_G$, the ideal value is $(kV_{in,CM}+V_{OCM})/(k+1)$. At $k=2$, a ground-centered source and 1.65 V output common mode put the amplifier inputs near 0.55 V. That may be outside an FDA whose valid input range begins at 1 V, even though both outputs look comfortably inside the rails. Single-ended drive, finite source resistance and termination change the node equations.

<h3>An ADC loads the driver dynamically</h3>

Many converters switch capacitors at their inputs, creating charge kickback. Equal series isolation resistors and a differential reservoir capacitor reduce the immediate disturbance and isolate the amplifier, but also impose settling time. With 33 Ω per side and 1 nF between ADC inputs, differential time constant is $2RC=66$ ns and the pole is about 2.41 MHz. A full-span step settling to half an LSB at 16 bits requires about $66\text{ ns}\times17\ln2=778$ ns in this RC-only model. An acquisition interval of 250 ns therefore cannot be justified from the sampling rate alone.

A 10 pC differential charge kick on 1 nF creates about 10 mV initial disturbance. Settling that to 15.3 µV, half an LSB for a 2 V span at 16 bits, takes about 428 ns in the same ideal RC model. The real driver adds its own recovery and stability constraints. A 5 nV/√Hz differential white-noise source integrated through this single pole has ENBW 3.79 MHz and roughly 9.73 µV RMS noise; resistor and amplifier noise must be added through their actual transfers. This charge-reservoir filter alone is not a sufficient anti-alias design for an arbitrary sampling rate.

Sharing supply rails can reduce some overdrive risks, but output transients, power sequencing and clamp currents still require checking. Some ADC common-mode reference outputs need buffering before driving an FDA's $V_{OCM}$ pin; verify permitted loading and bandwidth in both interfaces.

<h3>Balanced links and impedance matching need port-level gain definitions</h3>

For a 100 Ω differential cable, two 50 Ω source resistors provide a 100 Ω differential source termination. A driver producing 4 V peak-to-peak differentially delivers 2 V peak-to-peak into a matched 100 Ω receiver termination; peak differential load current is 10 mA. Include that factor of two in the gain specification. An unterminated short link has different gain and reflection behavior.

A single-ended source driving a feedback FDA does not necessarily see $R_G$ as its input resistance, because the input node is not fixed at ground. Choose termination so the **total** port impedance matches the source, include source/termination resistance in the two feedback-arm impedances, then rederive the gain. State whether gain refers to open-circuit source voltage, terminated input voltage or receiver voltage. Balanced amplitude alone also does not provide galvanic isolation: an ordinary FDA with low common-mode output impedance resists externally shifting both lines. Transformer coupling or a driver designed for high common-mode impedance can serve different grounding requirements, with their own bandwidth and overdrive limitations.

Select an FDA by supply and input range, per-output swing/current, differential noise and distortion, resistor/gain requirements, common-mode control range, capacitive-load stability, settling to the ADC's accuracy and package/layout constraints. Its differential and common-mode loops both matter.

<summary>Worked self-check: why can an ADC driver clip even though differential voltage is small?</summary>
<div>

A 1 V differential output centered at 3 V requires outputs at 3.5 and 2.5 V. On a 3.3 V supply the first is impossible. Conversely, moving common mode to 1.65 V gives 2.15 and 1.15 V, which may fit output swing but still does not establish the FDA's input common-mode range. Check the two coordinates and both amplifier/converter limits separately.

</div>

<section id="check">

<div className="section-heading"><span>08</span><h2>Check your understanding</h2></div>

<details>
<summary>1. What stops a mathematically correct analog function from being an accurate instrument?</summary>
<div>

Its elements only approximately obey their laws (junction saturation currents change with temperature, components have tolerances and drift), and every real stage adds offset, leakage, noise, range limits and settling time. Accuracy comes from a complete error budget, calibration where errors are repeatable, and checks at every extreme.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>2. Why does a log amplifier's accuracy fail at low currents?</summary>
<div>

Leakage currents (op-amp bias, protection diodes, board) add to the input current. At 10 nA, 1 nA of leakage is a 10% error in the measured quantity, about 2.5 mV in the logarithmic output. The log compresses the signal, but not the relative error.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>3. How does a matched reference transistor improve a log amplifier?</summary>
<div>

Subtracting the reference junction's voltage from the signal junction's gives $U_T\ln(I/I_{REF})$: the saturation current $I_S$, which roughly doubles every 10 °C, cancels if the two transistors are matched and at the same temperature. The remaining $U_T$ dependence is corrected with a temperature-dependent gain.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>4. In the Schmitt trigger, what sets the hysteresis?</summary>
<div>

The output swing times the attenuation of the positive-feedback divider: about $V_{swing}\,(R_1 \parallel R_2)/R_3$, 0.28 V with 10 kΩ, 10 kΩ and 82 kΩ on 5 V. Choose it larger than the input noise's peak-to-peak value.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>5. Why can a Wien-bridge oscillator not simply use a gain of exactly 3?</summary>
<div>

With $K$ exactly 3 the oscillation neither grows nor decays, so it would never start, and any tolerance would make it decay ($K < 3$) or grow until clipping ($K > 3$). A real oscillator starts above 3 and uses an amplitude-control loop to bring the effective gain to exactly 3 at the desired amplitude.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>6. What does "ratiometric" mean in the bridge chain, and what does it save?</summary>
<div>

The bridge's excitation and the ADC's reference are the same voltage. The bridge's output is proportional to the excitation, and the ADC measures relative to its reference, so excitation changes cancel in the digital result. The chain needs no precision voltage reference.

</div>
</details>
