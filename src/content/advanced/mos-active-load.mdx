<h1>Common-source stages with active loads<br/><em>Gain from two current sources fighting.</em></h1>

<p className="dek">Replace the drain resistor with a PMOS current source. Derive a gain that is independent of current, see on the load lines why the DC output becomes almost undefined, and trade the stage's large resistance between gain, bandwidth and bias sensitivity.</p>

<div className="chapter-meta"><span>50–70 min</span><span>Mirrors (Chs. 59–60) · Common source (Ch. 59)</span></div>

</div>

<div className="opening-grid">

<div><span className="tiny-label">PREDICT BEFORE CALCULATING</span><p className="opening-question">Two perfect current sources, one pulling up and one pulling down, meet at a node. What sets the voltage of that node?</p></div>

<div>

Chapter 59 found that a resistor-loaded stage's gain is $2V_{R_D}/u$, limited by how much supply voltage you can afford to drop across the resistor. The way out is a load whose **incremental** resistance is large while its **DC** voltage drop is small. A current source is exactly that: it passes its current with a fraction of a volt across it, yet resists changes like tens of kilohms.

With a PMOS current source as the load, a single transistor gives gains of 50 to 100, set by the devices' output resistances. That is the gain stage inside almost every CMOS op amp. But it raises a question that resistor loads never posed, the one above, and its answer decides how such stages must be used.

</div>

</div>

<div className="learning-map">

<span className="tiny-label">THE PATH THROUGH THIS CHAPTER</span><p>Two current laws meet at one node → Convert current imbalance into voltage → See it on the load lines → Hold bias fixed while changing AC loading → Close the DC and AC design loops → Check your understanding.</p>

</div>

<section id="model">

<div className="section-heading"><span>01</span><h2>Two current laws meet at one node</h2></div>

<ActiveLoadCircuit/>

$M_2$'s gate is held at a fixed bias $V_{BP}$ by the mirror, so it sources a nearly constant current into the output. $M_1$'s gate is the input, so it sinks a current that depends on $V_{in}$. KCL at the output demands that the two be equal.

Now the opening question. If both transistors were ideal current sources ($\lambda$ = 0), the PMOS would source exactly 1 mA at any output voltage in its compliance range, and the NMOS would sink a current fixed by $V_{in}$. If the two happen to be equal, **every** output voltage in the interval $u_N \le V_O \le V_{DD} - u_P$ satisfies KCL: the output is undefined. If they differ by any amount, **no** voltage in that interval works, and the output runs to whichever edge lets one device leave saturation and reduce its current.

With $\lambda > 0$, the currents depend slightly on $V_O$, and a unique intersection exists. But it is set by the tiny slopes $1/r_o$, so it is extraordinarily sensitive. With $|V_T|$ = 1 V and 1 V of overdrive on each device in a 5 V supply, the window is 1 V to 4 V.

</section>

<section id="derive">

<div className="section-heading"><span>02</span><h2>Convert current imbalance into voltage</h2></div>

For small signals the PMOS gate and both supplies are AC ground, so the load contributes only its output conductance $g_{oP}$. KCL at the output, with an optional AC-coupled load $R_L$:

<Equation tex={String.raw`g_{mN}v_i+\left(g_{oN}+g_{oP}+\frac1{R_L}\right)v_o=0\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad A_v=-g_{mN}\,(r_{oN}\parallel r_{oP}\parallel R_L)`}/>

Unloaded, substitute $g_m = 2I_D/u$ and $g_o = \lambda I_D$:

<Equation tex={String.raw`|A_v|=\frac{g_{mN}}{g_{oN}+g_{oP}}=\frac{2I_D/u_N}{(\lambda_N+\lambda_P)I_D}=\frac{2}{(\lambda_N+\lambda_P)\,u_N}`} label="ACTIVE-LOAD GAIN IS INDEPENDENT OF CURRENT"/>

The current cancels. Doubling the bias current doubles $g_m$ but halves both $r_o$, leaving the gain unchanged. The gain depends only on the overdrive and on $\lambda$, which falls with channel length. With $\lambda$ = 0.02 V⁻¹ for both devices and $u$ = 1 V, $|A_v|$ = 50. Halve the overdrive, or double both channel lengths, and it becomes 100. Current buys speed, not gain.

The same equation, read as DC, gives the sensitivity. If the PMOS source would deliver $\Delta I$ more than the NMOS would sink at the same output voltage, KCL at the output node demands that the excess flow somewhere. The only paths are the two output conductances, so the output moves until $\Delta I = -(g_{oN} + g_{oP})\Delta V_O$. A current mismatch $\Delta I$ between the two devices therefore moves the output by

<Equation tex={String.raw`\Delta V_O\approx-\Delta I\,(r_{oN}\parallel r_{oP})`}/>

until one device leaves saturation. The large resistance that makes the gain also magnifies every bias error.

</section>

<section id="loadlines">

<div className="section-heading"><span>03</span><h2>See it on the load lines</h2></div>

<ActiveLoadLineFigure/>

Compare this with the resistor load line of Chapter 52, a steep straight line crossing the transistor's flat curves at a well-defined angle. Here both curves are nearly flat, so they cross at a shallow angle, and moving either one slightly slides the intersection a long way. Each 10 mV of gate voltage raises the NMOS curve by $g_m × 10$ mV = 20 µA; the intersection must move until the slopes make up 20 µA, which takes $20\text{ µA} × 25\text{ kΩ}$ = 0.5 V.

<ActiveTransferFigure/>

<div className="worked-example">

<span className="tiny-label">WORKED EXAMPLE / HOW SENSITIVE IS THE DC OUTPUT?</span><h3>1 mA, $g_m$ = 2 mS, $r_{oN} = r_{oP}$ = 50 kΩ</h3>

<p>The output resistance is 25 kΩ, so a 20 µA mismatch between the NMOS and PMOS currents moves the output 0.5 V. Referred to the input, that is a gate error of 20 µA / 2 mS = 10 mV. A 10 mV threshold mismatch, or a 2% error in the PMOS mirror ratio, shifts the output half a volt.</p>

<p>An 80 µA error (8%) would demand a 2 V shift. From a 2.5 V quiescent point that pushes the output to 0.5 V, below the NMOS compliance of 1 V. $M_1$ enters triode and the "amplifier" is stuck at one rail. A single active-loaded stage cannot hold its own DC operating point to useful accuracy. Something else must, and in practice that something is negative feedback around the whole amplifier.</p>

</div>

<div className="margin-note">

**The answer, precisely.** Two ideal current sources do not define a node voltage. Real ones do, through their output resistances, but only weakly: the node voltage is set by a ratio of two small slopes and moves by volts for a microamp-level imbalance. High-gain nodes in CMOS op amps are always placed inside a feedback loop that measures the output and adjusts the input until KCL is satisfied at the right voltage.

</div>

</section>

<section id="cases">

<div className="section-heading"><span>04</span><h2>Hold bias fixed while changing AC loading</h2></div>

Keep $I_D$ = 1 mA, $g_{mN}$ = 2 mS and $r_{oN} = r_{oP}$ = 50 kΩ, put 10 pF of total capacitance on the output, and add three AC-coupled loads:

<MosCases kind="active"/>

Unloaded, the node resistance is 25 kΩ and the gain −50. A 100 kΩ load reduces it to −40 and a 10 kΩ load to −14.3. Meanwhile the output pole, $1/(2\pi R_{out}C)$, rises from 637 kHz to 2.2 MHz. Loading appears to speed the stage up, but only by spending gain:

<ActiveGbwFigure/>

All three curves share one asymptote and cross unity gain at the same frequency:

<Equation tex={String.raw`\text{GBW}=|A_v|\,f_p=g_mR_{out}\cdot\frac{1}{2\pi R_{out}C}=\frac{g_m}{2\pi C}`} label="GAIN–BANDWIDTH PRODUCT OF A SINGLE NODE"/>

At 2 mS and 10 pF that is 32 MHz. The only ways to raise it are more $g_m$ (more current or smaller overdrive) or less capacitance. This single equation drives op-amp design: Chapter 76 uses it to set the unity-gain frequency of a two-stage CMOS amplifier with a deliberately added compensation capacitor.

<div className="worked-example">

<span className="tiny-label">WORKED EXAMPLE / SWING AND INPUT RANGE</span><h3>Gain −50 in a 1–4 V output window</h3>

<p>From a 2.5 V quiescent output the stage can swing about ±1.5 V before either device leaves saturation. At a gain of 50 that corresponds to only <strong>±30 mV</strong> at the input. The stage is a precision amplifier for small signals around one exact operating point; it is not a general-purpose gain block.</p>

<p>If each device's $\lambda$ doubles (shorter channels) at the same current, each $r_o$ halves: gain −25 and the unloaded pole doubles to 1.3 MHz. That is a change of device, not something the gate voltage can adjust.</p>

</div>

</section>

<section id="limits">

<div className="section-heading"><span>05</span><h2>Close the DC and AC design loops</h2></div>

**Noise and errors from the load.** The "fixed" PMOS gate is only as quiet as the mirror that drives it. Noise on $V_{BP}$ appears at the output multiplied by $g_{mP}R_{out}$, the same gain as the input. The load device's current noise and mismatch join the input device's errors on equal terms. An active load roughly doubles the input-referred noise compared with an ideal noiseless load.

**Measurement.** A 10 MΩ, 10 pF oscilloscope probe barely changes the 25 kΩ resistance but adds as much capacitance as the circuit itself, halving the bandwidth you are trying to measure. A load connected to ground at DC draws current and moves the operating point, as the worked example showed.

**Design flow.** Choose the DC stabilization before cascading the stage. In a two-stage op amp (Chapter 70), overall feedback sets the output; the intermediate high-gain node stays in its window only because the loop forces the input difference to near zero. Verify the low-frequency loop polarity first, then the compensation.

<div className="takeaway"><span className="tiny-label">THE IDEA TO KEEP</span><p>An active load makes the gain $2/(\lambda_N + \lambda_P)u$, independent of current, but leaves the DC output resting on the ratio of two tiny slopes. <em>High-gain nodes live inside feedback loops.</em></p></div>

</section>

<section id="check">

<div className="section-heading"><span>06</span><h2>Check your understanding</h2></div>

<details>
<summary>1. Why does a PMOS current-source load give more gain than a drain resistor with the same DC drop?</summary>
<div>

A resistor's incremental resistance equals its DC voltage divided by its current, so 2 V at 1 mA is 2 kΩ. A saturated PMOS passes 1 mA with perhaps 1–2 V across it, yet its incremental resistance is $r_o$ ≈ 50 kΩ, because its current barely changes with voltage. The gain is $g_m$ times that much larger resistance.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>2. Why is the active-loaded gain independent of the bias current?</summary>
<div>

$g_m = 2I_D/u$ grows in proportion to current, and $r_o = 1/(\lambda I_D)$ shrinks in proportion to current. Their product is $2/(\lambda u)$, with the current cancelled. More current makes the stage faster (higher $g_m/C$) but not higher-gain.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>3. What determines the DC output if both devices were ideal current sources?</summary>
<div>

Nothing. If the commanded currents were exactly equal, any voltage in the compliance window would satisfy KCL; if they differed at all, none would, and the output would slam to whichever edge lets one device leave saturation. Real output resistance, or an external feedback loop, selects the point.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>4. What limits the output from above?</summary>
<div>

The PMOS needs $V_{SD} \ge u_P$, so $V_O \le V_{DD} - u_P$, 4 V in the example. Above that it enters triode, its current falls, and it is no longer a high-resistance load.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>5. A 10 kΩ load raises the output pole from 637 kHz to 2.2 MHz. Has the stage become faster?</summary>
<div>

Only in the sense that its corner frequency moved up; its gain fell from 50 to 14.3 by the same factor. The gain–bandwidth product $g_m/2\pi C$ is unchanged at 32 MHz. For any given closed-loop gain in a feedback circuit, the useful bandwidth is the same.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>6. Does noise on the PMOS bias voltage reach the output?</summary>
<div>

Yes, with the same gain as the input signal: a voltage on $V_{BP}$ changes the PMOS current by $g_{mP}v_{bp}$, and that current flows into the 25 kΩ output node. The bias line must be filtered, or derived so that its noise is common to both sides of a differential stage, where it cancels (Chapter 67).

</div>
</details>
