<div className="opening-grid">

<div><span className="tiny-label">PREDICT BEFORE CALCULATING</span><p className="opening-question">In an equal-component Sallen–Key filter, why does raising the amplifier's gain make the resonance sharper?</p></div>

<div>

Chapter 23 found that cascading RC sections never gives a sharp cutoff: real poles on the negative axis all have $Q \le 0.5$, and their corners blur together. A sharp filter needs complex poles, which passive circuits get from inductors. At audio frequencies inductors are large, lossy and pick up hum.

An op amp can supply the missing ingredient. Feed part of its output back through a capacitor to the middle of an RC chain, and the poles move off the real axis to wherever the designer puts them. The Sallen–Key circuit does this with one op amp, two resistors and two capacitors. Cascade such sections and any classical filter (Butterworth, Bessel, Chebyshev) can be built from a table of section values.

</div>

</div>

<div className="learning-map">

<span className="tiny-label">THE PATH THROUGH THIS CHAPTER</span><p>Name the capacitors by their actual connections → Identify natural frequency and damping → Compare three damping choices → Choose a filter family → Design a unity-gain section and a cascade → Design against the specification → Check your understanding.</p>

</div>

<section id="model">

<div className="section-heading"><span>01</span><h2>Name the capacitors by their actual connections</h2></div>

<OpAmpFigure mode="filter"/>

$R_1$ and $R_2$ form the input chain. $C_1$ connects the junction $V_X$ between them to the **output**; $C_2$ connects the plus-input node $V_Y$ to **ground**. The op amp is a noninverting amplifier with gain $K = 1 + R_F/R_G$, so $V_O = KV_Y$. Textbooks label these capacitors differently; what matters is which one goes to the output.

Write KCL at the two internal nodes:

<Equation tex={String.raw`\frac{V_Y-V_X}{R_2}+sC_2V_Y=0,\qquad\frac{V_X-V_I}{R_1}+\frac{V_X-V_Y}{R_2}+sC_1(V_X-V_O)=0`}/>

and eliminate $V_X$ and $V_Y$. Two RC sections do not simply multiply: $C_1$ returns a signal from the output, and that feedback is what moves the poles.

</section>

<section id="derive">

<div className="section-heading"><span>02</span><h2>Identify natural frequency and damping</h2></div>

<Equation tex={String.raw`H(s)=\frac{K}{1+bs+as^2},\qquad a=R_1R_2C_1C_2,\qquad b=C_2(R_1+R_2)+C_1R_1(1-K)`} label="SALLEN–KEY LOW-PASS"/>

<Equation tex={String.raw`f_0=\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt a},\qquad Q=\frac{\sqrt a}{b}`}/>

Now the opening question. The damping coefficient $b$ contains $C_1R_1(1 - K)$: the larger the gain, the more $C_1$'s feedback cancels the RC chain's natural damping. With equal resistors and capacitors,

<Equation tex={String.raw`f_0=\frac{1}{2\pi RC},\qquad Q=\frac{1}{3-K}`}/>

$K$ = 1 gives $Q$ = 0.5, no better than passive RC; $K$ = 1.586 gives the Butterworth $Q$ of 0.707; $K$ = 2 gives 1; at $K$ = 3 the damping vanishes and the circuit oscillates. More gain means more positive feedback through $C_1$ near $f_0$, a sharper resonance.

</section>

<section id="cases">

<div className="section-heading"><span>03</span><h2>Compare three damping choices</h2></div>

<div className="worked-example">

<span className="tiny-label">WORKED EXAMPLE / ONE SECTION, THREE GAINS</span><h3>10 kΩ and 10 nF throughout, so $f_0$ = 1591.5 Hz</h3>

<p>At $K$ = 1, 1.586 and 2.5, $Q$ = 0.5, 0.707 and 2.0. At $f_0$ the gain is $KQ$: 0.5, 1.12 and 5.0, which relative to each section's DC gain is −6.0, −3.0 and +6.0 dB. Only the Butterworth section has its −3 dB point at $f_0$; the $Q$ = 2 section peaks 6 dB above its passband.</p>

<p><strong>Sensitivity.</strong> $dQ/dK = 1/(3 - K)^2$. At $K$ = 2.5 that is 4: a gain error of +0.01 raises $Q$ by 0.04, 2%. At $K$ = 2.9, where $Q$ = 10, the same error raises $Q$ by 1, 10%. The resistor ratio $R_F/R_G$ that sets $K$ must be held correspondingly tightly.</p>

</div>

</section>

<section id="families">

<div className="section-heading"><span>04</span><h2>Choose a filter family</h2></div>

A filter of order $n$ has $n$ poles; each Sallen–Key section supplies two. Where they go is decided by what the application values:

<FilterFamiliesFigure/>

<FilterStepFigure/>

| Family (4th order) | Section 1: $f/f_c$, $Q$ | Section 2: $f/f_c$, $Q$ | At $2f_c$ | Step overshoot |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Butterworth | 1.000, 0.541 | 1.000, 1.307 | −24 dB | 11% |
| Bessel | 1.419, 0.522 | 1.591, 0.806 | −14 dB | 1% |
| Chebyshev, 1 dB | 0.529, 0.785 | 0.993, 3.559 | −33 dB | 22% |

**Butterworth** is the default: maximally flat passband, moderate edge. **Bessel** gives up the edge for a nearly constant group delay, so pulses keep their shape: the choice before an ADC sampling pulses, or in an oscilloscope. **Chebyshev** trades passband ripple for steepness: the choice when a strong signal sits just outside the band. Each section needs a different $f_0$ and $Q$, which is the whole point: two identical Butterworth sections cascaded do not make a fourth-order Butterworth filter, they make a filter that is 6 dB down at $f_c$.

</section>

<section id="design">

<div className="section-heading"><span>05</span><h2>Design a unity-gain section and a cascade</h2></div>

The equal-component design sets $Q$ with the gain, which makes $Q$ sensitive to resistor ratios. The **unity-gain** design uses a follower ($K$ = 1, exact) and sets $Q$ with the capacitor ratio instead. With $R_1 = R_2 = R$:

<Equation tex={String.raw`f_0=\frac{1}{2\pi R\sqrt{C_1C_2}},\qquad Q=\frac12\sqrt{\frac{C_1}{C_2}}\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad\frac{C_1}{C_2}=4Q^2`}/>

<UnitySkCircuit/>

<div className="worked-example">

<span className="tiny-label">WORKED EXAMPLE / A 4TH-ORDER BUTTERWORTH AT 1 kHz</span><h3>Two unity-gain sections, $R$ = 10 kΩ</h3>

<p><strong>Section 1</strong>, $Q$ = 0.541: $C_1/C_2 = 4(0.541)^2$ = 1.17, and $\sqrt{C_1C_2} = 1/(2\pi × 10\text{ kΩ} × 1\text{ kHz})$ = 15.9 nF. So $C_2$ = 14.7 nF, $C_1$ = 17.2 nF.</p>

<p><strong>Section 2</strong>, $Q$ = 1.307: $C_1/C_2$ = 6.83, so $C_2$ = 6.09 nF, $C_1$ = 41.6 nF.</p>

<p><strong>Order.</strong> Put the low-Q section first. The high-Q section peaks by about 3 dB internally near 1 kHz; if it came first, a large input near 1 kHz could clip it before the second section's attenuation helped.</p>

<p><strong>Tolerance.</strong> With 1% capacitors, $Q$ (which depends on $\sqrt{C_1/C_2}$) varies by about ±1% and $f_0$ by about ±1%; the passband edge moves by a fraction of a decibel. A Chebyshev design's $Q$ = 3.56 section would be far more sensitive.</p>

</div>

</section>

<section id="limits">

<div className="section-heading"><span>06</span><h2>Design against the specification, not one cutoff</h2></div>

Start from the specification: passband ripple, stopband attenuation, the frequencies of both, and any phase or step-response requirement. Choose the family and order that meet it, then realize each pole pair as a section, and check gain distribution so no internal node clips.

**Source and load.** The source's resistance adds to $R_1$ unless the source is buffered; a heavy load on the output can disturb the op amp. **The op amp.** It needs loop gain at the filter's frequencies and enough slew rate for the largest internal signal. A Sallen–Key low-pass also has a known flaw: at very high frequency the op amp's output impedance rises and signal feeds through $C_1$ directly to the output, so the stopband attenuation stops improving at some tens of decibels; a small RC after the filter or a multiple-feedback topology avoids it.

**Three different frequencies.** A section's natural frequency, its −3 dB frequency and its equivalent noise bandwidth are different numbers unless $Q$ = 0.707. Use the right one for each claim.

<div className="takeaway"><span className="tiny-label">THE IDEA TO KEEP</span><p>A Sallen–Key section places a complex pole pair at $f_0 = 1/2\pi\sqrt{R_1R_2C_1C_2}$ with a $Q$ set by gain or by $C_1/C_2$. <em>Cascade sections with different $f_0$ and $Q$</em>, taken from the family's table, to build any classical filter.</p></div>

</section>

<section id="check">

<div className="section-heading"><span>07</span><h2>Check your understanding</h2></div>

<details>
<summary>1. Why does more gain make an equal-component Sallen–Key resonance sharper?</summary>
<div>

$C_1$ returns $K$ times the plus-input signal to the middle of the RC chain. That positive feedback cancels part of the chain's own damping, $b = RC(3 - K)$. As $K$ approaches 3, the damping vanishes and $Q = 1/(3 - K)$ grows without limit.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>2. Is a unity-gain equal-component Sallen–Key section a Butterworth filter?</summary>
<div>

No. With $K$ = 1 and equal parts, $Q$ = 0.5, which is critically damped and 6 dB down at $f_0$. Butterworth needs $Q$ = 0.707: either $K$ = 1.586 with equal parts, or unity gain with $C_1 = 2C_2$.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>3. Why are two identical Butterworth sections not a fourth-order Butterworth filter?</summary>
<div>

A fourth-order Butterworth needs its four poles spread on a circle, which means two sections with the same $f_0$ but $Q$ values of 0.541 and 1.307. Two $Q$ = 0.707 sections give $(−3\text{ dB})^2$ = −6 dB at $f_c$ and a less flat passband.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>4. When would you choose a Bessel filter over a Butterworth?</summary>
<div>

When the waveform's shape matters, for pulses, step responses or data. The Bessel filter's group delay is nearly constant through the passband, so it overshoots less than 1%, at the cost of a much gentler cutoff.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>5. For a unity-gain section with $Q$ = 2, what capacitor ratio is needed?</summary>
<div>

$C_1/C_2 = 4Q^2$ = **16**. High-Q unity-gain sections need large capacitor spreads; at some point a different topology (multiple feedback, state variable) is more practical.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>6. In a cascade, why put the low-Q section first?</summary>
<div>

High-Q sections have internal gain peaks near their natural frequency. Placing them after the low-Q sections means the signal has already been partly filtered, so a large out-of-band component cannot clip the high-Q section's op amp.

</div>
</details>
