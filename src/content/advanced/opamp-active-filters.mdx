<div><span className="tiny-label">PREDICT BEFORE CALCULATING</span><p className="opening-question">In an equal-component Sallen–Key filter, why does raising the amplifier's gain make the resonance sharper?</p></div>

<div>

Chapter 16 found that cascading RC sections never gives a sharp cutoff: real poles on the negative axis all have $Q \le 0.5$, and their corners blur together. A sharp filter needs complex poles, which passive circuits get from inductors. At audio frequencies inductors are large, lossy and pick up hum.

An op amp can supply the missing ingredient. Feed part of its output back through a capacitor to the middle of an RC chain, and the poles move off the real axis to wherever the designer puts them. The Sallen–Key circuit does this with one op amp, two resistors and two capacitors. Cascade such sections and any classical filter (Butterworth, Bessel, Chebyshev) can be built from a table of section values.

</div>

</div>

<section id="model">

<div className="section-heading"><span>01</span><h2>Name the capacitors by their actual connections</h2></div>

<OpAmpFigure mode="filter"/>

$R_1$ and $R_2$ form the input chain. $C_1$ connects the junction $V_X$ between them to the **output**; $C_2$ connects the plus-input node $V_Y$ to **ground**. The op amp is a noninverting amplifier with gain $K = 1 + R_F/R_G$, so $V_O = KV_Y$. Textbooks label these capacitors differently; what matters is which one goes to the output.

Write KCL at the two internal nodes:

<Equation tex={String.raw`\frac{V_Y-V_X}{R_2}+sC_2V_Y=0,\qquad\frac{V_X-V_I}{R_1}+\frac{V_X-V_Y}{R_2}+sC_1(V_X-V_O)=0`}/>

The second node is the easy one. Its only current paths are $R_2$ and $C_2$ because the ideal plus input takes no current. Rearranging gives

<Equation tex={String.raw`V_X=(1+sR_2C_2)V_Y,\qquad V_X-V_O=(1-K+sR_2C_2)V_Y`}/>

Now solve the first-node equation for the input. This makes each current's contribution to the required input voltage visible:

<Equation tex={String.raw`\begin{aligned}V_I&=V_X+R_1\left[\frac{V_X-V_Y}{R_2}+sC_1(V_X-V_O)\right]\\&=(1+sR_2C_2)V_Y+sR_1C_2V_Y+sR_1C_1(1-K+sR_2C_2)V_Y\\&=\left[1+s\{C_2(R_1+R_2)+C_1R_1(1-K)\}+s^2R_1R_2C_1C_2\right]V_Y.\end{aligned}`}/>

Finally multiply $V_Y/V_I$ by $K$. The constant term is dimensionless, the coefficient of $s$ has units of seconds, and the coefficient of $s^2$ has units of seconds squared. Those checks catch missing resistors and capacitors. This derivation assumes a zero-impedance voltage source, an ideal amplifier with constant gain, and zero initial capacitor voltages for the transfer function; initial energy reappears in the transient solution.

</section>

<section id="derive">

<div className="section-heading"><span>02</span><h2>Identify natural frequency and damping</h2></div>

<Equation tex={String.raw`H(s)=\frac{K}{1+bs+as^2},\qquad a=R_1R_2C_1C_2,\qquad b=C_2(R_1+R_2)+C_1R_1(1-K)`} label="SALLEN–KEY LOW-PASS"/>

Compare with the standard form $1 + s/(\omega_0Q) + s^2/\omega_0^2$: matching the $s^2$ coefficients gives $1/\omega_0^2 = a$, and matching the $s$ coefficients gives $1/(\omega_0Q) = b$, so $Q = 1/(\omega_0b) = \sqrt a/b$:

<Equation tex={String.raw`f_0=\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt a},\qquad Q=\frac{\sqrt a}{b}`}/>

Now the opening question. The damping coefficient $b$ contains $C_1R_1(1 - K)$: the larger the gain, the more $C_1$'s feedback cancels the RC chain's natural damping. With equal resistors and capacitors, $a = R^2C^2$ and $b = 2RC + RC(1-K) = (3-K)RC$, so

<Equation tex={String.raw`f_0=\frac{1}{2\pi RC},\qquad Q=\frac{1}{3-K}`}/>

At $K=1$, equal components give $Q=0.5$. At $K=3-\sqrt2\approx1.586$, they give the second-order Butterworth $Q=1/\sqrt2$. At $K=2$, $Q=1$; at $K=2.5$, $Q=2$. At exactly $K=3$, the ideal model is **undamped**, with poles on the imaginary axis. Initial energy persists and resonant forcing has no bounded steady state. It does not spontaneously create a sinusoid from zero energy. Above 3 the poles move into the right half-plane and disturbances grow; a real circuit then encounters clipping and other nonlinearities.

<h3>Read damping directly from the differential equation</h3>

<Equation tex={String.raw`a\frac{d^2V_O}{dt^2}+b\frac{dV_O}{dt}+V_O=KV_I,\qquad s_{1,2}=-\frac{\omega_0}{2Q}\pm\omega_0\sqrt{\frac{1}{4Q^2}-1}`}/>

The coefficient $a$ sets the time scale; $b$ removes stored energy. For positive parts, stability requires $b>0$, or $K<1+C_2(R_1+R_2)/(C_1R_1)$. Below $Q=0.5$ there are two distinct real poles. At 0.5 they coincide. Above 0.5 they form a complex pair, but a frequency-response peak appears only above $Q=1/\sqrt2$. Complex poles, step overshoot and passband peaking are related but have different thresholds.

<h3>Why the output-connected capacitor changes everything</h3>

At low frequency with $K=1$, the output follows $V_Y$, which nearly follows $V_X$. Both ends of $C_1$ move together: **bootstrapping** reduces its voltage and its current. In fact $I_{C1}=sC_1(V_X-V_O)=s^2R_2C_1C_2V_Y$. Its leading contribution is second order in frequency. Grounding that capacitor instead would add an ordinary first-order loading current.

<SallenComparisonFigure/>

For equal parts, two unbuffered passive RC sections have denominator $1+3sRC+(sRC)^2$ and $Q=1/3$. Two separately buffered RC sections have $(1+sRC)^2$ and $Q=1/2$. The unity-gain equal-component Sallen–Key matches the **buffered** result with one amplifier. It already removes the extra interstage-loading term; gain above unity cancels further damping. The op amp supplies energy through $C_1$, allowing a sharp response without a physical inductor.

At sufficiently high frequency the output tends toward zero, so $C_1$ behaves approximately like a capacitor to AC ground and the two-capacitor network gives a −40 dB/decade asymptote. This statement applies while the op amp still behaves as the low-impedance controlled source assumed in the derivation.

<h3>A phasor check at the natural frequency</h3>

For equal parts at $\omega_0RC=1$, choose $V_Y$ as the phasor reference. Then

<Equation tex={String.raw`V_X=(1+j)V_Y,\quad I_{R2}=j\frac{V_Y}{R},\quad I_{C1}=[-1+j(1-K)]\frac{V_Y}{R},\quad V_I=j(3-K)V_Y`}/>

Thus $V_O/V_I=-jKQ$: the output lags the input by 90° and its magnitude is $KQ$. The feedback capacitor's current contains both real and imaginary parts. Calling the path “positive feedback” describes its effect on damping; it does not mean every node voltage and current is in phase with the input. With $K=2.5$ and a 100 mV peak input, $V_Y$ is 200 mV peak, $V_X$ is 283 mV peak and the output is 500 mV peak. Check **all** of those excursions against common-mode and output limits.

</section>

<div className="section-heading"><span>03</span><h2>Compare three damping choices</h2></div>

<div className="worked-example">

<span className="tiny-label">WORKED EXAMPLE / ONE SECTION, THREE GAINS</span><h3>10 kΩ and 10 nF throughout, so $f_0$ = 1591.5 Hz</h3>

<p>At $K$ = 1, 1.586 and 2.5, $Q$ = 0.5, 0.707 and 2.0. At $f_0$ the gain is $KQ$: 0.5, 1.12 and 5.0, which relative to each section's DC gain is −6.0, −3.0 and +6.0 dB. Only the Butterworth section has its −3 dB point at $f_0$; the $Q$ = 2 section is 6.02 dB above its passband at $f_0$ and peaks slightly earlier, at $0.935f_0$, by 6.30 dB.</p>

<p><strong>Sensitivity.</strong> $dQ/dK = 1/(3 - K)^2$. At $K$ = 2.5 that is 4: a gain error of +0.01 raises $Q$ by approximately 0.04, or 2%; the exact result is $1/0.49=2.0408$. At $K$ = 2.9, where $Q$ = 10, the differential estimate is +1, but the exact new value is $1/0.09=11.111$, an 11.1% increase. Small-error linearization becomes less reliable as the stability boundary approaches. The resistor ratio $R_F/R_G$ that sets $K$ must be held correspondingly tightly.</p>

</div>

<h3>Translate the frequency response into what a pulse does</h3>

<SallenStepFigure/>

For $Q>0.5$, a unit step produces a decaying sinusoid around its final value. Its envelope decays at $\alpha=\omega_0/(2Q)$, and its damped ringing frequency is $\omega_d=\omega_0\sqrt{1-1/(4Q^2)}$. The fractional first overshoot is $\exp[-\pi/\sqrt{4Q^2-1}]$. At 1591.5 Hz, the $Q=2$ section rings near 1541 Hz, overshoots by **44.4%**, and has a 400 µs envelope time constant. A 1 V input step eventually gives 2.5 V at $K=2.5$, but its first peak requests 3.61 V. Output range must include that transient. The second-order Butterworth overshoots by 4.32%; “flat amplitude response” does not mean “no overshoot.”

<section id="families">

<div className="section-heading"><span>04</span><h2>Choose a filter family</h2></div>

A filter of order $n$ has $n$ poles; each Sallen–Key section supplies two. Where they go is decided by what the application values:

<FilterFamiliesFigure/>

<FilterStepFigure/>

| Family (4th order) | Section 1: $f/f_c$, $Q$ | Section 2: $f/f_c$, $Q$ | At $2f_c$ | Step overshoot |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Butterworth | 1.000, 0.541 | 1.000, 1.307 | −24 dB | 11% |
| Bessel | 1.419, 0.522 | 1.591, 0.806 | −14 dB | 1% |
| Chebyshev, 1 dB | 0.529, 0.785 | 0.993, 3.559 | −33 dB | 22% |

**Butterworth** is the default: maximally flat passband, moderate edge. **Bessel** gives up the edge for a nearly constant group delay, so pulses keep their shape: the choice before an ADC sampling pulses, or in an oscilloscope. **Chebyshev** trades passband ripple for steepness: the choice when a strong signal sits just outside the band. Each section needs a different $f_0$ and $Q$, which is the whole point: two identical Butterworth sections cascaded do not make a fourth-order Butterworth filter, they make a filter that is 6 dB down at $f_c$.

</section>

<section id="design">

<div className="section-heading"><span>05</span><h2>Design a unity-gain section and a cascade</h2></div>

The equal-component design sets $Q$ with the gain, which makes $Q$ sensitive to resistor ratios. The **unity-gain** design uses a follower ($K$ = 1, exact) and sets $Q$ with the capacitor ratio instead. With $R_1 = R_2 = R$ and $K = 1$, the general coefficients become $a = R^2C_1C_2$ and $b = 2RC_2$ (the $C_1$ term vanishes). Then $Q = \sqrt a/b = RC_1^{1/2}C_2^{1/2}/(2RC_2) = \tfrac12\sqrt{C_1/C_2}$:

<Equation tex={String.raw`f_0=\frac{1}{2\pi R\sqrt{C_1C_2}},\qquad Q=\frac12\sqrt{\frac{C_1}{C_2}}\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad\frac{C_1}{C_2}=4Q^2`}/>

<UnitySkCircuit/>

<div className="worked-example">

<span className="tiny-label">WORKED EXAMPLE / A 4TH-ORDER BUTTERWORTH AT 1 kHz</span><h3>Two unity-gain sections, $R$ = 10 kΩ</h3>

<p><strong>Section 1</strong>, $Q$ = 0.541: $C_1/C_2 = 4(0.541)^2$ = 1.17, and $\sqrt{C_1C_2} = 1/(2\pi × 10\text{ kΩ} × 1\text{ kHz})$ = 15.9 nF. So $C_2$ = 14.7 nF, $C_1$ = 17.2 nF.</p>

<p><strong>Section 2</strong>, $Q$ = 1.307: $C_1/C_2$ = 6.83, so $C_2$ = 6.09 nF, $C_1$ = 41.6 nF.</p>

<p><strong>Order.</strong> Put the low-Q section first. The high-Q section peaks by about 3 dB internally near 1 kHz; if it came first, a large input near 1 kHz could clip it before the second section's attenuation helped.</p>

<p><strong>Tolerance.</strong> With 1% capacitors, $Q$ (which depends on $\sqrt{C_1/C_2}$) varies by about ±1% and $f_0$ by about ±1%; the passband edge moves by a fraction of a decibel. A Chebyshev design's $Q$ = 3.56 section would be far more sensitive.</p>

</div>



<h3 id="book-notes">A state-variable filter exposes the states and controls damping separately</h3>

A state-variable realization combines a summer with two integrators. In one explicit sign convention, let h, b and l be high-pass, band-pass and low-pass outputs, and u the input:

<Equation tex={String.raw`\dot b=\omega_0 h,\qquad\dot l=\omega_0 b,\qquad h=u-\frac bQ-l`}/>

The integrators can be built from the inverting integrators in Chapter 83 with the required sign inversions in the summing paths. Eliminating the internal states gives a common denominator $D(s)=s^2+(\omega_0/Q)s+\omega_0^2$ and three transfers:

<Equation tex={String.raw`\begin{aligned}H_{HP}&=\frac{s^2}{D}\\H_{BP}&=\frac{\omega_0s}{D}\\H_{LP}&=\frac{\omega_0^2}{D}\\H_{notch}&=H_{HP}+H_{LP}\end{aligned}`}/>

<StateVariableExtensionFigure/>

At $f_0=1$ kHz and Q = 2, the two integrator scale factors correspond to $RC=159.15$ µs; 10 nF and about 15.9 kΩ give that scale. The damping coefficient in the summer is $1/Q=0.5$. At the center frequency, all three state magnitudes are 2, while the high-pass and low-pass states are opposite in phase and ideally sum to zero at the notch output. For a unit-peak input, internal states can therefore need **2 V peak**, even where the externally selected notch output is tiny.

Changing damping without changing both integrator time constants makes Q easier to adjust independently than in some Sallen–Key arrangements. The cost is more active devices, noise paths, internal swing and sensitivity to finite integrator gain. A 1% relative amplitude mismatch between the two center-frequency notch contributions leaves about 0.02 of input amplitude in this Q = 2 example, roughly **34 dB** rejection rather than an infinite notch. Select topology by tuning, internal range and component sensitivity as well as the final transfer function.

</section>

<section id="limits">

<div className="section-heading"><span>06</span><h2>Design against the specification, not one cutoff</h2></div>

Start from the specification: passband ripple, stopband attenuation, the frequencies of both, and any phase or step-response requirement. Choose the family and order that meet it, then realize each pole pair as a section, and check gain distribution so no internal node clips.

**Source and load.** The source's resistance adds to $R_1$ unless the source is buffered; a heavy load on the output can disturb the op amp. **The op amp.** It needs loop gain at the filter's frequencies and enough slew rate for the largest internal signal. A Sallen–Key low-pass also has a known flaw: at very high frequency the op amp's output impedance rises and signal feeds through $C_1$ directly to the output, so the stopband attenuation stops improving at some tens of decibels; a small RC after the filter or a multiple-feedback topology avoids it.

<h3>Natural frequency, cutoff, peak and noise bandwidth</h3>

Let $x=f/f_0$. Dividing the magnitude by its DC gain gives $1/\sqrt{(1-x^2)^2+x^2/Q^2}$. Set its squared denominator to 2 to find the −3 dB frequency relative to DC:

<Equation tex={String.raw`\left(\frac{f_{-3}}{f_0}\right)^2=\frac{2-Q^{-2}+\sqrt{(Q^{-2}-2)^2+4}}{2},\qquad B_N=\int_0^\infty\frac{|H(j2\pi f)|^2}{K^2}\,df=\frac{\pi Q}{2}f_0`}/>

| Q | $f_{-3}/f_0$ | $B_N/f_0$ | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.5 | 0.644 | 0.785 | Critical damping; corner below natural frequency |
| $1/\sqrt2$ | 1.000 | 1.111 | Butterworth; noise bandwidth still exceeds cutoff |
| 2 | 1.485 | 3.142 | Large resonance raises integrated noise power |

The noise-bandwidth expression concerns white noise entering **before the whole filter**. Op-amp voltage noise, resistor noise and later-stage noise enter through different transfers and need separate integration. For $Q>1/\sqrt2$, the peak occurs at $f_0\sqrt{1-1/(2Q^2)}$ with normalized amplitude $Q/\sqrt{1-1/(4Q^2)}$.

<h3>A source-resistance and amplifier check</h3>

Adding 1 kΩ source resistance to an equal 10 kΩ, 10 nF Butterworth section changes $R_1$ to 11 kΩ. Keeping $K=3-\sqrt2$ gives $f_0\approx1517.5$ Hz and $Q\approx0.721$: both the time scale and damping change. Buffer the source or include its impedance in the design; adding it only to a cutoff estimate misses the Q error. An output load does not enter the ideal input-network equations, but it changes the real amplifier's available swing, current and output impedance.

A finite amplifier has $K(s)=A(s)/[1+\beta A(s)]$, where $\beta=1/K_{set}$ is the feedback-divider fraction and $K_{set}=1+R_F/R_G$ is the intended gain. The actual DC gain approaches that value only when open-loop gain is sufficiently large. Substitute this complex $K(s)$ into the node derivation. The result is generally **higher order**; a falling magnitude and extra phase alter Q and pole positions. For a first-order feasibility estimate use $|A\beta|\approx f_t/(Kf_0)$, then allow for the increased sensitivity of a high-Q section. At $K=1.586$, $f_0=1.59$ kHz and $f_t=1$ MHz, that estimate is about 396, not infinite. Validate the complete circuit model and measure its peak and step response rather than relying on a universal GBW multiplier.

Slew rate must exceed $2\pi fV_{O,pk}$ at the **largest output amplitude**, including resonant gain. A 1 V peak input at $f_0$ with $K=2.5$, $Q=2$ requests 5 V peak and 0.050 V/µs. A ±4 V output limit clips before a 0.1 V/µs slew limit is reached. Clipping invalidates the linear transfer function even though the small-signal poles remain mathematically well placed.

<div className="takeaway"><span className="tiny-label">THE IDEA TO KEEP</span><p>A Sallen–Key section places a complex pole pair at $f_0 = 1/2\pi\sqrt{R_1R_2C_1C_2}$ with a $Q$ set by gain or by $C_1/C_2$. <em>Cascade sections with different $f_0$ and $Q$</em>, taken from the family's table, to build any classical filter.</p></div>

<section id="check">

<div className="section-heading"><span>07</span><h2>Check your understanding</h2></div>

<details>
<summary>1. Why does more gain make an equal-component Sallen–Key resonance sharper?</summary>
<div>

$C_1$ returns $K$ times the plus-input signal to the middle of the RC chain. That positive feedback cancels part of the chain's own damping, $b = RC(3 - K)$. As $K$ approaches 3, the damping vanishes and $Q = 1/(3 - K)$ grows without limit.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>2. Is a unity-gain equal-component Sallen–Key section a Butterworth filter?</summary>
<div>

No. With $K$ = 1 and equal parts, $Q$ = 0.5, which is critically damped and 6 dB down at $f_0$. Butterworth needs $Q$ = 0.707: either $K$ = 1.586 with equal parts, or unity gain with $C_1 = 2C_2$.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>3. Why are two identical Butterworth sections not a fourth-order Butterworth filter?</summary>
<div>

A fourth-order Butterworth needs its four poles spread on a circle, which means two sections with the same $f_0$ but $Q$ values of 0.541 and 1.307. Two $Q$ = 0.707 sections give $(−3\text{ dB})^2$ = −6 dB at $f_c$ and a less flat passband.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>4. When would you choose a Bessel filter over a Butterworth?</summary>
<div>

When the waveform's shape matters, for pulses, step responses or data. The Bessel filter's group delay is nearly constant through the passband, so it overshoots less than 1%, at the cost of a much gentler cutoff.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>5. For a unity-gain section with $Q$ = 2, what capacitor ratio is needed?</summary>
<div>

$C_1/C_2 = 4Q^2$ = **16**. High-Q unity-gain sections need large capacitor spreads; at some point a different topology (multiple feedback, state variable) is more practical.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>6. In a cascade, why put the low-Q section first?</summary>
<div>

High-Q sections have internal gain peaks near their natural frequency. Placing them after the low-Q sections means the signal has already been partly filtered, so a large out-of-band component cannot clip the high-Q section's op amp.

</div>
</details>
