<div className="opening-grid">

<div><span className="tiny-label">PREDICT BEFORE CALCULATING</span><p className="opening-question">An instrumentation amplifier's output is comfortably within its range. Can one of its internal amplifiers be saturated anyway?</p></div>

<div>

Chapter 85's difference amplifier had two weaknesses: its inputs loaded the sources unequally, which spoiled its CMRR, and its CMRR was limited by resistor matching. The instrumentation amplifier fixes both. Two noninverting buffers present high, equal impedances to the sources; a single resistor between them sets a differential gain without touching the common mode; and a difference amplifier at the end subtracts.

It is the standard front end for bridges, thermocouples, biopotential electrodes and any sensor whose signal is a small difference. It also has a subtle failure mode, the one in the question above, which datasheets describe with a peculiar diamond-shaped graph.

</div>

</div>

<section id="model">

<div className="section-heading"><span>01</span><h2>Inspect the internal outputs</h2></div>

<InstrumentationFigure/>

The two input op amps are noninverting stages with equal feedback resistors $R$. Their minus inputs are joined through a single gain resistor $R_G$. The final stage is a matched-ratio difference amplifier that forms $V_B - V_A$, the difference of the two internal outputs.

Feedback holds each minus input at its own plus input's voltage, $V_1$ and $V_2$. So $R_G$ has exactly $V_2 - V_1$ across it and carries $(V_2 - V_1)/R_G$. That same current must flow through both $R$ resistors (the op amp inputs take none), so the internal outputs spread apart by $(V_2 - V_1)(1 + 2R/R_G)$. The inputs draw only bias current, but that current still needs a DC path to ground through the source.

</section>

<section id="derive">

<div className="section-heading"><span>02</span><h2>Keep common mode at unity gain</h2></div>

<Equation tex={String.raw`V_B-V_A=G_1\,(V_2-V_1),\qquad G_1=1+\frac{2R}{R_G},\qquad G=G_1G_D`} label="THREE-OP-AMP INA GAIN"/>

Now apply a common-mode voltage alone, $V_1 = V_2 = V_C$. Both minus inputs sit at $V_C$, no current flows in $R_G$, and both internal outputs equal $V_C$: the first stage passes common mode at **unity** gain. With both together,

<Equation tex={String.raw`V_A=V_C-\frac{G_1v_d}{2},\qquad V_B=V_C+\frac{G_1v_d}{2}`}/>

One resistor, $R_G$, sets the differential gain; there is nothing to match in the first stage. All the common-mode rejection happens in the final subtractor, which now receives a difference $G_1$ times larger than the original.

</section>

<section id="cmrr">

<div className="section-heading"><span>03</span><h2>Gain multiplies the CMRR</h2></div>

The subtractor's common-mode gain is set by its resistor mismatch (Chapter 85) and is the same whatever $G_1$ is, because the common mode reaches it unamplified. Its differential input has been multiplied by $G_1$. So the overall CMRR is

<Equation tex={String.raw`\mathrm{CMRR}_{INA}\approx G_1\cdot\mathrm{CMRR}_{subtractor}`}/>

<InaCmrrGainFigure/>

With a 0.1% subtractor (66 dB) and a first-stage gain of 100, the INA reaches about 106 dB, a hundred times better than the difference amplifier alone, with the same resistors. That is why INA datasheets show CMRR rising with gain. The source-impedance problem of Chapter 85 also disappears: the sources drive op amp inputs of gigaohms, so a 100 Ω difference in source resistance no longer matters at DC.

</section>

<section id="cases">

<div className="section-heading"><span>04</span><h2>Move gain from one stage to another</h2></div>

<div className="worked-example">

<span className="tiny-label">WORKED EXAMPLE / THE INTERNAL OUTPUTS LIMIT THE RANGE</span><h3>$R$ = 10 kΩ, $R_G$ = 2 kΩ ($G_1$ = 11), unity subtractor, 5 V supply</h3>

<p>At $V_C$ = 2.5 V, differential inputs of 10, 100 and 400 mV give internal outputs of 2.445/2.555, 1.95/3.05 and <strong>0.30/4.70 V</strong>, and final outputs (relative to the reference) of 0.11, 1.10 and 4.40 V.</p>

<p>If the internal op amps can swing only from 0.2 to 4.5 V, the 400 mV case fails: $V_B$ would need 4.70 V. The final output, 4.40 V above a 0 V reference, might itself be valid, so nothing at the output warns you. That answers the opening question: yes.</p>

<p>Raise the common mode instead and the limits close in from the other side: at $V_C$ = 4 V even the 100 mV case needs $V_B$ = 4.55 V, beyond the 4.5 V swing, and many input op amps would already be outside their common-mode range. Every one of these limits depends on both the signal and the common mode.</p>

</div>

<InaDiamondFigure/>

**Distributing the gain.** For a total gain of 100, $G_1 = 100$ with a unity subtractor gives the best CMRR and makes the subtractor's offset and noise negligible, but a 20 mV input spreads the internal outputs by 2 V. $G_1 = 10$ with $G_D = 10$ spreads them by only 0.2 V, widening the allowed common-mode range, at the cost of 20 dB of CMRR and more error from the subtractor. Choose by the common-mode range you need, not by habit.

</section>

<section id="bridge">

<div className="section-heading"><span>05</span><h2>Design a bridge amplifier</h2></div>

<BridgeInaCircuit/>

<div className="worked-example">

<span className="tiny-label">WORKED EXAMPLE / A 350 Ω STRAIN GAUGE ON 5 V</span><h3>2 mV/V full scale, 2 V output span</h3>

<p><strong>Gain.</strong> With 5 V excitation the bridge gives up to ±10 mV at full-scale strain of either sign. For an output of 2.5 V ± 1 V the gain must be 1 V/10 mV = 100. With $R$ = 10 kΩ, $R_G = 2R/(G - 1)$ = 202 Ω.</p>

<p><strong>Internal voltages.</strong> Both bridge outputs sit near 2.5 V. At full scale the internal outputs are $2.5 \pm 100 × 0.01/2$ = 2.0 and 3.0 V: comfortably inside a 0.2–4.5 V swing.</p>

<p><strong>Errors.</strong> The INA's 50 µV offset, referred to the input, is 0.5% of the 10 mV span: calibrate it, or use a zero-drift INA. CMRR of 100 dB turns a 50 mV change in the bridge's common mode (excitation drift) into 0.5 µV, negligible. The gain resistor's tolerance is the gain tolerance; a 0.1%, 10 ppm/°C part keeps the gain to 0.1% plus 0.04% over 40 °C.</p>

<p><strong>Bias return.</strong> The bridge connects both inputs to the supply and ground through 175 Ω, so the input bias currents have a DC path. A transformer-coupled or capacitively coupled source would need explicit return resistors.</p>

</div>

### Filter the inputs symmetrically

Long sensor cables pick up radio-frequency interference as common mode, and an INA's CMRR falls at high frequency. An RC low-pass in each input helps, but only if the two are matched. If one leg's filter capacitor is 5% larger than the other's, a common-mode signal is filtered slightly differently on the two sides and becomes a differential one. With 4.99 kΩ and 1 nF per leg, at 10 kHz, a 5% capacitor mismatch converts about **1.4%** of the common mode into a differential signal, which the INA then amplifies by its full gain. Standard practice adds a differential capacitor across the inputs, about ten times larger than each common-mode capacitor, so that the differential bandwidth is set by one part and the mismatch matters far less.



<h3 id="book-notes">The reference pin is part of the precision signal path</h3>

The familiar expression $V_O=G(V_+-V_-)+V_{REF}$ assumes a sufficiently stiff reference node. In a discrete three-op-amp INA, that reference enters the final difference amplifier through a resistor network. Reference-source resistance changes the network ratio and lets common-mode output from the first stage appear at the final output.

For a unity-gain final difference stage with four nominal 10 kΩ resistors, adding 100 Ω in series with the reference-side resistor makes that arm 10.1 kΩ. The noninverting divider factor becomes $10.1/(10+10.1)=0.502488$ instead of 0.5. With the inverting side still balanced, equal 2 V first-stage outputs then leave about **9.95 mV** of unintended final output. A “small” 100 Ω reference impedance has consumed substantial common-mode rejection.

A divider making mid-supply may therefore need a suitable buffer rather than being connected directly to the reference pin. For an integrated INA, use its documented reference-input model: internal architecture and allowable source impedance vary. Keep load-return current away from the reference's sensing connection; a millivolt ground difference is directly a millivolt output error in the ideal transfer.

When several channels share a reference, calculate the loading and transient coupling of the complete group. A reference capacitor can reduce high-frequency movement while leaving DC source impedance, buffer stability and startup to solve. “Common ground” on a schematic does not make every physical return point equipotential.

<section id="limits">

<div className="section-heading"><span>06</span><h2>Give the reference a real driver</h2></div>

The final subtractor's ground end, the **reference** pin, sets the output's zero. It is one end of the subtractor's resistor divider, so it must be driven from a low impedance: a resistor in series with it unbalances the ratio and degrades both gain and CMRR. Drive it with a buffer or a low-impedance reference. Its noise and drift appear directly at the output.

**Integrated or discrete?** An integrated INA has laser-trimmed, matched resistors, a characterized diamond plot and specified CMRR at each gain. Three separate op amps and resistors can match its gain, but rarely its CMRR or its drift. The circuit constraints of this chapter apply to both.

**Verify:** internal-output range at the worst common mode and signal, input common-mode range, gain-resistor tolerance and drift, reference impedance and noise, bandwidth at the chosen gain (each input op amp runs at noise gain $G_1$), and input filtering.

<div className="takeaway"><span className="tiny-label">THE IDEA TO KEEP</span><p>The input stage amplifies the difference by $1 + 2R/R_G$ while passing common mode at unity, so <em>CMRR grows with gain</em>. Check the internal outputs, $V_C \pm G_1v_d/2$, against their swing: the final output cannot tell you they have clipped.</p></div>

</section>

<section id="precision-depth">

<div className="section-heading"><span>07</span><h2>Design the INA input, protection, range and programmable gain together</h2></div>

<h3>Why two followers and a subtractor are only a first attempt</h3>

Two unity followers prevent source loading, but leave the subtractor's resistor mismatch and output-stage noise unchanged. The classic three-amplifier INA instead puts differential gain in the first pair while their common-mode gain remains unity. That reduces the subtractor's input-referred noise and common-mode error. With unequal first-stage feedback resistors, the differential gain becomes $1+(R_{F1}+R_{F2})/R_G$; pure common mode still follows with unity gain, although a differential signal can shift the first-stage output common mode. Matching helps preserve internal range even where the gain law needs only their sum.

A discrete INA lets the designer choose quiet inputs, access internal nodes and tailor protection. An integrated INA can provide trimmed resistor tracking, compact layout and specified CMRR. Neither eliminates the need to check the internal-output range. Other architectures use two amplifiers, current feedback, replicated differential voltage across a gain resistor, or switched-capacitor transfer. Their reference range, differential-input limits and gain laws can differ from the three-amplifier model.

<h3>Current noise and source balance remain input problems</h3>

For two independent input voltage-noise sources each at 10 nV/√Hz, their differential contribution is 14.14 nV/√Hz. A downstream 20 nV/√Hz noise source contributes only 0.2 nV/√Hz referred through preceding differential gain 100. But 1 pA/√Hz input current noise through each 100 kΩ source contributes 100 nV/√Hz per side, or 141.4 nV/√Hz differentially. Early gain cannot reduce noise already generated at the sensor inputs.

Equal average bias currents flowing through unequal source resistances create a differential offset. With 2 nA bias and a 10 kΩ resistance imbalance, that error is 20 µV. Bias mismatch adds another term even with balanced sources. Match impedances where practical, select suitable input technology, and include capacitance when predicting AC CMRR. A common-mode guard can reduce leakage and loading to a surrounding shield; it does not remove capacitance **between** the two signal conductors. Individually driven guards address that differential loading but require stable drivers and correct guard voltages.

<h3>Give isolated sources a defined bias path</h3>

A transformer or capacitor-coupled sensor may supply no DC return. Add balanced resistors from the inputs to a valid bias reference. With 10 MΩ returns and 10 nA input current, the inputs can move by 100 mV. If the two currents differ by 1 nA, another 10 mV differential error appears before gain. A sensor can have a tiny signal and still drive the INA outside its input range through missing bias paths.

The output relation $v_o=Gv_d+V_R$ is only an external constraint. For first-stage gain 100 and $v_d=10$ mV, its internal outputs are approximately $V_{CM}\pm0.5$ V. On a 3.3 V supply with internal output limits 0.2–3.1 V, that requires $V_{CM}$ between 0.7 and 2.6 V before final-stage constraints are applied. Setting the final reference to 1.65 V does not move those internal outputs. Use the device's gain-dependent common-mode/output range diagram when supplied.

<h3>Protection and filtering must preserve balance</h3>

An illustrative input filter uses 1 kΩ in each arm, matched 100 pF capacitors from each input to the local quiet reference, and 10 nF between inputs. Common-mode bandwidth is about 1.59 MHz; differential bandwidth is $1/[2\pi R(C_{CM}+2C_D)]\approx7.92$ kHz. The large differential capacitor makes small mismatches in common-mode capacitors less dominant for differential disturbances, but it does not excuse poor component matching or RF layout. The two resistors add roughly 5.76 nV/√Hz differential Johnson noise at 300 K.

RF can rectify in input junctions and appear as a DC shift, so an output low-pass cannot undo it. Filter before the nonlinear input, shorten loops and manage shielding. Protection also needs a transient current and energy calculation: $i=C\,dv/dt$ means just 1 pF passes 100 mA during a 100 V/ns edge. Series impedance and clamping must handle both the initial capacitive pulse and sustained overdrive. Verify clamp leakage, capacitance, maximum input current, power-off behavior and where the clamped current goes. A bypassed protection node inside the permitted input range can be more effective than a clamp one diode drop beyond a rail.

<h3>Trim, sense and program the quantity that matters</h3>

Offset trim changes the zero; CMRR trim balances common-mode feedthrough. Adjusting one does not necessarily fix the other. A REF pin tied through a resistive potentiometer can spoil internal ratio matching; use a low-impedance driver if the architecture requires it. A separate SENSE pin, where available, can correct output lead resistance by returning feedback from the load. That adds cable impedance and capacitance to the loop and must be checked for stability.

At gain 100 with $R_F=10$ kΩ per side, $R_G=202.02$ Ω. A 1 Ω switch resistance in series with $R_G$ gives gain about 99.51, a 0.49% error. Integrated programmable-gain INAs can avoid this error with carefully placed switches and trimmed ratios, but each gain still has its own noise, bandwidth, CMRR and settling. Allow time after channel or gain switching before conversion, and calibrate gain-dependent offset if necessary. Auto-zero INAs reduce drift but retain switching artifacts, input pulses and overload constraints from Chapter 82.

<h3>An INA can measure current or close a current-control loop</h3>

Amplifying a Kelvin shunt voltage gives $v_s=G I R_S$. Comparing it with a command and driving a transistor yields $I=V_{cmd}/(GR_S)$ while the actuator has compliance. For $G=20$, $R_S=0.1$ Ω and a 1 V command, the target is 0.5 A; a 20 µV INA input offset contributes 0.2 mA current error. The INA adds poles, so the current-loop bandwidth must account for its gain setting and the transistor/load dynamics. Chapter 92 develops those dynamics.

To generate two outputs from a single-ended INA result, drive a follower and an inverter around a controlled reference: $v_{o+}=V_C+v_d/2$, $v_{o-}=V_C-v_d/2$. Independent errors and settling can unbalance them. A fully differential amplifier controls differential gain and output common mode together; Chapter 94 derives that interface and its ADC constraints.

<details>
<summary>Worked self-check: does a higher gain always improve the measurement?</summary>
<div>

Increasing first-stage gain from 10 to 100 reduces downstream input-referred noise tenfold. But a 20 mV differential input now requires 2 V separation between internal outputs, rather than 0.2 V. On a low supply that can invalidate the common-mode range. Increase gain only after checking the largest signal, baseline and transient at all internal nodes.

</div>
</details>

<section id="check">

<div className="section-heading"><span>08</span><h2>Check your understanding</h2></div>

<details>
<summary>1. Can the final output be in range while a first-stage amplifier is saturated?</summary>
<div>

Yes. The internal outputs sit at $V_C \pm G_1v_d/2$, which depends on the common mode, while the final output depends only on their difference. At a high common mode, a moderate difference can drive one internal output into its rail although the final output looks normal.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>2. Why does the first stage pass common mode at unity gain?</summary>
<div>

With equal inputs, both minus nodes sit at the same voltage, so no current flows through $R_G$, and none through the $R$ resistors. Each input op amp acts as a follower for the common mode.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>3. Why does INA CMRR increase with gain?</summary>
<div>

The common mode reaches the subtractor at unity gain, so its leakage there is fixed. The difference reaches it multiplied by $G_1$. The ratio of differential to common-mode gain, the CMRR, therefore grows in proportion to $G_1$, until the op amps' intrinsic CMRR limits it.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>4. For $R$ = 10 kΩ, what $R_G$ gives a first-stage gain of 100?</summary>
<div>

$G_1 = 1 + 2R/R_G$ = 100, so $R_G = 2R/99$ ≈ **202 Ω**. Its tolerance and drift become the gain's.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>5. A floating source (a thermocouple not connected to ground) drives an INA. What else is needed?</summary>
<div>

A DC path for the input bias currents: typically a resistor from each input (or one input) to the reference or ground. Without it, the inputs float to a rail and the INA saturates. Equal return resistors on both inputs keep the bias-current error common mode.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>6. Why must input filters on an INA be matched?</summary>
<div>

A mismatched pair of RC filters attenuates a common-mode signal by different amounts on the two inputs, producing a differential signal the INA cannot distinguish from the real one. A larger capacitor directly across the inputs makes the differential bandwidth depend on one component and reduces the mismatch's effect.

</div>
</details>
