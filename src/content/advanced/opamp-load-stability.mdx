<div className="opening-grid">

<div><span className="tiny-label">PREDICT BEFORE CALCULATING</span><p className="opening-question">Feedback is taken before a series resistor to the load. Who corrects the voltage dropped across that resistor?</p></div>

<div>

Op amps drive cables, ADC inputs, MOSFET gates and long traces, and all of those are capacitive. A follower that is perfectly stable on the bench may ring or oscillate once it drives a few nanofarads: Chapter 83 warned about it, and Chapter 92's current servo met a version of the same problem.

The cause is a pole in the feedback loop that the datasheet's open-loop curve does not show. The cures all involve a resistor between the op amp and the load, and the question is where the feedback comes from: before the resistor, after it, or both. Each choice gives a different combination of stability and accuracy.

</div>

</div>

<section id="model">

<div className="section-heading"><span>01</span><h2>A capacitor adds a pole</h2></div>

<OpAmpFigure mode="stability"/>

Inside the loop, the op amp's output stage has an open-loop output resistance $R_O$, typically tens of ohms. With a capacitive load it forms a pole:

<Equation tex={String.raw`f_{load}=\frac{1}{2\pi R_OC_L}`}/>

That pole sits between the op amp's internal gain and the feedback point, so it adds phase lag around the loop. A follower, whose noise gain of 1 puts its loop crossing at the highest frequency, suffers most:

<CapLoadLagFigure/>

Real output impedance is not a pure resistance: it rises with frequency as the loop gain falls, and some op amps have inductive-looking output impedance. The single-resistor model explains the problem; the datasheet's capacitive-load curves or a validated model decide a real design.

</section>

<section id="derive">

<div className="section-heading"><span>02</span><h2>Decide where the resistor sits relative to feedback</h2></div>

<IsolationCircuits/>

**Isolation resistor outside the loop.** Put $R_{ISO}$ between the op amp's pin and the load, and take feedback at the pin. The loop's load is now $R_{ISO}$ in series with $C_L$, which at high frequency looks resistive, so the pole is tamed: there is a zero at $1/(2\pi R_{ISO}C_L)$ that cancels much of its lag. But the load is now on the far side of an RC filter that the loop does not see. With a resistive load in parallel, the DC load voltage is

<Equation tex={String.raw`V_{load}=V_{pin}\cdot\frac{R_L}{R_L+R_{ISO}}`}/>

and that is the answer to the opening question: nobody corrects it. The loop holds the pin exactly; the resistor's drop is outside its reach.

**Feedback after the resistor.** Take feedback from the load instead, and the DC drop is corrected, but the $R_{ISO}C_L$ pole is now inside the loop, and the circuit is usually worse than with no resistor at all.

**Dual-path feedback.** Take DC feedback from the load through $R_F$ and high-frequency feedback from the pin through $C_F$. Below $1/(2\pi R_FC_F)$ the loop regulates the load; above it, the loop sees the pin, as in the isolated case. Choose $R_FC_F$ several times larger than $R_{ISO}C_L$, so that the crossover between the two paths happens where the load pole cannot cause trouble.

</section>

<section id="cases">

<div className="section-heading"><span>03</span><h2>Compare direct drive, isolation and dual feedback</h2></div>

<CapLoadStepFigure/>

<div className="worked-example">

<span className="tiny-label">WORKED EXAMPLE / 10 nF AND 1 kΩ FROM A 1 MHz FOLLOWER</span><h3>$R_O$ = 50 Ω, $R_{ISO}$ = 50 Ω, $R_FC_F$ = 30 µs</h3>

<p><strong>Direct drive.</strong> The load pole is at $1/(2\pi × 50 × 10\text{ nF})$ = 318 kHz. At the 1 MHz crossing it adds 72° of lag, leaving less than 20° of margin. The step overshoots about 40% and rings for several microseconds.</p>

<p><strong>Isolation outside the loop.</strong> 1% overshoot, settled within 2% in about 1.5 µs. But the load sees $1000/1050$ = 0.952 of the pin voltage: a 4.8% gain error. With 100 Ω of isolation there is no overshoot at all, settling stretches to about 4 µs, and the error doubles to 9%.</p>

<p><strong>Dual-path feedback.</strong> The load settles to exactly 1.000 V with no overshoot, but its last few percent are slow, about 16 µs, because the DC path's time constant is 30 µs. A shorter $R_FC_F$ settles faster but brings back ringing: at 1–3 µs the overshoot is 14–28%.</p>

</div>

None of the three is best at everything. If the load is purely capacitive (an ADC input, a reference pin), isolation outside the loop has no DC error and is the simplest choice. If the load also draws DC current and accuracy matters, use dual feedback, or pick a driver specified for the load.

Set the noise gain to 1 and move the pole from 10 MHz down to 300 kHz: the peaking grows as the margin disappears. This is an abstract open-loop pole, not a complete output-impedance and isolation model.</p>

</section>

<section id="charge">

<div className="section-heading"><span>04</span><h2>Deliver the charge</h2></div>

Stability is a small-signal question. A capacitor also demands current, which is a large-signal one:

<Equation tex={String.raw`I=C_L\frac{dV}{dt}`}/>

<div className="worked-example">

<span className="tiny-label">WORKED EXAMPLE / CAN IT FOLLOW THE EDGE?</span><h3>2 V step into 10 nF in 1 µs</h3>

<p>The average current is 10 nF × 2 V/1 µs = <strong>20 mA</strong>. An op amp limited to 5 mA of output current slews the load at only 0.5 V/µs, taking 4 µs, however good its phase margin. The capacitor ends up storing $\tfrac12CV^2$ = 20 nJ; delivering that charge takes time set by the current limit.</p>

<p>A step that hits the current limit also leaves the loop: while current-limited, the op amp is effectively open loop, its internal nodes may saturate, and recovery adds more time. Check the large-signal step separately from the small-signal one.</p>

</div>

</section>

<section id="limits">

<div className="section-heading"><span>05</span><h2>Verify both stability and delivered accuracy</h2></div>

**Measure with the real load.** Small steps reveal ringing; large steps reveal current limiting and slewing. A long cable is not a lumped capacitor: it has propagation delay and inductance, and above a few megahertz it should be terminated in its characteristic impedance rather than treated as $C_L$.

**Mind the probe.** A 10 pF probe on a lightly loaded node changes the very response being measured. Probe the load end, use a low-capacitance probe, and compare a few load values.

**Use the datasheet.** Many op amps specify a maximum capacitive load for unity-gain stability, or recommend an isolation resistor against $C_L$. Some are designed to drive any capacitance, with a dominant pole that moves with the load. When the load is heavy and the edge fast, a dedicated buffer or line driver may be simpler than any feedback trick.

<div className="takeaway"><span className="tiny-label">THE IDEA TO KEEP</span><p>A capacitive load forms a pole with the op amp's output resistance. An isolation resistor tames it if feedback is taken <em>before</em> the resistor, at the cost of an uncorrected drop; dual-path feedback gets both, more slowly. Then check the current $C\,dV/dt$.</p></div>

</section>

<section id="precision-depth">

<div className="section-heading"><span>+</span><h2>Combine precision and power without hiding an extra loop</h2></div>

<h3>A power buffer helps only if its position in the loop is explicit</h3>

A buffer outside the precision amplifier's feedback path leaves its offset, gain error and load-dependent voltage drop uncorrected. Putting it inside the path corrects these errors at frequencies with sufficient loop gain, but adds its delay and poles. “Unity gain” describes DC signal gain; it does not mean zero phase shift or infinite bandwidth.

Let the precision amplifier have gain $A(s)$, the buffer have transfer $B(s)$ and the overall feedback fraction be $\beta$. Then $H=AB/(1+AB\beta)$ and the return ratio is $T=AB\beta$. A buffer with a 200 kHz pole can seriously degrade a follower using a nominal 1 MHz amplifier: in the same two-pole teaching model developed in Chapter 79, crossover is about 425 kHz and phase margin only about 25°. Check the combined loop, not each device's individual “stable follower” claim.

<h3>A separate fast feedback path can combine precision and drive</h3>

The dual-feedback principle in the earlier schematic returns the remote load voltage at low frequency and the precision amplifier's local output at high frequency. In a functional model let $u$ be the buffer drive, $y=Bu$, and $u=A[v_i-\beta_L y-\beta_Hu]$. Then

<Equation tex={String.raw`T=A(\beta_H+\beta_L B),\qquad \frac{y}{v_i}=\frac{AB}{1+A(\beta_H+\beta_LB)}`}/>

A useful conceptual split is $\beta_L=\beta/(1+s\tau)$ and $\beta_H=\beta s\tau/(1+s\tau)$. At DC, the full amplifier/buffer path is corrected. At high frequency the local path dominates, avoiding the buffer's full phase contribution around the precision amplifier. The delivered high-frequency output still includes $B$; bypassing it in feedback cannot make the buffer infinitely fast. Real resistor/capacitor networks may not follow this simple split exactly, and the transition region can introduce a new peak. Derive and verify the actual network.

This is one route to combining low drift, low noise and high speed/current in a composite amplifier. Another uses a slow precision servo to correct the offset of a fast path. Both have multiple bandwidths and states. Set a separation between their operating bands and measure startup, overload recovery and settling tails; merely cascading the best-looking specifications from two devices does not create their sum.

<h3>Output crossover, impedance and heating limit precision</h3>

A poorly biased push-pull stage can need extra differential input around current reversal. Feedback reduces that error where loop gain is high, but available correction falls with frequency and can depend on load. Measure differential input error against output voltage under a slow triangle drive to distinguish offset, smooth finite-gain error and localized crossover/nonlinearity. Repeat with the actual load and both current directions.

For an additive 10 mV output-stage error and a real low-frequency loop gain of 100, the residual is about 99 µV. At higher frequency use the complex $1/(1+T)$, not that scalar reduction. Harmonics generated by the output stage see loop gain at their own frequencies. Rail saturation and current limiting create nonlinear states that the small-signal correction formula does not describe.

A buffer driving 50 mA while dropping 5 V dissipates 250 mW. At 100 °C/W thermal resistance its steady temperature rise is about 25 °C. Keeping that heat out of the precision input amplifier reduces thermal gradients and offset drift. A shared dual package can couple output-channel heating into the quiet channel; physical partitioning may matter as much as circuit gain.

<h3>Calculate charge current before promising a waveform</h3>

For a 100 nF load and 2 V peak sine at 20 kHz, the peak capacitor current is $2\pi fCV_{pk}=25.1$ mA. With 22 Ω isolation the resistor's peak drop is about 0.553 V, adding to required amplifier output swing. A 10 mA DC resistive load adds 0.22 V drop if the feedback senses before that resistor; feedback after it can correct the DC drop while altering stability. The resistor also adds output impedance and a load-dependent transfer function.

For an ADC, delivered settling at the sampling instant matters more than the unloaded amplifier waveform. Include the switched input capacitance, isolation resistor, charge reservoir, previous-channel voltage and acquisition window. Stability, droop, current limit and final voltage accuracy are separate checks.

<details>
<summary>Worked self-check: can a buffer guarantee the same precision into every load?</summary>
<div>

No. The buffer's output swing and current limit set the feasible load, its poles change loop margin, its load-dependent gain changes finite-gain error, and its heat can shift offsets. Verify the largest and smallest load, capacitive extremes and both current polarities. A buffer can solve a specific drive problem while introducing new dynamic constraints.

</div>
</details>

<section id="check">

<div className="section-heading"><span>06</span><h2>Check your understanding</h2></div>

<details>
<summary>1. If feedback senses before a series resistor, who corrects the load's voltage drop?</summary>
<div>

No one. The loop holds the voltage at its feedback point, the op amp's pin. The resistor's drop with any resistive load, and the delay of its RC with the load capacitance, are outside the loop and appear uncorrected at the load.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>2. Why does a capacitive load threaten a follower more than a gain-of-10 amplifier?</summary>
<div>

The follower's loop crosses unity at the op amp's full $f_t$, where the load pole $1/(2\pi R_OC_L)$ adds the most phase lag. At a noise gain of 10 the crossing is ten times lower, the same pole adds far less lag, and the margin survives.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>3. With $R_O$ = 50 Ω and a 1 MHz crossing, how much phase does 1 nF cost?</summary>
<div>

The pole is at $1/(2\pi × 50 × 1\text{ nF})$ = 3.2 MHz, and at 1 MHz it adds $\arctan(1/3.2)$ ≈ **17°**.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>4. Why does moving the feedback point to after the isolation resistor usually make things worse?</summary>
<div>

It puts the $R_{ISO}C_L$ pole, now lower in frequency than the original $R_OC_L$ pole, directly inside the loop, adding even more phase lag. The isolation resistor helps only when the loop is closed before it at high frequency.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>5. In dual-path feedback, what sets how fast the load voltage reaches its final value?</summary>
<div>

The DC path's time constant $R_FC_F$. Above $1/(2\pi R_FC_F)$ the loop regulates the pin; the load voltage converges to the correct value only as the slower DC path takes over. Making $R_FC_F$ too short brings the load pole back into the high-frequency loop and causes ringing.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>6. An op amp with 5 mA of output current drives 10 nF. How fast can it slew the load?</summary>
<div>

$dV/dt = I/C$ = 5 mA/10 nF = **0.5 V/µs**, regardless of its small-signal bandwidth or phase margin. A 2 V step takes at least 4 µs.

</div>
</details>
