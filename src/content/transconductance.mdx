<div className="opening-grid">

<div><span className="tiny-label">A PRACTICAL QUESTION</span><p className="opening-question">A push–pull output stage needs two drive signals, one the inverse of the other. Can a single transistor provide both?</p></div>

<div>

In a common-emitter stage the same signal current flows through the emitter resistor and the collector resistor. The emitter voltage rises with the input; the collector voltage falls. Make the two resistors equal and you have two outputs of equal size and opposite sign: a **phase splitter**.

The phase splitter is also a good place to change viewpoint. Every stage so far has done the same two things: turned an input voltage into a current, then turned that current back into a voltage across a resistor. Naming the first step—**transconductance**—gives a single, cleaner description of the follower, the current source and the common-emitter amplifier, and sets up the Ebers–Moll model in Chapter 34.

</div>

</div>

<section id="splitter">

<div className="section-heading"><span>01</span><h2>Equal and opposite outputs</h2></div>

Take a common-emitter stage, set $R_C$ = $R_E$, and use both the collector and the emitter as outputs. The emitter current $i_e$ = $v_{\mathrm{in}}$/($R_E$ + $r_e$) flows through both resistors (to within $\alpha$), so

<Equation tex="G_E=\frac{R_E}{R_E+r_e}\approx+1,\qquad G_C=-\frac{R_C}{R_E+r_e}\approx-1\qquad(R_C=R_E)" label="UNITY-GAIN PHASE SPLITTER"/>

With $R_C$ = $R_E$ = 4.7 kΩ and the base biased at 5 V on 15 V, the bias current is 0.93 mA, $r_e$ = 28 Ω, and the gains are +0.994 and −0.994: equal and opposite to 0.1%.

### Biasing for swing

Both outputs move, toward each other on positive input swings. At rest, $V_E$ ≈ 4.4 V and $V_C$ ≈ 10.6 V, leaving 6.2 V between them. The transistor saturates when $V_{CE}$ falls to about 0.2 V, so each output can swing about 3 V on the positive input half-cycle. On the negative half-cycle, the emitter can fall 4.4 V before the transistor cuts off. The symmetric limit is therefore about ±3 V. Splitting the supply evenly among $R_C$, $R_E$ and $V_{CE}$ is the general rule.

### A phase shifter

Connect a resistor R from the collector output and a capacitor C from the emitter output to a common node $x$. Treat the two outputs as ideal sources, $-\mathbf v$ at the collector and $+\mathbf v$ at the emitter.

<div className="derivation">

<span className="tiny-label">DERIVATION / THE ALL-PASS PHASE SHIFTER BY KCL</span>

1. **KCL at node $x$.** With nothing else connected, the current arriving through R equals the current leaving through C:
<Equation tex="\frac{-\mathbf v-\mathbf V_x}{R}+j\omega C\,(\mathbf v-\mathbf V_x)=0"/>

2. **Collect the $\mathbf V_x$ terms.** Multiply by $R$:
<Equation tex="-\mathbf v-\mathbf V_x+j\omega RC\,\mathbf v-j\omega RC\,\mathbf V_x=0\ \Longrightarrow\ \mathbf V_x=\mathbf v\,\frac{j\omega RC-1}{j\omega RC+1}"/>

3. **Magnitude.** Numerator and denominator have the same magnitude, $\sqrt{1+(\omega RC)^2}$, so $|\mathbf V_x| = |\mathbf v|$ at every frequency.

4. **Phase.** The numerator $-1 + j\omega RC$ lies in the second quadrant at angle $180° - \tan^{-1}(\omega RC)$; the denominator is at $+\tan^{-1}(\omega RC)$. Subtracting:
<Equation tex="\angle\mathbf V_x=180^\circ-2\tan^{-1}(\omega RC)"/>

</div>

The amplitude is constant at every frequency while the phase moves from 180° (low frequency) through 90° (at ωRC = 1) to 0°. Making R variable gives an adjustable phase shifter with constant amplitude—an **all-pass** network—that phasors (Chapter 12) make easy to analyze.

</section>

<section id="impedance">

<div className="section-heading"><span>02</span><h2>Two very different output impedances</h2></div>

The two outputs have the same gain but not the same character:

- The **emitter output** is a follower. Looking back into it, you see $r_e$ plus the base source resistance divided by $\beta$ + 1, in parallel with $R_E$: about 28 Ω here.
- The **collector output** looks into the collector—a current source—in parallel with $R_C$: about 4.7 kΩ.

A load on the collector output forms a divider with 4.7 kΩ. A 10 kΩ load reduces the collector gain from −0.994 to **−0.68**, while the same load on the emitter output reduces its gain only from 0.994 to 0.991. Worse, loading the emitter output changes the *collector* gain: $R_E$ ∥ $R_L$ sets the emitter current, so a 10 kΩ emitter load raises the collector gain to **−1.46**. The outputs are only balanced if both see high-impedance loads, or if $R_C$ is adjusted to compensate a known load.

</section>

<section id="gm">

<div className="section-heading"><span>04</span><h2>Transconductance</h2></div>

**Transconductance** is the ratio of an output current to the input voltage that produces it, measured in siemens:

<Equation tex="G_m=\frac{\Delta I_{\mathrm{out}}}{\Delta V_{\mathrm{in}}}"/>

For a bare transistor driven at its base with the emitter at signal ground, Chapter 30 found $g_m$ = $I_C$/$V_T$: 38.7 mS at 1 mA. With an emitter resistor, the input voltage must also drive $R_E$. Chapter 32's nodal analysis found $v_{be} = v_b\,r_\pi/[r_\pi + (\beta+1)R_E]$. Multiply by $g_m$ to get the output current, then divide numerator and denominator by $r_\pi$ (with $(\beta+1)/r_\pi \approx g_m$):

<Equation tex="i_c=g_mv_{be}=\frac{g_m}{1+(\beta+1)R_E/r_\pi}\,v_b\approx\frac{g_m}{1+g_mR_E}\,v_b"/>

The stage’s transconductance is therefore smaller and set mainly by the resistor:

<Equation tex="G_m=\frac{1}{R_E+r_e}=\frac{g_m}{1+g_mR_E}\approx\frac{1}{R_E}\qquad(g_mR_E\gg1)" label="DEGENERATED TRANSCONDUCTANCE"/>

With $R_E$ = 1 kΩ at 1 mA, $G_m$ = 1/1026 Ω = 0.975 mS. The factor 1 + $g_mR$_E, which reduced the transconductance by 40 times, is the same factor that raised the current source’s output resistance in Chapter 31. Degeneration trades one for the other.

### Every stage as $G_m$ plus a resistor

Now every circuit so far has a one-line description:

<div className="table-wrap">

<table><thead><tr><th>Stage</th><th>Voltage → current</th><th>Current → voltage</th><th>Voltage gain</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>Common emitter</td><td>$G_m$ = 1/($R_E$ + $r_e$)</td><td>−$R_C$ (∥ $R_L$)</td><td>−$G_m$ $R_C$</td></tr><tr><td>Bypassed common emitter</td><td>$g_m$ = $I_C$/$V_T$</td><td>−$R_C$</td><td>−$g_m$ $R_C$</td></tr><tr><td>Emitter follower</td><td>$G_m$ = 1/($R_E$ + $r_e$)</td><td>+$R_E$</td><td>$R_E$/($R_E$ + $r_e$)</td></tr><tr><td>Current source</td><td>$G_m$, with fixed input</td><td>load (any)</td><td>— (output is a current)</td></tr><tr><td>Phase splitter</td><td>$G_m$ = 1/($R_E$ + $r_e$)</td><td>−$R_C$ and +$R_E$</td><td>∓1</td></tr></tbody></table>

</div>

The gain of a stage is its transconductance times the resistance its output current flows into. This view generalizes to field-effect transistors, vacuum tubes and integrated transconductance amplifiers.

</section>

<section id="views">

<div className="section-heading"><span>05</span><h2>Two views of one transistor</h2></div>

Chapter 27 described the transistor as a **current amplifier**: $I_C$ = $\beta I_B$. This chapter describes it as a **transconductance device**: $I_C$ is set by $V_{BE}$. Which is right?

Physically, the transconductance view is (Chapter 26). The base–emitter voltage sets the injected electron density, and the collector current follows; the base current is a by-product of losses, roughly $I_C$/$\beta$. The two descriptions are consistent because both $I_C$ and $I_B$ follow $V_{BE}$. Each is useful in its place:

- Use **$\beta$** when the question is about **base current**: loading of a bias divider, input impedance, how much drive a switch needs.
- Use **$g_m$** (or 1/($R_E$ + $r_e$)) when the question is about **gain**, distortion, temperature effects and anything depending on $V_{BE}$.

A designer thinking “the base current controls the collector” tends to design circuits whose behavior depends on $\beta$. Thinking “$V_{BE}$ controls the collector, and an emitter resistor controls $V_{BE}$” leads to circuits whose behavior depends on resistors.

</section>

<section id="uses">

<div className="section-heading"><span>06</span><h2>Transconductance at work</h2></div>

**A gain set by current.** In a bypassed stage, gain is −$g_mR$_C = −$I_CR$_C/$V_T$. Change the bias current and the gain changes in proportion. Automatic gain control in radio receivers, analog multipliers and voltage-controlled amplifiers exploit exactly this—with care, because the DC collector voltage moves too. The differential pair (a later topic) separates the gain-setting current from the output’s DC level.

**A voltage-to-current converter.** A degenerated transistor turns a control voltage into a load current independent of the load: a meter driver, an LED brightness control, a 4–20 mA transmitter. The current source of Chapter 31 is this with a fixed input.

**Why $g_m$ sets fundamental limits.** Because $g_m$ depends only on $I_C$ and temperature, so do the maximum voltage gain of a bypassed stage ($V_{RC}$/$V_T$), the input noise of a transistor at a given current, and the input voltage range over which a stage stays linear (a few $V_T$). The Ebers–Moll chapter turns these into rules of thumb.

### Compensate a known collector load

The collector output is $-i_c(R_C\parallel R_{LC})$ and the emitter output is $+i_e(R_E\parallel R_{LE})$, with $i_c = \alpha i_e$. Equal magnitudes therefore require, to within $\alpha$, $R_C\parallel R_{LC}=R_E\parallel R_{LE}$. With an effectively unloaded emitter output, $R_E=4.7$ kΩ and a 10 kΩ collector load, solve $R_C\parallel10\ \mathrm{k\Omega}=4.7\ \mathrm{k\Omega}$:

<Equation tex="R_C=\frac{4.7\times10}{10-4.7}\ \mathrm{k\Omega}=8.87\ \mathrm{k\Omega}" />

This can restore small-signal gain balance for the specified load, but it changes the DC collector voltage. At about 0.92 mA bias, an 8.87 kΩ resistor drops 8.16 V, leaving roughly 6.84 V on the collector while the emitter is about 4.35 V. The collector now has much less downward headroom. Recalculate bias and signal swing after compensating the gain; AC balance and DC headroom are separate requirements.

The all-pass expression likewise assumes ideal equal-and-opposite drive voltages. Finite and unequal output impedances load its arms differently, so a real one-transistor phase shifter is not perfectly constant-amplitude without including those impedances.

<div className="takeaway"><span className="tiny-label">THE IDEA TO KEEP</span><p>Every stage is a <em>transconductance followed by a resistor</em>. The phase splitter just takes the same current through two resistors—and shows how differently they behave under load.</p></div>

<section id="check">

<div className="section-heading"><span>07</span><h2>Check your understanding</h2></div>

<details>
<summary>1. Do equal collector and emitter resistors guarantee balanced loaded outputs?</summary>
<div>

No. Their effective AC resistances, including loads, must be matched. The two outputs have different impedances.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>2. Why can loading the emitter change collector gain?</summary>
<div>

The added emitter load changes the signal emitter current, which also changes the collector current and collector voltage swing.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>3. What are the units of transconductance?</summary>
<div>

Siemens: output current divided by input voltage. Milliamps per volt are millisiemens.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>4. Why is degenerated transconductance mostly set by a resistor?</summary>
<div>

When $g_mR_E\gg1$, $G_m=g_m/(1+g_mR_E)\approx1/R_E$.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>5. What must be rechecked after increasing $R_C$ to restore AC balance?</summary>
<div>

The DC collector voltage, transistor active-region margin, and available signal swing. A correct gain ratio can coexist with insufficient headroom.

</div>
</details>
