A single RC filter at the signal’s edge attenuates a component three times higher by only about 10 dB. More rejection requires more sections, and the way sections combine is not as simple as it first looks: connected directly, they interfere with each other.

This chapter introduces the vocabulary of **poles**, **zeros**, octaves and decades, which turns filter design into adding straight lines. It then builds band-pass and band-stop filters from the pieces of Chapter 15 and shows the limits of what resistors and capacitors alone can do.

<section id="octaves">

<div className="section-heading"><span>01</span><h2>Octaves, decades and what a pole is</h2></div>

An **octave** is a factor of 2 in frequency; a **decade** is a factor of 10. A single RC section well beyond its corner has |H| ∝ 1/f, so doubling frequency halves the output:

<Equation tex="20\log_{10}\tfrac12=-6.02\ \mathrm{dB\ per\ octave}\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad 20\log_{10}\tfrac1{10}=-20\ \mathrm{dB\ per\ decade}"/>

These two descriptions are the same slope. Engineers usually say “6 dB per octave” and “20 dB per decade” interchangeably, and often call this a **single-pole** rolloff.

### Why “pole”?

Write the low-pass transfer function with the variable s in place of jω:

<Equation tex="H(s)=\frac{1}{1+sRC},\qquad s=j\omega\ \text{for steady sinusoids}"/>

As an algebraic function of a complex variable s, H becomes infinite at s = −1/RC. That point is the **pole**. Real frequencies lie along the imaginary axis, s = jω, so we never sit on the pole, but its location governs the response:

<Equation tex="|H(j\omega)|=\frac{1/RC}{\left|\,j\omega-(-1/RC)\,\right|},\qquad \angle H=-\angle\left(j\omega+1/RC\right)"/>

Picture a pole on the negative real axis at distance 1/RC from the origin. The magnitude response is inversely proportional to the *distance from the pole to the point jω*. At low frequency that distance is about 1/RC and the gain is 1. At $\omega$ = 1/RC the distance is √2 times larger: −3 dB. Far up the axis the distance grows in proportion to $\omega$: −20 dB/decade. The phase is minus the angle of the line from the pole to jω: 0° at DC, 45° at the corner, approaching 90°.

A factor in the numerator that goes to zero is a **zero**. The high-pass has a zero at s = 0, which is why its gain rises from nothing at DC. Every first-order RC response is built from one pole and possibly one zero; higher-order responses are products of such factors.

The general s-variable (the Laplace transform) also describes transients, which is why the pole is at −1/RC: the step response contains e^(−t/RC). The same number appears as a decay rate in time and as a corner in frequency. Later chapters develop that connection; here the pole is a compact name for a corner frequency.

</section>

<section id="cascade">

<div className="section-heading"><span>02</span><h2>Cascade sections</h2></div>

### Buffered: responses multiply

If each RC section drives the next through a unity-gain **buffer**—an amplifier with high input impedance and low output impedance—no section loads the previous one. The overall response is the product of the individual responses, so gains in dB add and phases add:

<Equation tex="H=\prod_{k=1}^{n}\frac{1}{1+jf/f_k},\qquad \text{ultimate slope}=-20n\ \mathrm{dB/decade}=-6n\ \mathrm{dB/octave}"/>

For n identical poles at $f_c$, the gain at $f_c$ is −3n dB, and the overall −3 dB frequency moves down to $f_c$√(2^(1/n) − 1): $0.644f_c$ for two poles, $0.435f_c$ for four. The phase approaches −90n°.

### Unbuffered: sections interact

<CascadeCircuitFigure/>

Connect two identical RC low-pass sections directly (R, C, R, C). The second section’s input impedance loads the first section’s output, and nodal analysis gives, with x = ωRC:

<Equation tex="H=\frac{1}{1-x^2+3jx}\qquad\text{instead of}\qquad\frac{1}{(1+jx)^2}=\frac{1}{1-x^2+2jx}"/>

The term 3jx instead of 2jx means extra loss near the corners: at x = 1 the gain is 1/3 (−9.54 dB) rather than 1/2 (−6.02 dB). The denominator factors as (1 + jx/0.382)(1 + jx/2.618): the two poles have **split apart**, one moving down by a factor of 2.6 and one up by the same factor. The ultimate slope is still −40 dB/decade, but the knee is softer.

A remedy without amplifiers is **impedance scaling** (Chapter 13): make the second section’s R ten times larger and its C ten times smaller. Its time constant is unchanged but it barely loads the first section. The gain at x = 1 becomes 1/2.1 (−6.44 dB), close to the buffered −6.02 dB.

The loading term follows directly from KCL. Let $v_1$ be the intermediate node and $v_2$ the output, with $s=j\omega$. At the output, $(v_1-v_2)/R_2=sC_2v_2$, giving $v_1=(1+sR_2C_2)v_2$. Substitute this into the first node equation:

<Equation tex="\frac{v_{in}-v_1}{R_1}=sC_1v_1+\frac{v_1-v_2}{R_2}\quad\Rightarrow\quad H(s)=\frac{1}{1+s(R_1C_1+R_2C_2+R_1C_2)+s^2R_1R_2C_1C_2}" />

The extra $R_1C_2$ term is the interaction. When $R_2=kR_1$ and $C_2=C_1/k$, the denominator becomes $1+(2+1/k)sRC+(sRC)^2$. It approaches the buffered form as k grows, but larger resistors also increase noise and sensitivity to input leakage.

<CascadeLoadingFigure/>

<div className="worked-example">

<span className="tiny-label">WORKED EXAMPLE / THE OPENING QUESTION</span><h3>Reject 3 kHz while passing 1 kHz</h3>

<p>One RC pole at 1 kHz passes the signal at −3.01 dB and attenuates 3 kHz to 1/√10, only −10 dB. Moving the corner up to 1.5 kHz improves the signal to −1.6 dB but leaves 3 kHz at just −7 dB.</p>

<p>Four buffered poles at 2 kHz: the signal sees −4 × 10 $\log_{10}$(1 + 0.25) = −3.9 dB, while 3 kHz sees −4 × 10 $\log_{10}$(1 + 2.25) = −20.5 dB. Better, but the passband now droops noticeably. Sharp separation between nearby frequencies needs filters whose poles are not all on the real axis—resonant LC filters (Chapter 17) or active filters designed for flat passbands—not simply more identical RC sections.</p>

</div>

<section id="bandpass">

<div className="section-heading"><span>04</span><h2>Band-pass from two corners</h2></div>

Cascade a high-pass with corner $f_L$ and a low-pass with corner $f_H$ above it. Below $f_L$ the high-pass removes the signal; above $f_H$ the low-pass does; between them both pass. With a buffer between the sections:

<Equation tex="H=\frac{jf/f_L}{1+jf/f_L}\cdot\frac{1}{1+jf/f_H}"/>

The response peaks at the geometric center $f_0$ = √($f_L$ $f_H$), with +20 dB/decade below and −20 dB/decade above. When the corners are far apart, the passband gain approaches 0 dB and each edge behaves like its own single-pole filter.

<div className="worked-example">

<span className="tiny-label">WORKED EXAMPLE / A TELEPHONE VOICE BAND</span><h3>300 Hz to 3.4 kHz</h3>

<p>The center is √(300 × 3400) = <strong>1.01 kHz</strong>. With a buffer, the gain there is −0.73 dB: each corner contributes a little loss at the center because they are only a decade apart. Connected directly with equal resistors, the second section loads the first and the center gain falls to −1.41 dB.</p>

<p>Each edge has only a 20 dB/decade slope, so 100 Hz is attenuated by about 10 dB and 10 kHz by about 9.9 dB. Real telephone-band filters use many more poles.</p>

</div>

### Narrow bands need resonance

Move the corners together and the passband narrows, but the peak gain falls with it. With equal corners and a buffer, the peak is −6.02 dB; directly connected with equal resistors, −9.54 dB. A useful measure of narrowness is the **quality factor**, Q = $f_0$/(bandwidth between −3 dB points). A passive RC network can only have poles on the negative real axis, which limits its band-pass Q to at most ½. Narrow, selective filters need either inductors with capacitors (resonance) or amplifiers with feedback.

</section>

<section id="notch">

<div className="section-heading"><span>05</span><h2>Band-stop and the twin-T</h2></div>

A **band-stop** filter rejects a range of frequencies and passes those on either side. A narrow band-stop is a **notch**. (Band-stop is sometimes loosely called a band gap; that term belongs to semiconductor physics, Chapter 19.)

One approach adds the outputs of a low-pass and a high-pass whose corners are far apart: frequencies in the gap between them are attenuated. For a deep null at one frequency, use a network in which two signal paths cancel.

<TwinTFigure/>

### The twin-T

The twin-T places two T-networks in parallel between input and output. One is a high-pass T (two capacitors C in series, with R/2 to ground at their junction); the other is a low-pass T (two resistors R in series, with 2C to ground at their junction). At low frequency the resistor path passes the signal; at high frequency the capacitor path does. At

<Equation tex="f_0=\frac{1}{2\pi RC}"/>

the two paths deliver equal amplitudes with opposite phase and cancel exactly. The unloaded transfer function is:

<Equation tex="H=\frac{1-x^2}{1-x^2+4jx},\qquad x=\frac{f}{f_0}"/>

The numerator is zero at x = 1: a perfect null in theory. The 4jx term makes it broad: the effective Q is only ¼. At 2$f_0$ and at $f_0$/2 the attenuation is still 9.1 dB; at 3$f_0$ it is 5.1 dB.

<div className="worked-example">

<span className="tiny-label">WORKED EXAMPLE / A HUM FILTER</span><h3>Remove 60 Hz from a sensor signal</h3>

<p>Choose C = 100 nF, so 2C = 200 nF. Then R = 1/(2π × 60 × 100 nF) = <strong>26.5 kΩ</strong> and R/2 = 13.3 kΩ.</p>

<p>The null is only as deep as the component matching. With 1% parts the depth is typically limited to a few tens of dB, and the broad shoulders attenuate 120 Hz by 9 dB too—a problem if the wanted signal lives nearby. Feeding back part of the output with an amplifier (a later topic) sharpens the notch. The twin-T must be driven from a low impedance and see a high-impedance load; otherwise the cancellation is spoiled.</p>

</div>

</section>

<section id="sketch">

<div className="section-heading"><span>06</span><h2>Sketch any response from its corners</h2></div>

Bode’s method turns many transfer functions into a sum of straight lines:

1. Factor H into a constant times terms of the form (1 + jf/$f_k$), either in the numerator (zeros) or denominator (poles), plus any pure jf factors.
2. Start at low frequency with the constant’s gain in dB.
3. At each pole frequency, the slope decreases by 20 dB/decade; at each zero, it increases by 20 dB/decade.
4. Each pole adds −90° of phase spread over the two decades centered on it; each zero adds +90°.

### A pole and a zero: the shelf

A series resistor $R_1$ followed by a shunt branch of $R_2$ in series with C gives:

<Equation tex="H=\frac{R_2+1/(j\omega C)}{R_1+R_2+1/(j\omega C)}=\frac{1+j\omega R_2C}{1+j\omega(R_1+R_2)C}"/>

There is a pole at 1/(2π($R_1$ + $R_2$)C) and a higher zero at $1/(2πR_2C)$. Between them the response falls at 20 dB/decade; above the zero, the slopes cancel and the gain levels off at $R_2$/($R_1$ + $R_2$). This **shelf** or **lag network** is used to shape loop responses and tone controls.

<div className="worked-example">

<span className="tiny-label">WORKED EXAMPLE / A −20 dB SHELF</span><h3>$R_1$ = 9 kΩ, $R_2$ = 1 kΩ, C = 100 nF</h3>

<p>Pole at 1/(2π × 10 kΩ × 100 nF) = <strong>159 Hz</strong>, zero at 1/(2π × 1 kΩ × 100 nF) = <strong>1.59 kHz</strong>. Straight-line sketch: 0 dB to 159 Hz, falling 20 dB over the decade to 1.59 kHz, then flat at −20 dB, matching $R_2$/($R_1$ + $R_2$) = 0.1.</p>

<p>The phase dips and recovers: the greatest lag is at the geometric mean, 503 Hz, where it is −54.9° and the gain is −10 dB. A pure single pole would have kept on lagging toward −90°.</p>

</div>

<h3 id="book-notes">An all-pass circuit changes phase without changing magnitude</h3>

Poles do not always mean attenuation. A first-order all-pass has a left-half-plane pole and a right-half-plane zero:

<Equation tex={String.raw`H(s)=\frac{1-s\tau}{1+s\tau},\quad |H(j\omega)|=1,\quad\angle H=-2\tan^{-1}(\omega\tau)`}/>

<AllPassExtensionFigure/>

For $\tau=100$ µs, the phase is −90° at 1.592 kHz while amplitude remains unity. This can adjust the phase between signals without deliberately changing their relative amplitudes. A plain RC low-pass at that frequency has −45° phase and −3.01 dB magnitude; it cannot supply the same operation.

An all-pass is also not a constant time delay over all frequencies. Its group delay is $\tau_g=-d\angle H/d\omega=2\tau/[1+(\omega\tau)^2]$: 200 µs near DC, 100 µs at the corner and falling thereafter. Equal-amplitude frequencies can therefore arrive with different relative timing and distort a pulse. Cascaded all-pass sections can shape that timing over a chosen band, with extra amplifier bandwidth, tolerance and headroom requirements. The right-half-plane zero is why magnitude alone cannot reveal this circuit's phase response.

<p className="reference-note">Further reading: Van Valkenburg, §4.5, PDF p. 124 (printed p. 109). This extension uses original explanations and design values.</p>

<div className="takeaway"><span className="tiny-label">THE IDEA TO KEEP</span><p>Each pole bends the magnitude down 20 dB/decade and takes 90° of phase; each zero does the reverse. <em>Corners add</em>—if the sections do not load each other.</p></div>

<p className="reference-note">Coverage: The Art of Electronics §1.7.13, with band-pass and notch networks from §§1.7.8–1.7.9 extended. Resonant filters, which escape the RC limit on Q, follow in Chapter 17.</p>

<section id="check">

<div className="section-heading"><span>07</span><h2>Check your understanding</h2></div>

<details>
<summary>1. What gain do four identical buffered poles have at their individual corner?</summary>
<div>

$-4\times3.0103=-12.04$ dB. Their combined −3 dB point is lower, about $0.435f_c$.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>2. Why is direct RC cascading different from multiplying isolated responses?</summary>
<div>

The second section loads the first. The nodal denominator includes the extra $R_1C_2$ term, moving the poles and increasing loss near the corner.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>3. How can you reduce loading without changing the second section time constant?</summary>
<div>

Increase its resistor by k and decrease its capacitor by k. RC stays the same, while its impedance increases.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>4. Why do the twin-T values include R/2 and 2C?</summary>
<div>

Those ratios balance the two paths so their output contributions cancel at the notch. Arbitrary equal-value parts do not produce the stated null.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>5. Why does the shelf stop falling at high frequency?</summary>
<div>

Its zero adds +20 dB/decade and cancels the pole’s −20 dB/decade slope. The high-frequency divider sets the final gain.

</div>
</details>
