<section id="current">

## 01 / “No gate current” has a frequency and a temperature

An ideal FET gate draws no steady current. A real JFET gate is a reverse-biased junction with leakage; a real MOS gate has finite insulation leakage and may include protection structures. Both also draw displacement current whenever terminal voltages change.

Keep three mechanisms separate: leakage under normal bias, capacitive charging during a transition, and conduction when a junction or protection clamp turns on. They have different equations and different consequences. A gate that draws a picoampere at DC may briefly need milliamperes to follow a fast edge.

For a linear capacitor, i = C dv/dt. A 20 pF input driven through 5 V in 10 ns requires 10 mA during the edge. For voltage-dependent capacitance, the fundamental relation is i = dQ/dt; replace a single capacitance with a charge curve when the voltage excursion is large.

JFET gate-source forward bias injects junction current and invalidates the normal high-input-resistance model. MOS oxide does not act like an ordinary forward-biased p–n junction; exceeding gate ratings can damage it. Input clamps, if present, introduce their own current limits and leakage. Always provide a defined gate DC return and consider what happens while supplies are starting or missing.

</section>

<section id="capacitance">

## 02 / The gate sees the motion of the drain

Use Cgs between gate and source and Cgd between gate and drain. For a common-source stage whose source is approximately at AC ground, the current through Cgd is:

<Equation tex="i_{gd}=C_{gd}\frac{d(v_g-v_d)}{dt}=C_{gd}(1-A_v)\frac{dv_g}{dt}" />
<Equation tex="C_{\mathrm{in}}\approx C_{gs}+C_{gd}(1-A_v)" />

For Av = −10, Cgs = 10 pF and Cgd = 3 pF, the input looks like approximately 43 pF. A 100 kΩ signal-source resistance then gives an input pole near 37 kHz. Calling the transistor a “10 pF input” would miss most of the actual loading in this circuit.

The Miller replacement assumes a reasonably defined gain over the frequency range of interest. When gain itself becomes complex and varies rapidly, the equivalent is not simply a fixed physical capacitor. A full nodal AC analysis retains Cgd directly and can reveal additional poles and zeros.

A source follower reduces the voltage change across Cgs because the source moves with the gate. The approximate input contribution is Cgs(1 − Av), plus the gate-drain capacitance to an AC-grounded drain. This is capacitive bootstrapping. It reduces loading but cannot remove gate leakage or guarantee stability with every capacitive load.

The intrinsic cutoff-frequency estimate is:

<Equation tex="f_T\approx\frac{g_m}{2\pi(C_{gs}+C_{gd})}" />

This short-circuit current-gain estimate is not the bandwidth of a high-gain voltage amplifier. The latter depends on source impedance, loading, Miller multiplication and every other pole.

</section>

<section id="noise">

## 03 / High input resistance is useful, not free

A FET input often suits a high-resistance sensor because it does not require BJT-like DC base current. Yet the resistor that defines the gate's bias produces thermal noise:

<Equation tex="e_{n,R}=\sqrt{4k_BTR}\quad\mathrm{V}/\sqrt{\mathrm{Hz}}" />

At 300 K, 1 MΩ contributes about 129 nV/√Hz as an open-circuit voltage-noise density. Capacitance shapes its bandwidth. A 10 MΩ bias resistor may preserve low-frequency signal amplitude while increasing susceptibility to leakage, interference and slow settling.

For a simple long-channel saturation model, channel thermal current-noise density is approximately 4kBTγgm in A²/Hz, corresponding to input voltage-noise density squared of 4kBTγ/gm. The coefficient and excess low-frequency noise are device dependent. Increasing current can increase gm and reduce this contribution, while also changing power, capacitance and bias requirements.

JFETs are often attractive in low-noise analog inputs, but “JFET is always quieter than MOSFET” is not a design rule. Compare noise at the intended frequency and source impedance, including current noise, voltage noise, leakage, device size and process. Layout cleanliness, shielding and guarding can matter as much as the part number.

</section>

<section id="oscillator">

## 04 / Derive the Wien feedback network

An oscillator replaces an external signal with a small fluctuation that recirculates through positive feedback. At a candidate frequency, the complete loop must return the appropriate phase, and initial loop magnitude must exceed one for growth. These conditions identify a possible startup mode; nonlinear amplitude behavior and other modes still need analysis.

A Wien bridge uses a series RC arm from output to the feedback node and a parallel RC arm from that node to ground. For equal R and equal C:

<Equation tex="Z_s=R+\frac1{sC},\qquad Z_p=\frac{R}{1+sRC},\qquad \beta(s)=\frac{Z_p}{Z_s+Z_p}=\frac{sRC}{(sRC)^2+3sRC+1}" />

At ω0 = 1/RC, the real terms in the denominator cancel, leaving β = 1/3 with zero phase. A noninverting amplifier therefore needs gain three for marginal sustained oscillation in the ideal linear model. A gain slightly above three initiates growth from noise or an initial disturbance.

For R = 10 kΩ and C = 10 nF, f0 = 1/(2πRC) = 1.592 kHz. A gain of 3.1 gives a loop magnitude of 1.033 at that frequency. Without amplitude regulation, growth continues until some nonlinearity or clipping reduces the effective gain.

<section id="amplitude">

## 05 / Use a JFET to regulate amplitude

Consider a noninverting amplifier with feedback resistor RF and lower resistor RG, so gain is 1 + RF/RG. Put a JFET in series with the lower resistor and keep its channel signal small. A detector rectifies the output; a slow envelope filter creates a control voltage that makes an n-channel JFET gate more negative as amplitude rises. Its channel resistance rises, RG increases, and amplifier gain falls. The sign of that chain is the essential amplitude-stabilizing feedback.

For RF = 20 kΩ and a lower branch consisting of 9.5 kΩ plus a variable JFET, a 500 Ω channel gives a total RG of 10 kΩ and gain three. At 100 Ω, gain is 3.083, providing startup excess. The channel sees only a small share of the lower-arm voltage, helping reduce distortion. These numbers describe a design target; a real JFET must be selected or adjusted to reach the required resistance within reverse-biased gate operation.

The envelope time constant should average many oscillator cycles. Too fast, and gain changes within a cycle, distorting the waveform. Too slow, and startup can overshoot or amplitude recovery can take a long time. Detector thresholds, FET spread and amplifier output limits determine the attainable amplitude. A 100 ms envelope time constant averages roughly 159 cycles at 1.592 kHz, but it is not by itself a proof of amplitude-loop stability.

Half-channel-voltage gate tracking from the previous chapter can reduce the JFET's resistor nonlinearity. The DC control and AC correction must coexist without forward-biasing the gate. A complete implementation also needs an amplifier with enough bandwidth and slew rate at the chosen amplitude.

</section>

<section id="lc">

## 06 / Other FET oscillator roles

A Colpitts oscillator uses an inductor and a capacitive divider. The tank's effective capacitance is C1C2/(C1 + C2), so an unloaded first estimate is:

<Equation tex="f_0\approx\frac1{2\pi\sqrt{L\,C_1C_2/(C_1+C_2)}}" />

With L = 100 μH and C1 = C2 = 10 nF, the estimate is 225 kHz. The divider sets feedback fraction, while the transistor topology supplies the required phase and energy to replace tank losses. Reversing a feedback connection or choosing a common-source versus common-gate arrangement changes the sign analysis; a resonant frequency formula alone does not establish oscillation.

Tank loading, transistor capacitance and probe capacitance shift the result. A crystal replaces the broad LC resonance with a much sharper electromechanical resonance; its specified load capacitance and drive level become design constraints. FET gates can reduce resistive loading, but their capacitance still participates in the frequency-setting network.

The general workflow is to draw the complete loop, establish its DC bias, derive the frequency-selective feedback, estimate startup margin including losses, and explain how amplitude stops growing. That final step distinguishes an oscillator design from a loop-gain sketch.

</section>
