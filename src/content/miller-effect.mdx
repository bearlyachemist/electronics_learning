<div className="opening-grid">

<div><span className="tiny-label">A PRACTICAL QUESTION</span><p className="opening-question">A transistor with $f_T$ = 400 MHz, used in a common-emitter stage with a gain of 280, has a bandwidth under 300 kHz. Where did a factor of a thousand go?</p></div>

<div>

Every transistor has capacitance between its terminals, a few picofarads in a small-signal device. Small as that sounds, the capacitance between base and collector sits across a stage whose output swings hundreds of times more than its input. The current it draws—and hence the capacitance the source “sees”—is multiplied by the gain.

This is the **Miller effect**. It explains why common-emitter stages with high gain are slow, why the source impedance driving them matters so much, and why circuit configurations like the cascode exist. It is also the inverse of bootstrapping (Chapter 39), with the same 1/(1 − G) algebra and a negative G.

</div>

</div>

<section id="capacitances">

<div className="section-heading"><span>01</span><h2>The transistor’s capacitances and $f_T$</h2></div>

<HighFreqModelFigure/>

Two capacitances matter most:

- **$C_π$**, between base and emitter. It is mostly the *diffusion capacitance* from the stored base charge of Chapter 26, $g_m\tau_F$, plus the base–emitter depletion capacitance. Because it grows with $g_m$, it grows in proportion to the collector current.
- **$C_μ$** (often $C_{cb}$ or $C_{ob}$ on datasheets), between base and collector. It is the reverse-biased collector junction’s depletion capacitance: a few picofarads, falling slowly as $V_{CB}$ rises.

### The transition frequency

Drive the base with a current and short the collector to AC ground. The base current divides between $r_π$ and the capacitances; only the part through $r_π$ produces collector current.

<div className="derivation">

<span className="tiny-label">DERIVATION / CURRENT GAIN VERSUS FREQUENCY</span>

1. **KCL at the base.** With the collector grounded, $C_\pi$ and $C_\mu$ both run from the base to (AC) ground, in parallel with $r_\pi$. A base current $i_b$ develops
<Equation tex="v_{be}=\frac{i_b}{1/r_\pi+j\omega(C_\pi+C_\mu)}"/>

2. **Collector current.** The controlled source gives $g_mv_{be}$. (The current through $C_\mu$ into the grounded collector is $j\omega C_\mu v_{be}$, negligible next to $g_mv_{be}$ below $f_T$.) Divide by $i_b$ and use $g_mr_\pi = \beta_0$:
<Equation tex="\beta(j\omega)=\frac{i_c}{i_b}=\frac{g_mr_\pi}{1+j\omega r_\pi(C_\pi+C_\mu)}=\frac{\beta_0}{1+jf/f_\beta}"/>

3. **The transition frequency.** For $f \gg f_\beta$, $|\beta| \approx \beta_0f_\beta/f$. Setting this to 1 gives $f_T = \beta_0f_\beta$; substituting $f_\beta$ and $\beta_0 = g_mr_\pi$, the $r_\pi$ cancels.

</div>

<Equation tex="\beta(f)=\frac{\beta_0}{1+jf/f_\beta},\qquad f_\beta=\frac{1}{2\pi r_\pi(C_\pi+C_\mu)},\qquad f_T=\beta_0f_\beta=\frac{g_m}{2\pi(C_\pi+C_\mu)}" label="TRANSITION FREQUENCY"/>

$f_T$ is where the current gain has fallen to 1. Datasheets quote it, because from it the total capacitance follows. A transistor at 1 mA ($g_m$ = 38.7 mS) with $f_T$ = 400 MHz has $C_π$ + $C_μ$ = 38.7 mS/(2π × 400 MHz) = 15.4 pF; with $C_μ$ = 2 pF, $C_π$ ≈ 13.4 pF. With $\beta$ = 100, the current gain begins to fall at $f_β$ = 4 MHz.

$f_T$ measures the transistor alone, with no voltage gain. A stage with voltage gain is much slower, for the reason that follows.

</section>

<section id="theorem">

<div className="section-heading"><span>02</span><h2>The Miller theorem</h2></div>

Consider an impedance Z connected between the input and output of an amplifier with voltage gain A (so $v_{\mathrm{out}}$ = A $v_{\mathrm{in}}$). The current through Z is ($v_{\mathrm{in}}$ − $Av_{in}$)/Z. Seen from the input node, Z behaves like an impedance to ground of

<Equation tex="Z_{\mathrm{in}}=\frac{Z}{1-A}\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad C_{\mathrm{in}}=C\,(1-A)=C\,(1+|A|)\ \ \text{for an inverting gain }A=-|A|" label="MILLER THEOREM"/>

<MillerFigure/>

This is exactly the bootstrapping formula of Chapter 39. There, A was just below +1 and the impedance grew; here A is large and negative and the capacitance grows. From the output side the capacitor looks like C(1 − 1/A) ≈ C, essentially unchanged.

The theorem is exact at a single frequency if A is the actual gain with the capacitor connected. Using the midband gain is an approximation that works well when the input pole is the dominant one, as in the typical common-emitter stage.

</section>

<section id="bandwidth">

<div className="section-heading"><span>03</span><h2>Bandwidth of a common-emitter stage</h2></div>

In a common-emitter stage with collector load $R_C$, the voltage gain from base to collector is −$g_mR$_C. $C_μ$ bridges base and collector, so by the Miller theorem with $A = -g_mR_C$ the input node sees

<Equation tex="C_{\mathrm{in}}=C_\pi+C_\mu\left(1+g_mR_C\right)"/>

The source resistance $R_s$, in parallel with $r_π$, charges that capacitance. The result is a low-pass corner at

<Equation tex="f_{3\mathrm{dB}}\approx\frac{1}{2\pi\left(R_s\parallel r_\pi\right)\left[C_\pi+C_\mu(1+g_mR_C)\right]}" label="MILLER POLE"/>

<div className="worked-example">

<span className="tiny-label">WORKED EXAMPLE / THE OPENING QUESTION</span><h3>1 mA, $R_C$ = 10 kΩ, $R_s$ = 1 kΩ, $C_π$ = 13.4 pF, $C_μ$ = 2 pF</h3>

<p>$g_mR$_C = 387, so $C_{\mathrm{in}}$ = 13.4 + 2 × 388 = <strong>789 pF</strong>: the 2 pF $C_μ$ contributes 776 pF, dwarfing $C_π$. $R_s$ ∥ $r_π$ = 1 kΩ ∥ 2.59 kΩ = 721 Ω.</p>

<p>f₃dB ≈ 1/(2π × 721 Ω × 789 pF) = <strong>280 kHz</strong>. The exact nodal solution in the lab gives 269 kHz, with a midband gain of 279 (the divider of $R_s$ and $r_π$ takes some of $g_mR$_C).</p>

<p>Double $R_C$ to 20 kΩ: the gain doubles to 558 and the bandwidth halves to 136 kHz. Their product, about 75 MHz, barely changes. In a Miller-dominated stage, gain is traded directly for bandwidth.</p>

</div>

The Miller estimate is an approximation; derive the exact response to see where it comes from.

<div className="derivation">

<span className="tiny-label">DERIVATION / THE COMMON-EMITTER STAGE BY TWO-NODE ANALYSIS</span>

1. **Circuit.** Replace the source and $r_\pi$ by their Thévenin equivalent: $v_s' = v_s\,r_\pi/(R_s+r_\pi)$ behind $R' = R_s\parallel r_\pi$. The two unknowns are the base voltage $v_b$ and the collector voltage $v_c$. $C_\pi$ is from base to ground, $C_\mu$ from base to collector, and $R_C$ with the controlled source $g_mv_b$ from collector to ground.

2. **KCL at the base:**
<Equation tex="\frac{v_s'-v_b}{R'}=sC_\pi v_b+sC_\mu(v_b-v_c)"/>

3. **KCL at the collector.** Current arriving through $C_\mu$ leaves through the controlled source and $R_C$:
<Equation tex="sC_\mu(v_b-v_c)=g_mv_b+\frac{v_c}{R_C}"/>

4. **Solve step 3 for $v_c$:**
<Equation tex="v_c=-\,v_b\,\frac{g_mR_C-sC_\mu R_C}{1+sC_\mu R_C}"/>
   At low frequency this is $v_c = -g_mR_Cv_b$; the $-sC_\mu R_C$ in the numerator is a right-half-plane zero at $g_m/C_\mu$, far above the band of interest.

5. **Substitute into step 2** and keep only terms up to first order in $s$ (valid below the output pole $1/R_CC_\mu$). The term $sC_\mu(v_b - v_c)$ becomes $sC_\mu(1 + g_mR_C)v_b$:
<Equation tex="\frac{v_s'-v_b}{R'}\approx s\left[C_\pi+C_\mu(1+g_mR_C)\right]v_b"/>
   This is the Miller capacitance, now derived rather than assumed.

6. **Transfer function.**
<Equation tex="\frac{v_c}{v_s}\approx-\frac{r_\pi}{R_s+r_\pi}\cdot\frac{g_mR_C}{1+sR'\left[C_\pi+C_\mu(1+g_mR_C)\right]}"/>
   The midband gain is $g_mR_C$ reduced by the input divider (279 rather than 387 in the example), and the pole is the Miller pole above. The exact second-order solution adds a second pole near the output and moves the first slightly, which is why the lab reports 269 kHz rather than 280 kHz.

</div>

The output node has its own pole, set by $R_C$ and the capacitance there ($C_μ$, wiring and the next stage’s input). It is usually at a much higher frequency, but in high-gain stages with large $R_C$ it can come close to the Miller pole, and the two together can cause trouble inside feedback loops.

</section>

<section id="cures">

<div className="section-heading"><span>05</span><h2>Cures: low source impedance and the cascode</h2></div>

### Drive it from a low impedance

The Miller capacitance does no harm if the source can charge it quickly. Drive the stage from an emitter follower, whose output impedance is a few tens of ohms, and the Miller pole moves up by the ratio of source resistances. In the lab, reducing $R_s$ from 1 kΩ toward 50 Ω raises the bandwidth roughly tenfold, until other poles take over.

### Reduce the gain seen by $C_μ$: the cascode

The Miller multiplication depends on the voltage swing at the collector of the transistor whose $C_μ$ is involved. In a **cascode**, the common-emitter transistor $Q_1$ drives the emitter of a second transistor $Q_2$ whose base is held at a fixed DC voltage (AC ground). $Q_2$ is a common-base stage: it passes $Q_1$’s collector current through to the load almost unchanged, and its emitter is a low-impedance node, about 1/$g_m$.

$Q_1$’s collector therefore sees a load of only 1/$g_m$₂ ≈ $r_e$. To see this, write KCL at $Q_2$'s emitter with its base at AC ground: $Q_1$ pulls $g_{m1}v_{b}$ out of the node, and $Q_2$'s emitter supplies it, which requires $v_{e2} = -g_{m1}v_b/g_{m2}$ (Chapter 30: looking into the emitter with a grounded base gives about $1/g_m$). So the voltage gain from $Q_1$'s base to its collector is about −$g_m$₁/$g_m$₂ ≈ −1, since both carry the same current. Its $C_μ$ is multiplied only by 2. The full voltage swing appears at $Q_2$’s collector, but $Q_2$’s base is grounded, so its $C_μ$ sits from output to ground, not across a gain. The overall gain is unchanged, −$g_mR$_C, while the input capacitance falls from 789 pF to about 17 pF:

<Equation tex="C_{\mathrm{in,cascode}}\approx C_\pi+2C_\mu"/>

In the lab the bandwidth rises from 269 kHz to about **6.2 MHz**, with the same gain. The cascode also isolates the output from the input (better reverse isolation) and raises the output resistance, because $Q_2$’s emitter degeneration by $Q_1$’s high collector impedance is very effective (Chapter 31). It costs one transistor and about a volt of headroom. Cascodes are standard in wideband amplifiers, radio-frequency stages, and op-amp input stages.

<CascodeFigure/>

### Other remedies

A common-collector/common-base pair (a follower driving a common-base stage) avoids the Miller effect altogether. A differential pair driven at one input and loaded at the other behaves similarly. Lowering $R_C$, or putting the gain inside a feedback loop, trades gain for speed in a controlled way.

</section>

<section id="switching">

<div className="section-heading"><span>06</span><h2>Capacitance in switching</h2></div>

The same capacitance limits transistor switches. While a switching transistor is in its active region, its collector voltage changes rapidly while its base voltage stays near 0.65 V. Write KCL at the base: the drive current $I_B$ must supply the transistor's base current, $C_\pi\,dV_B/dt$ and $C_\mu\,d(V_B - V_C)/dt$. With $V_B$ nearly constant the $C_\pi$ term vanishes, and once the transistor's own base-current need is small compared with the drive, almost all of $I_B$ is spent charging $C_μ$, which holds the collector slew rate to

<Equation tex="\frac{dV_C}{dt}\approx\frac{I_B}{C_\mu}"/>

With 1 mA of base drive and $C_μ$ = 5 pF, the collector can slew at most 200 V/µs; with 50 µA, at 10 V/µs. On an oscilloscope this appears as a flat step in the base voltage during switching—the **Miller plateau**, also prominent in MOSFET gate waveforms. Faster switching needs more base drive, a lower-impedance driver, or a cascode, in addition to the charge-storage remedies of Chapters 26 and 27.

### Followers escape the multiplication

In an emitter follower the base and emitter move together, so $C_π$ is bootstrapped and nearly vanishes from the input, while $C_μ$ goes from base to the collector, which is at AC ground, and is not multiplied. A follower’s input capacitance is roughly $C_μ$ + $C_π$(1 − G): a few picofarads. This is one more reason followers are placed in front of high-gain stages.

### Why a high transition frequency can coexist with a slow amplifier

Using $C_π+C_μ\approx g_m/(2πf_T)$, the Miller input pole estimate can be written:

<Equation tex="f_{in}\approx\frac{1}{2π(R_s\parallel r_π)\left[g_m/(2πf_T)+g_mR_C C_μ\right]}"/>

The first capacitance term comes from the device’s intrinsic current-gain limit; the second comes from circuit gain. A large $R_C$ and a resistive source can make the circuit bandwidth orders of magnitude below $f_T$. A cascode reduces the second term by holding the lower collector nearly still, but introduces upper-device capacitance, an output pole and an extra headroom requirement.

<div className="worked-example">

<span className="tiny-label">WORKED EXAMPLE / REDUCE SOURCE RESISTANCE</span><h3>1 mA, 10 kΩ collector load and 2 pF collector–base capacitance</h3>

<p>At β = 100, $g_m=38.68$ mS and $r_π=2.585$ kΩ. With $C_π=13.4$ pF, the Miller input capacitance is about 789 pF. A 1 kΩ source gives $R_s\parallel r_π=721$ Ω and an input pole estimate of 280 kHz.</p>

<p>Reducing the source to 100 Ω makes the parallel resistance 96.3 Ω and the estimate 2.09 MHz. The source-to-base divider also improves from 0.721 to 0.963, so the source-to-output low-frequency gain changes as well. Once the input pole approaches other poles, use the full nodal model rather than scaling bandwidth by the resistance ratio alone.</p>

<p>The collector’s simple capacitance pole estimate is $1/(2πR_CC_μ)=7.96$ MHz. This comparison explains why reducing source resistance eventually gives diminishing returns, even before base resistance and wiring capacitance are added.</p>

</div>

<div className="takeaway"><span className="tiny-label">THE IDEA TO KEEP</span><p>A capacitance across an inverting gain looks <em>(1 + |A|) times larger</em> from the input. In a common-emitter stage that makes $C_μ$ dominate the bandwidth; lowering the source impedance or adding a cascode gives the speed back.</p></div>

<section id="check">

<div className="section-heading"><span>07</span><h2>Check your understanding</h2></div>

<details>
<summary>1. Why does an inverting stage multiply an input-output capacitor?</summary>
<div>

The output moves opposite the input, so the capacitor sees a larger voltage change and draws more input current than a grounded capacitor of the same value.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>2. Is the Miller equivalent a new physical capacitor?</summary>
<div>

No. It is an equivalent describing the current drawn at a port for an assumed gain relationship; the physical capacitor remains between the nodes.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>3. Why does reducing source resistance improve bandwidth?</summary>
<div>

The source charges the effective input capacitance through a smaller resistance. Other poles eventually limit the improvement.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>4. What does the upper cascode transistor hold nearly still?</summary>
<div>

The lower transistor’s collector, reducing voltage change across its collector–base capacitance and hence Miller multiplication.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>5. Does a large $f_T$ guarantee a large voltage-amplifier bandwidth?</summary>
<div>

No. Circuit gain, source and output resistance, loading, layout and the number of poles all matter.

</div>
</details>
